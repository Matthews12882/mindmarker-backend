# Andy Burnham

**Andrew Murray Burnham** (born 7 January 1970) is a British politician who has served as [Prime Minister of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom "Prime Minister of the United Kingdom") and [Leader of the Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leader_of_the_Labour_Party_(UK) "Leader of the Labour Party (UK)") since July 2026. He has been [Member of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom) "Member of Parliament (United Kingdom)") (MP) for [Makerfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makerfield_(constituency) "Makerfield (constituency)") in [Greater Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Manchester "Greater Manchester") since June 2026, and was [Mayor of Greater Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Greater_Manchester "Mayor of Greater Manchester") from 2017 to 2026; before that he was MP for [Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_(constituency) "Leigh (constituency)") from 2001 to 2017 and held several [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_Kingdom "Cabinet of the United Kingdom") positions. Burnham identifies as a [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist "Socialist") and is associated with the [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK) "Labour Party (UK)")'s [soft left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_left "Soft left"); he is also a member of the [Co-operative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-operative_Party "Co-operative Party"), which is [allied with Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_and_Co-operative_Party "Labour and Co-operative Party"). His political ideology has been called "[Manchesterism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchesterism "Manchesterism")".

Burnham was born and brought up in [northern England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_England "Northern England"). He joined Labour at the age of 15 and [read English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_studies "English studies") at [Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzwilliam_College,_Cambridge "Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge"). He worked as a journalist and [political adviser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_adviser_(United_Kingdom) "Special adviser (United Kingdom)") before being elected the MP for Leigh [in 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_United_Kingdom_general_election "2001 United Kingdom general election"). Under the prime ministers [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair "Tony Blair") and [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown "Gordon Brown"), Burnham was [Chief Secretary to the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Secretary_to_the_Treasury "Chief Secretary to the Treasury") and [Culture Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_Secretary "Culture Secretary"), in which capacity he launched the [Hillsborough Independent Panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsborough_Independent_Panel "Hillsborough Independent Panel"). In 2009, as [Health Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Health_and_Social_Care "Secretary of State for Health and Social Care"), he responded to the [swine flu pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swine_flu_pandemic "Swine flu pandemic") and launched an independent inquiry into the [Stafford Hospital scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stafford_Hospital_scandal "Stafford Hospital scandal").

Following Labour's defeat at the [2010 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election "2010 United Kingdom general election"), Burnham served in the [shadow cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_cabinet "Shadow cabinet") from 2010 to 2016. He stood for the Labour leadership [in 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2010 Labour Party leadership election (UK)") and [in 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2015 Labour Party leadership election (UK)"), coming fourth and second, respectively. In 2017, he stood down as an MP and [was elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2017 Greater Manchester mayoral election") Mayor of Greater Manchester. He reorganised Manchester's [tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink "Manchester Metrolink") and [bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buses_in_Manchester "Buses in Manchester") systems as the [Bee Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee_Network "Bee Network") and received the nickname "King of the North" for campaigning for more [furlough funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronavirus_Job_Retention_Scheme "Coronavirus Job Retention Scheme") for Northern communities during the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_Kingdom "COVID-19 pandemic in the United Kingdom"). Burnham's tenure as mayor increased his national profile and he was re-elected [in 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2021 Greater Manchester mayoral election") and [in 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2024 Greater Manchester mayoral election").

Following Labour's [return to government in 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election "2024 United Kingdom general election"), Burnham maintained strong approval ratings as those of the prime minister, [Keir Starmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keir_Starmer "Keir Starmer"), [declined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Labour_Party_leadership_crisis "2026 Labour Party leadership crisis"), and came to be seen as a potential successor. He returned to Parliament in 2026 by winning the [Makerfield by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Makerfield_by-election "2026 Makerfield by-election") and [resigned as mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_by-election "2026 Greater Manchester mayoral by-election"). Starmer announced his resignation, leading to [a party leadership election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Labour_Party_leadership_election "2026 Labour Party leadership election") in which Burnham won unopposed. During [his premiership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Andy_Burnham "Premiership of Andy Burnham"), Burnham has announced policies to move some government administration out of London as [Number 10 North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_10_North "Number 10 North"), cut fares on [buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_transport_in_the_United_Kingdom "Bus transport in the United Kingdom") in England, reduce business rates on [pubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pub "Pub"), increase government support for [apprenticeships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apprenticeships_in_the_United_Kingdom "Apprenticeships in the United Kingdom") and continue [support for Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine%E2%80%93United_Kingdom_relations "Ukraine–United Kingdom relations").

## Early life and education

Andrew Murray Burnham was born on 7 January 1970 in [Aintree, Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aintree,_Lancashire "Aintree, Lancashire"), a suburb of [Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool "Liverpool"), and was brought up in [Culcheth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culcheth "Culcheth"). His father, Kenneth Roy Burnham (1940-2026), was a telephone [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer "Engineer"), and his mother, Eileen Mary Burnham ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A9e "Née") Murray), was a [GP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_practitioner "General practitioner") receptionist. Burnham's father was a [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant "Protestant") while his mother is a [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic "Catholic") of [Irish-Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Scottish_people "Irish Scottish people") descent, born and raised in [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland "Scotland"). His great-grandfather was from [Drogheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogheda "Drogheda") and emigrated to Liverpool in the 19th century.

Burnham was raised Catholic, and served as an [altar boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar_server "Altar server") at his local church. He later described himself as "Catholic by upbringing" but "not particularly religious". He said: "[Catholic social teaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_social_teaching "Catholic social teaching") underpins my politics. We did have to read the [catechism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catechism#Catholic_catechisms "Catechism") at school but it is powerful and strong and right." He was educated at St Lewis Catholic Primary School and [St Aelred's Roman Catholic High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_Academy "Hope Academy") in [Newton-le-Willows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton-le-Willows "Newton-le-Willows"); there he earned the [nickname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickname "Nickname") "Seven Eights", because of his "inability to remember that seven eights are 56" when reciting his [times tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_table "Multiplication table"). He joined the [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK) "Labour Party (UK)") when he was 15.

Burnham read English as an undergraduate at [Fitzwilliam College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzwilliam_College,_Cambridge "Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge") of the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), receiving a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") (BA) in 1991 with [upper second-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_undergraduate_degree_classification#Upper_second-class_honours "British undergraduate degree classification") honours. As per tradition, his BA was later promoted to a [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_(Oxford,_Cambridge_and_Dublin) "Master of Arts (Oxford, Cambridge and Dublin)") (MA Cantab) degree.

## Early political career

After university, Burnham moved to London. From 1991 to 1994 he was a journalist for a private company, Baltic Publishing, assigned to their [trade magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_magazine "Trade magazine") *Tank Container World* (later *Bulk Distributor*), a magazine covering [tanker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tank_truck "Tank truck") and [intermodal transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_freight_transport "Intermodal freight transport"). From 1994 until the [1997 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_United_Kingdom_general_election "1997 United Kingdom general election") he was a researcher for [Tessa Jowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessa_Jowell "Tessa Jowell"). He joined the [Transport and General Workers' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_and_General_Workers%27_Union "Transport and General Workers' Union") in 1995. Following the 1997 election, he was a parliamentary officer for the [NHS Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_Confederation "NHS Confederation") from August to December 1997, before taking up the post as an administrator with the Football Task Force for a year. In 1998, he became a [special adviser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_adviser_(United_Kingdom) "Special adviser (United Kingdom)") to the [Secretary of State for Culture, Media and Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Culture,_Media_and_Sport "Secretary of State for Culture, Media and Sport"), [Chris Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Smith,_Baron_Smith_of_Finsbury "Chris Smith, Baron Smith of Finsbury"), a position he held until he was elected to the House of Commons in the [2001 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_United_Kingdom_general_election "2001 United Kingdom general election").

### Member of Parliament (2001–2017)

Following the retirement of [Lawrence Cunliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Cunliffe "Lawrence Cunliffe"), Burnham successfully applied to be the parliamentary candidate for [Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_(constituency) "Leigh (constituency)") in Greater Manchester, then a Labour [safe seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_seat "Safe seat"). At the 2001 election he was elected with a majority of 16,362, and gave his [maiden speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_speech "Maiden speech") in the House of Commons on 4 July 2001. Following his election to Parliament, Burnham was a member of the [Health Select Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_Select_Committee "Health Select Committee") from 2001 until 2003, when he was appointed [Parliamentary private secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_private_secretary "Parliamentary private secretary") (PPS) to the [Home Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Secretary "Home Secretary") [David Blunkett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Blunkett "David Blunkett"). Following Blunkett's first resignation in 2004, he became PPS to the [education secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Education "Secretary of State for Education") [Ruth Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Kelly "Ruth Kelly"). As well as Labour, Burnham is a member of the [Co-operative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-operative_Party "Co-operative Party"), which has been [allied to Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_and_Co-operative_Party "Labour and Co-operative Party") since 1927 and runs all candidates jointly with it.

### Frontbench roles (2005–2010)

#### 2005–2007: Early ministerial career

Following the [2005 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_United_Kingdom_general_election "2005 United Kingdom general election"), Burnham was promoted to serve in Tony Blair's government as [Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for the Home Office,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Under-Secretary_of_State_for_the_Home_Department "Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for the Home Department") where he was responsible for implementing the Identity Cards Act 2006. In the government reshuffle on 5 May 2006, he left the [Home Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Office "Home Office") to become [Minister of State for Delivery and Reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_State_for_Health_(Secondary_Care) "Minister of State for Health (Secondary Care)") at the Department of Health.

When Gordon Brown formed his first cabinet on 28 June 2007, Burnham was appointed [Chief Secretary to the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Secretary_to_the_Treasury "Chief Secretary to the Treasury"), a position he held until 2008. During his tenure at the Treasury, he played a key role in drafting the 2007 Comprehensive Spending Review.

#### 2008–2009: Culture Secretary and the Hillsborough campaign

In a January 2008 reshuffle, Burnham was promoted to Secretary of State for Culture, Media and Sport, replacing James Purnell. In June 2008, he issued an apology to Shami Chakrabarti, the director of the human rights pressure group Liberty, after she threatened to sue him for libel over comments that smeared her reputation in an article Burnham wrote for *Progress* magazine.

Later that year, Burnham announced government plans to tighten controls on internet content to rectify what he described as an imbalance with television regulations. This announcement was followed by an address to the music industry lobbying group, UK Music, where he called for "a partnership between Government and the music business as a whole: one with rewards for both of us; one with rewards for society as a whole." He added, "My job – Government's job – is to preserve the value in the system."

In April 2009, after being heckled by the crowd at the 20th anniversary memorial of the Hillsborough disaster, Burnham raised the issue at the next day's cabinet meeting in Downing Street. He requested permission from Gordon Brown to raise the disaster in Parliament, and Brown agreed. This intervention ultimately led to the establishment of the second Hillsborough inquiry. By 2014, when Burnham spoke at the 25th anniversary of the disaster, his pivotal role was recognized as he was cheered and applauded by the crowd.

On 27 April 2016, the day after the Hillsborough inquest verdict that found the 96 Hillsborough deaths had occurred as a result of [unlawful killing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unlawful_killing "Unlawful killing"), Burnham made a speech to the House of Commons calling for those responsible to be held to account. Condemning [South Yorkshire Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Yorkshire_Police "South Yorkshire Police"), which had instigated a cover-up in the aftermath of the tragedy, he described the force as being "rotten to the core" while suggesting that the cover-up had been "advanced in the committee rooms of this House and in the press rooms of 10 Downing Street". The eleven-minute statement drew applause from MPs, a response that is generally against convention at Westminster.

This campaign reached a historic milestone in July 2026 when the House of Commons passed the Public Office (Accountability) Bill, widely known as the Hillsborough Law. Pushed through by the departing [Keir Starmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keir_Starmer "Keir Starmer") [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starmer_ministry "Starmer ministry"), the legislation establishes a statutory duty of candour for public officials and ensures non-means-tested legal aid for families facing the state at inquests. Burnham hailed the Bill's passage as a fundamental "rewiring of the state" that would finally dismantle institutional cover-up culture, completing a personal full-circle political journey just days before he took office as Prime Minister.

#### 2009–2010: Health Secretary and the Stafford Hospital inquiry

In June 2009, Burnham was promoted to Secretary of State for Health, a post he held until the Labour government resigned following the [2010 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election "2010 United Kingdom general election"). In July 2009, just a month after taking office, he launched an independent inquiry chaired by Robert Francis QC into [unusually high mortality rates at Stafford Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stafford_Hospital_scandal "Stafford Hospital scandal") (later renamed [County Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Hospital,_Stafford "County Hospital, Stafford")). The inquiry uncovered systematic failures and heavily criticized the care provided by the Mid Staffordshire NHS Foundation Trust. A subsequent, wider public inquiry—also led by Francis—was launched in 2010 by Burnham's Conservative successor, [Andrew Lansley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Lansley "Andrew Lansley"). While this wider inquiry confirmed serious failings, it concluded that linking those failings to a specific number of deaths would be "misleading".

After leaving office, reports emerged claiming that Burnham and his predecessor as Health Secretary, [Alan Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Johnson "Alan Johnson"), had rejected 81 separate requests for a public inquiry into the Stafford Hospital deaths. Burnham also faced criticism for not visiting the hospital or meeting with the affected families during his tenure. *The Daily Telegraph* reported that up to 2,800 more deaths than expected had occurred across 14 NHS trusts highlighted for high mortality rates since initial concerns were raised in 2005. However, these figures were later discredited. The Keogh Review, led by Bruce Keogh into the 14 trusts, subsequently described the use of such statistical mortality measures as "clinically meaningless and academically reckless."

#### National Care Service proposal

As Health Secretary, Burnham pioneered the creation of a [National Care Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Care_Service "National Care Service") (NCS), aiming to introduce a publicly funded social care system free at the point of use, mirrored on the [National Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Health_Service "National Health Service"). In July 2009, the Department of Health published its green paper, *Shaping the Future of Care Together*, which proposed an NCS "on par with the NHS." This was followed in September by a public consultation dubbed the "Big Care Debate", which Gordon Brown promoted as a "crucial national debate".

The consultation confirmed strong public appetite for social care reform, leading the government to opt for a phased rollout. The first stage began with the Personal Care at Home Act 2010, passed in April 2010. Burnham had formally launched the NCS a month earlier, granting all elderly and disabled people free social care. The second stage, scheduled for 2014, was designed to extend free social care to individuals in residential care for more than two years, with a third and final stage fully introducing free social care for all adults after 2015. However, following Labour's defeat in the 2010 general election, the [Conservative-Liberal Democrat coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron%E2%80%93Clegg_coalition "Cameron–Clegg coalition") led by [David Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cameron "David Cameron") and [Nick Clegg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Clegg "Nick Clegg") scrapped the initiative, and the Personal Care at Home Act 2010 was subsequently repealed.

### Shadow portfolios

In May 2010, Burnham became [Shadow Secretary of State for Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_Secretary_of_State_for_Health "Shadow Secretary of State for Health") in the [Miliband shadow cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miliband_shadow_cabinet "Miliband shadow cabinet"). In October 2010, Burnham was appointed [Shadow Secretary of State for Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_Secretary_of_State_for_Education "Shadow Secretary of State for Education") and election co-ordinator for the Labour Party. As shadow education secretary, Burnham opposed the coalition government's plans for "[free schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_school_(England) "Free school (England)")". He argued for moving the education system back towards a [comprehensive system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_school_(England_and_Wales) "Comprehensive school (England and Wales)"). A year later, he was again appointed to the role of Shadow Secretary of State for Health, which he held until 2015.

In September 2015, Burnham accepted an appointment as [shadow home secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_home_secretary "Shadow home secretary") in the first [Corbyn shadow cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corbyn_shadow_cabinet "Corbyn shadow cabinet") and remained in the role after the 2016 reshuffle. Burnham opposed the [Prevent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CONTEST#Prevent "CONTEST") counter-terrorism strategy. Appearing in 2016 alongside the anti-Prevent organisation MEND, Burnham said: "The Prevent duty to report extremist behaviour is today's equivalent of internment in Northern Ireland."

On 25 April 2017, as his final act in Parliament, he delivered an adjournment debate that lasted over an hour on the [infected blood scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infected_blood_scandal_in_the_United_Kingdom "Infected blood scandal in the United Kingdom"). Burnham used the debate to present a raft of evidence stating "this scandal amounts to a criminal cover-up on an industrial scale" and that "these are criminal acts". He said that if the Government did not set up an Investigation into the scandal that he would refer his evidence to the police. In July 2017, the then Prime Minister [Theresa May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theresa_May "Theresa May") announced an independent public inquiry into the scandal, after successive governments since the 1980s had refused to do so. The final report was published on 20 May 2024, concluding that the scandal could have been largely avoided, patients were knowingly exposed to "unacceptable risks", and that doctors, the NHS and the government tried to cover up what happened by "hiding the truth". The then Prime Minister [Rishi Sunak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi_Sunak "Rishi Sunak") apologised for the failures over the scandal, calling it "a decades-long moral failure".

### Previous leadership campaigns

In May 2010, following Brown's resignation as leader of the Labour Party after the formation of the [Cameron–Clegg coalition between the Conservatives and Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron%E2%80%93Clegg_coalition "Cameron–Clegg coalition"), Burnham declared his intention to stand in the subsequent [leadership contest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2010 Labour Party leadership election (UK)"). He launched his leadership campaign in his Leigh constituency on 26 May. Burnham stood on his philosophy of "aspirational socialism", aligning himself with [Intern Aware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intern_Aware "Intern Aware")'s campaign to end unpaid internships. He made policy commitments including re-creating the National Care Service, which he had previously introduced as health secretary before its abolition by the Coalition, and replacing [inheritance tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance_tax "Inheritance tax") with a [land value tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_value_tax "Land value tax"). Burnham finished fourth, eliminated on the second ballot with 10.4% of the vote. The leadership contest was won by [Ed Miliband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Miliband "Ed Miliband").

On 13 May 2015, following Labour's defeat in [the general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_United_Kingdom_general_election "2015 United Kingdom general election") to Cameron's Conservatives, Burnham announced that he would stand to replace Miliband in the [2015 leadership election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2015 Labour Party leadership election (UK)"). He stressed the need to unite the party and country and "rediscover the beating heart of Labour". Burnham was initially considered the frontrunner in the race to succeed Miliband. The emergence of [Jeremy Corbyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Corbyn "Jeremy Corbyn") as the candidate representing the [left-wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing "Left-wing") faction of the party, in June 2015, and the consequent growth in support for [Corbyn's campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Corbyn_2015_Labour_Party_leadership_campaign "Jeremy Corbyn 2015 Labour Party leadership campaign") saw Burnham's lead diminish to the point that Corbyn overtook him – this was first reflected in a [YouGov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouGov "YouGov") poll published by *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* on 21 July.

In his official manifesto, Burnham pledged to offer "a balanced plan for a strong economy and sound public finances, providing a genuine alternative to [George Osborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Osborne "George Osborne")'s punishing [austerity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_government_austerity_programme "United Kingdom government austerity programme")". His platform involved re-balancing the [tax system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_in_the_United_Kingdom "Taxation in the United Kingdom") by restoring the 50p income tax rate that had been cut in the [2012 budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_United_Kingdom_budget "2012 United Kingdom budget"). He also signalled that he would not introduce the '[mansion tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansion_tax "Mansion tax")' that was included in [Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK) "Labour Party (UK)")'s 2015 election manifesto, calling the proposal "the politics of envy". His other policies included increasing the [minimum wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_wage_law "Minimum wage law") and scrapping the youth rate, to create a "true living wage for all ages" and abolishing [zero-hour contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-hour_contract "Zero-hour contract") and unpaid internships. He also announced that he would address the [house price and rent crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordability_of_housing_in_the_United_Kingdom "Affordability of housing in the United Kingdom") by giving councils greater freedom and increased borrowing powers to build more houses, regulate the [private rented sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_rented_sector "Private rented sector") and introduce a [land value tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_value_tax "Land value tax") on commercial properties.

Burnham was criticised in late May 2015 for claiming £17,000 a year from the taxpayer to rent a London flat while owning another within walking distance of the Palace of Westminster. A spokesman for Burnham said that renting out the original flat was necessary to "cover his costs", as parliamentary rule changes meant he was no longer able to claim for mortgage interest expenses. He was also criticised for refusing to be interviewed by *[The Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sun_(United_Kingdom) "The Sun (United Kingdom)")* when it emerged he had given an interview to the newspaper during his first run for the Labour leadership, and been photographed in the back of a cab for the newspaper.

In August 2015, Burnham announced that he would commit Labour to "a policy of progressive re-nationalisation" of the railway system. There was some speculation in the media that the announcement was an attempt by the Burnham campaign to align itself further to the left of the party and win back voters it had lost to Corbyn's campaign, as nationalisation had formed a key part of Corbyn's economic policy. He continued to voice his opposition to the [TTIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TTIP "TTIP") [free trade agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade_agreement "Free trade agreement") between the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union "European Union") and the United States, arguing that it would undermine the National Health Service, as it would "open the floodgates" to private healthcare providers. He was also in favour of [building a third runway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_of_Heathrow_Airport "Expansion of Heathrow Airport") at [Heathrow Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Airport "Heathrow Airport"). In September 2015, Burnham came second to Corbyn in the election, with 19% of the vote in the first round, compared to 59% for Corbyn.

## Mayor of Greater Manchester (2017–2026)

### Candidacy and election

On 5 May 2016, a spokesperson for Burnham confirmed that he had been approached by party officials in [Greater Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Manchester "Greater Manchester"), asking him to consider resigning from Corbyn's shadow cabinet in order to run in the upcoming [mayoral election in 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2017 Greater Manchester mayoral election"). On 18 May 2016, he confirmed that he was standing for mayorship. Burnham was selected as the Labour candidate in August 2016. In September 2016, Burnham said that he would resign as Shadow Home Secretary once a replacement had been found, in order to concentrate on his mayoral bid. He was succeeded by [Diane Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Abbott "Diane Abbott") in October. Burnham said, if elected as Greater Manchester's mayor, he would resign his seat as the member of parliament for [Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_(UK_Parliament_constituency) "Leigh (UK Parliament constituency)"). In the event, the [2017 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election "2017 United Kingdom general election") was declared two weeks before the mayoral election, and Burnham did not stand as a candidate for parliament.

Burnham was elected to the new role of [Mayor of Greater Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Greater_Manchester "Mayor of Greater Manchester") on 5 May 2017. He received 63% of the vote, winning majorities in all ten of Greater Manchester's boroughs. In his victory speech, he said: "This is the dawn of a new era, not just for this city region but for politics in our country. It has been too London-centric for too long. The old political and party structures haven't delivered for all people and for all places. ... Greater Manchester is going to take control. We are going to change politics and make it work better for people."

In the [election of 6 May 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2021 Greater Manchester mayoral election"), Burnham was re-elected as mayor, with 67% of the vote on a turnout of 34.7%. In the [2024 mayoral election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2024 Greater Manchester mayoral election"), Burnham was elected for a third term with 63.4% of the vote, on a turnout of 32.5%. This was the first Greater Manchester mayoral election to use the [first-past-the-post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-past-the-post "First-past-the-post") voting system, with Burnham winning in every ward but one.

On Burnham stepping down as mayor, the local *[Manchester Evening News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Evening_News "Manchester Evening News")* (*MEN*) newspaper gave him positive ratings for economic development and action on homelessness, although it felt he had not been as successful with police management. Its polling reported that he had a 65% approval rating. Jennifer Williams, who covered Burnham for the *MEN* and *[Financial Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Times "Financial Times")*, felt that Burnham had joined a longstanding "economic project dating back to the 1980s" to boost Manchester's economy and national standing, but had "injected a zeal into defining the city...to the masses" that increased its profile nationally while managing a "byzantine system" of different local authorities and bus operators, and was "loved by his workforce". She felt his career had faced more difficulties with bridging a divide between "Greater Manchester's resurgent [city centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_city_centre "Manchester city centre") and its struggling post-industrial towns" and a tendency to "respond emotionally" to issues like homelessness "and leave the detail for later".

### Homelessness and housing

The issue of [homelessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homelessness "Homelessness") in Greater Manchester was a major focus of Burnham's mayoral campaign. He pledged to donate 15% of his mayoral salary to charities tackling homelessness if elected. After his election he outlined his plan to launch a "homelessness fund", with money going to homeless charities and mental health and rehabilitation services. He pledged to end [rough sleeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rough_sleeping "Rough sleeping") in Greater Manchester by 2020, but in November 2019 he admitted he would miss his target.

As mayor, Burnham continued longstanding local policies of supporting construction, especially of housing and [high-rise development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_and_structures_in_Greater_Manchester "List of tallest buildings and structures in Greater Manchester"); Jacob Reid of [Bloomberg News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_News "Bloomberg News") felt that this had succeeded in keeping Manchester's city centre "booming again" after "decades of decline". Burnham opposed adding targets for affordable housing into private construction projects, arguing that it "doesn't work" and deters construction.

### Public transport

In 2020, Burnham signed off on a new £10 yearly charge for pensioners who wished to continue to use their TFGM travel passes on the region's trains and trams. The charge was intended to help fund a London-style bus system. Pensioners resident in London boroughs get free travel on most public transport in London from the age of 60, while Burnham kept the Manchester system linked to the much later state pension age.

Burnham pledged to bring Manchester's bus network back into public ownership by 2025. The plans were legally challenged by bus operators [Stagecoach Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecoach_Group "Stagecoach Group") and [Rotala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotala "Rotala"), but in March 2022 the mayor and authority won the case at the High Court. Media analysts commented that the ruling could pave the way for other city regions in England to regulate bus services that had been [privatised since the 1980s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_deregulation_in_Great_Britain "Bus deregulation in Great Britain"). Capped fares of £2 for adult single fares were introduced in September 2022. The [integrated transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_transport_network "Integrated transport network") [Bee Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee_Network "Bee Network") was established in Greater Manchester in 2023.

### COVID-19 pandemic

In March 2020, Burnham called for clearer advice on slowing the spread of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_Kingdom "COVID-19 pandemic in the United Kingdom"), citing his previous experience as health secretary during the [2009 swine flu pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_swine_flu_pandemic "2009 swine flu pandemic"). He welcomed the additional measures implemented across Greater Manchester and [Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire "Lancashire") by [Secretary of State for Health and Social Care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Health_and_Social_Care "Secretary of State for Health and Social Care") [Matt Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Hancock "Matt Hancock") and [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer "Chancellor of the Exchequer") [Rishi Sunak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi_Sunak "Rishi Sunak") in July during the pandemic, in the knowledge that (at the time) some areas across [North West England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_England "North West England") had lower infection rates than the rest of the country.

On 15 October 2020, Burnham, along with other North West leaders, backed away from government talks to place Greater Manchester in tier 3 – the most restrictive level – of a new [three-tier categorisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_COVID-19_tier_regulations_in_England "First COVID-19 tier regulations in England"). He cited the grants system for businesses and 80% furlough scheme for employees as insufficient, saying they would push people into poverty and destitution which would outweigh the impact of the virus if mitigated correctly. Many of the concerns such as the impact on businesses and employees were shared by local Conservative MPs in Greater Manchester and surrounding areas. For his campaigning to secure more [furlough funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronavirus_Job_Retention_Scheme "Coronavirus Job Retention Scheme"), he was dubbed the "King of the [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_England "Northern England")" by the media. He did not secure as much extra money as he had wanted, being forced to lower his request for £90 million to £65 million.

### Investigations into child sexual exploitation

Shortly after first being elected as mayor in 2017, Burnham initiated a review of historical child sexual abuse allegations in Manchester and Rochdale, later expanding the scope of the investigation to Oldham. Part one of the review (focusing on Manchester) reported in 2020, part two (focusing on Oldham) reported in 2022, part three (focusing on Rochdale) reported in 2023 and part four (led by [HMICFRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMICFRS "HMICFRS") and covering the whole of Greater Manchester) reported in 2025.

In January 2025, Burnham backed calls for a national [public inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_inquiry "Public inquiry") with limited scope into [group-based child sexual exploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grooming_gangs_scandal "Grooming gangs scandal") and the power to compel people to give evidence. This came a day after the House of Commons voted against a [wrecking amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrecking_amendment "Wrecking amendment") to the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Bill calling for an inquiry. The amendment, proposed by the Conservative Party, had been subject to a whip which prevented Labour MPs from supporting it.

### Greater Manchester Baccalaureate

Upon his third re-election in 2024, Burnham expressed his wish to "give everyone growing up [in Manchester] an equal alternative to the university route"[*[non-primary source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research#Primary,_secondary_and_tertiary_sources "Wikipedia:No original research")*] through the creation of a baccalaureate in collaboration with local and prospective businesses, in an attempt to provide certainty that young people in Greater Manchester have the skills needed by businesses in the area. The scheme, nicknamed the MBacc, aims to be fully operational by 2030.

### National profile and resignation

Burnham was touted by many commentators as a potential successor to Labour Party leader and incumbent Prime Minister [Keir Starmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keir_Starmer "Keir Starmer"), whom he supported during the [2020 leadership election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2020 Labour Party leadership election (UK)"). Following Burnham's landslide re-election in 2021, he emerged as the bookmakers' favourite to become the next Labour leader. Asked about his ambitions, Burnham said his role as mayor was his priority, but made himself available in case "they [Labour] needed me one day in the future". In September 2023, Burnham was ranked twelfth on the *New Statesman*'s Left Power List, described as a "key dissenter" and a "crucial voice" in the party, as well as a potential future party leader.

Starmer went on to win the [2024 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election "2024 United Kingdom general election"), returning Labour to government. During the Sky News 2024 general election night broadcast, Burnham served as a guest analyst alongside former Scottish Conservatives leader [Ruth Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Davidson "Ruth Davidson"). While Starmer's honeymoon period was brief, Burnham maintained high approval in Greater Manchester and, by August 2025, polls identified Burnham as the most popular senior Labour figure.

On 24 January 2026, Burnham applied to stand as Labour's candidate in the [2026 Gorton and Denton by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Gorton_and_Denton_by-election "2026 Gorton and Denton by-election"), but was blocked in an 8–1 vote by the NEC on 25 January 2026. The by-election was ultimately won by the [Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Party_of_England_and_Wales "Green Party of England and Wales") candidate [Hannah Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Spencer "Hannah Spencer"). Burnham had previously beaten Spencer in the 2024 mayoral election by a margin of 375,000 votes.

The Labour Party's poor performance in the [2026 local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_Kingdom_local_elections "2026 United Kingdom local elections") led to a [crisis around Starmer's leadership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Labour_Party_leadership_crisis "2026 Labour Party leadership crisis"), during which Burnham was widely speculated to succeed Starmer as prime minister and Labour leader. On 11 May, [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner "Angela Rayner") said that the NEC blocking Burnham's return to parliament was a mistake. Burnham travelled to [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London") on 12 May to meet MPs.

Following Burnham's by-election victory in Makerfield and subsequent return to parliament in June 2026, Burnham resigned as Mayor of Greater Manchester on 19 June 2026, since MPs are not allowed to hold the office of a mayor with police and crime commissioner powers. The resulting [mayoral by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_by-election "2026 Greater Manchester mayoral by-election") was set for 30 July 2026, with the winner, [Bev Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bev_Craig "Bev Craig") – former leader of [Manchester City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_City_Council "Manchester City Council"), due to serve until the [election in 2028](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2028_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election "2028 Greater Manchester mayoral election"). [Paul Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dennett "Paul Dennett"), the [mayor of Salford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Salford "Mayor of Salford") and Burnham's deputy mayor, served as an interim mayor until the election.

## 2026 leadership campaign

### Return to parliament (2026)

On 14 May 2026, [Josh Simons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Simons "Josh Simons"), the MP for [Makerfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makerfield_(constituency) "Makerfield (constituency)"), announced his resignation. He said that he wanted to trigger a [by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Makerfield_by-election "2026 Makerfield by-election") in which Burnham could stand, and be eligible to challenge Starmer for the party leadership and premiership if he won. On 19 May 2026, Burnham became Labour's candidate for Makerfield, after no other potential candidate made the shortlist. The by-election was described as one of the most consequential in recent British history.

The *[Manchester Evening News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Evening_News "Manchester Evening News")* reported that Burnham would frame his campaign around [trust in politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_efficacy "Political efficacy") and "economic renewal", alongside more [devolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution_in_the_United_Kingdom "Devolution in the United Kingdom") of local powers. He named [house building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_building "House building") as one of his priorities for the area, alongside tackling [anti-social behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-social_behaviour "Anti-social behaviour") by building "[youth zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youth_center "Youth center")" in the area. He also pledged support for the area's [coalfield community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_mining_in_the_United_Kingdom "Coal mining in the United Kingdom"). In an interview with [ITV News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_News "ITV News"), he denied that he would campaign on a platform of rejoining the European Union, despite his support for the proposal "in the long term"; he also stated that the time had come for Labour to "take on" Reform, and would not be asking other parties to stand down for him.

The campaign's slogan was "Andy — For Us", with a stylised cartoon image of Burnham by Mancunian artist [Stanley Chow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Chow "Stanley Chow") featured on Labour's campaign material. The Manchester band [Oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oasis_(band) "Oasis (band)") gave Burnham permission to use their song "[Some Might Say](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Some_Might_Say "Some Might Say")" in the campaign. Burnham's use of social media was widely noted as a strength of his campaign, with reports that he had been resharing memes and fan account content supporting his candidacy, particularly referencing his daily running routine.[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

Burnham's victory returned him to the House of Commons for the first time since 2017 and made him eligible to stand in any Labour leadership contest. He won 54.8% of the vote, increasing Labour's majority in Makerfield to 9,231 votes, compared with Josh Simons' 5,399 vote majority over Reform UK at the 2024 general election. Burnham won more votes than all other parties combined, with the Conservative, Liberal Democrat and Green candidates all losing their deposits. Turnout was 58.8%, up from 52.5% in the seat for the 2024 general election, the highest for a parliamentary by-election since [Brecon and Radnorshire in 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Brecon_and_Radnorshire_by-election "2019 Brecon and Radnorshire by-election"), with the third highest rise on a general election since 1945.

In his victory speech, Burnham said Labour had a "final chance to change" and described the result as a possible turning point in British politics. He called for a "Makerfield test" at the heart of national politics, under which policies would be judged by whether they worked for the many regional communities who felt they were consistently overlooked and neglected by Westminster. He also said he would prioritise local needs over party point-scoring, focus on practical problem-solving, seek to heal divisions after the campaign, and support greater powers for the North.

Starmer congratulated Burnham and said the result showed that the tide was turning on Reform UK, which he said had "probably" reached the peak of its support. He also initially said he would stand in any Labour leadership contest and would not "walk away". The result increased pressure on Starmer within the Labour Party, with some Labour figures calling for an orderly leadership transition. News media reported that over 100 Labour MPs were preparing to call on Starmer to resign, and allies suggested Burnham might have the backing of around 300 out of 403 Labour MPs.

### Leadership election and transition

On 22 June 2026, Starmer announced that he would stand down as prime minister, remaining in office until a new leader was elected. In response to Burnham's subsequent announcement that he would stand in the [ensuing leadership election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2026 Labour Party leadership election (UK)"), [Wes Streeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wes_Streeting "Wes Streeting") endorsed him, a move which led political experts to conclude the election would be a "coronation" more than a formal contest, as Streeting had been considered Burnham's most likely opponent. Burnham was [sworn in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_Allegiance_(United_Kingdom)#Parliamentarians "Oath of Allegiance (United Kingdom)") as an MP later the same day, with BBC News and Sky News covering his entire train journey from Manchester to London. Burnham was already widely seen as Starmer's likely successor; he was the only declared candidate and had received a significant number of endorsements.

Burnham secured nominations from 349 MPs for the leadership election by 13 July, making it mathematically impossible for anyone else to stand (under Labour rules, a leadership candidate must be nominated by 20 per cent of the party's MPs in order to stand). By the close of nominations on 15 July, Burnham had secured 379 nominations, more than 90% of the Parliamentary Labour Party. He was therefore elected unopposed on 17 July.

## Premiership (2026–present)

### Appointment

As the leader of the majority party in the House of Commons, Burnham was appointed prime minister, [First Lord of the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Lord_of_the_Treasury "First Lord of the Treasury") and [Minister for the Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_the_Civil_Service "Minister for the Civil Service") by [King Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III "King Charles III") on 20 July 2026. Burnham and his wife, [Marie-France van Heel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie-France_van_Heel "Marie-France van Heel"), were driven from [Buckingham Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckingham_Palace "Buckingham Palace") to [Downing Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_Street "Downing Street"). Upon taking office, Burnham became the first prime minister to have been raised [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") and the first to belong to the [Co-operative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-operative_Party "Co-operative Party").

In his first speech as prime minister, Burnham said he was "acutely conscious" of being the sixth prime minister in ten years, and vowed to bring power "out of here and carry it into every postcode in the land".

### Cabinet

Burnham started to form his cabinet on 20 July 2026, which first met on 21 July, and he completed his ministerial appointments on 22 July. The initial appointments resulted in major cabinet changes, featuring a significant removal of close Starmer allies, while returning and rewarding those contributing to his downfall. Key departures from the [previous ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starmer_ministry "Starmer ministry") included [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves "Rachel Reeves"), who was removed as Chancellor after reportedly turning down an alternative role; [David Lammy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lammy "David Lammy"), who was dismissed as Deputy Prime Minister and Justice Secretary; Business Secretary [Peter Kyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Kyle "Peter Kyle"); Housing Secretary [Steve Reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Reed_(politician) "Steve Reed (politician)"); Attorney General [Lord Hermer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hermer,_Baron_Hermer "Richard Hermer, Baron Hermer"); Chief Secretary to the Prime Minister, Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster and Minister for Intergovernmental Affairs [Darren Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darren_Jones "Darren Jones"); and Science, Innovation and Technology Secretary [Liz Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Kendall "Liz Kendall"), whose department was dissolved. 15 were dismissed from the cabinet.[*[non-primary source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research#Primary,_secondary_and_tertiary_sources "Wikipedia:No original research")*]

Following the major dismissals, Burnham began selecting the senior members of his government, making many high-profile appointments and retentions. [John Healey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Healey "John Healey") returned to government as Chancellor of the Exchequer, while [Ed Miliband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Miliband "Ed Miliband") was promoted to Foreign Secretary from his former Energy and Net Zero brief. Close ally [Louise Haigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Haigh "Louise Haigh") was appointed First Secretary of State, Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster, and Minister for the Cabinet Office (his *de facto* Deputy Prime Minister). [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner "Angela Rayner") returned to government in her previous post of Housing Secretary, [Yvette Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvette_Cooper "Yvette Cooper") was reshuffled to become Health Secretary and [Wes Streeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wes_Streeting "Wes Streeting") re-joined the cabinet with his appointment as Defence Secretary. [Jonathan Reynolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Reynolds "Jonathan Reynolds") was promoted to the newly expanded brief of Business, Science, Innovation and Trade Secretary, while Labour Deputy Leader [Lucy Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Powell "Lucy Powell") became Education Secretary, and [Emma Reynolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Reynolds "Emma Reynolds") was appointed as Chief Secretary to the Treasury. Retentions from Starmer's cabinet included [Shabana Mahmood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabana_Mahmood "Shabana Mahmood") as Home Secretary, [Pat McFadden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_McFadden "Pat McFadden") as Work and Pensions Secretary, [Alan Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Campbell_(politician) "Alan Campbell (politician)") as Leader of the Commons, [Lisa Nandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Nandy "Lisa Nandy") as Culture Secretary, and [Heidi Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidi_Alexander "Heidi Alexander") as Transport Secretary.

Burnham's cabinet is the first in history with a majority of female ministerial appointments, with 12 of the 23 members of the cabinet (which includes the Prime Minister) being women. Additionally, another two female ministers will attend cabinet meetings.

### Domestic policy

In his first speech as Prime Minister, Burnham mentioned [re-industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Re-industrialisation "Re-industrialisation"), mitigating [the cost-of-living crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_cost-of-living_crisis "United Kingdom cost-of-living crisis"), education system reform, construction of [council homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_homes "Council homes"), and ending [rough sleeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rough_sleeping "Rough sleeping") as priorities. An additional £340 million is to be allocated to tackling rough sleeping over the course of three years.

A removal of the 5% [value-added tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value-added_tax "Value-added tax") on household electricity bills in England, Scotland, and Wales is scheduled for October 2026. The cut will not apply in Northern Ireland, where [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union "European Union") VAT rates apply; instead, the Northern Irish government will be allocated its own funding for cost-of-living measures. The government claimed the policy will be funded with savings from scrapping Starmer's planned digital ID scheme.

Single bus fares in England (except London) are to be capped at £2 from January 2027 to December 2027. As of July 2026, England's nationwide cap was £3, which was planned under Starmer's premiership to expire in March 2027. Money is to be allocated for the devolved governments to take similar action. Approximately £400 million of the scheme's expected cost (more than £500 million) is to be funded by turning international climate-related donations into loans. [Business rates for English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_rates_in_England "Business rates in England") pubs, clubs, and live music venues are planned to be reduced by 20% from April 2027. The reduction is to be targeted, excluding the most successful music venues. In July 2026, Burnham paused the implementation of [Sentencing Act 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentencing_Act_2026 "Sentencing Act 2026")'s prisoner early release scheme in England and Wales, which was previously scheduled for September 2026, to review the scheme.

Along with still working at the prime minister's traditional office at [10 Downing Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10_Downing_Street "10 Downing Street") in London, Burnham established [Number 10 North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_10_North "Number 10 North"), an office initially at Heron House moving to [Manchester Digital Campus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Digital_Campus "Manchester Digital Campus") upon completion in 2032. Both locations were grouped together under a new Office for the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, overseen by [Louise Haigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Haigh "Louise Haigh"), [First Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Secretary_of_State "First Secretary of State"). Burnham is expected to work at Number 10 North at least one day per week, barring scheduling conflicts. Relocation packages to work at Number 10 North have been offered to [civil servants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Service_(United_Kingdom) "Civil Service (United Kingdom)") based in London. Burnham is open to expanding devolution of policing to Wales.

### Foreign policy

Burnham held introductory phone calls with United States president [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump"), Ukrainian president [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volodymyr_Zelenskyy "Volodymyr Zelenskyy"), and European Commission president [Ursula von der Leyen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_von_der_Leyen "Ursula von der Leyen") shortly after taking office on 20 July 2026. In his first official international conversation, Burnham congratulated President Trump on a successful World Cup hosting tournament, outlined plans for British reindustrialisation, and extended an invitation to visit Manchester for the upcoming G20 summit, while Trump detailed that they discussed trade, North Sea oil, and military alliances. During his subsequent dialogue with President Zelenskyy, Burnham reaffirmed that the United Kingdom's diplomatic, military, and political backing for Ukraine amidst the [Russian invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine "2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine") remains steadfast and unchanged under his new government. To conclude his initial round of foreign diplomacy, Burnham spoke with Ursula von der Leyen to express a desire to build a closer and more ambitious relationship with the European Union, resulting in an agreement for their respective teams to coordinate toward a UK-EU summit later in the year.

## Political views

Burnham has said that he joined the Labour Party at the age of 15. He identifies as a [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist "Socialist"). In his [2010 leadership bid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Labour_Party_leadership_election_(UK) "2010 Labour Party leadership election (UK)") Burnham emphasised his philosophy of "aspirational socialism", which he described as [redistributive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistribution_of_income_and_wealth "Redistribution of income and wealth"), [collectivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivist "Collectivist") and [internationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalism_(politics) "Internationalism (politics)"). He has been cited as being a [social democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democrat "Social democrat"), favouring stronger public services but not fully opposing a [market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy "Market economy").

Burnham is a strong opponent of [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism"), which he has described as an "ugly brand of politics". In 2020, [Iain Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iain_Martin "Iain Martin") of *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* described Burnham as a "former [Blairite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blairite "Blairite")" and associated with [New Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Labour "New Labour"). In a 2010 interview on *[The Andrew Marr Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Andrew_Marr_Show "The Andrew Marr Show")*, Burnham said New Labour "did great things during its thirteen years in office" but "there was a perception that we weren't listening to people." As mayor of Greater Manchester, Burnham rejected the approach taken by New Labour on housing and transport, but remained committed to its "[tough on crime, tough on the causes of crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tough_on_crime,_tough_on_the_causes_of_crime "Tough on crime, tough on the causes of crime")" approach. Politically, Burnham places himself on the [soft left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_left "Soft left") of the Labour Party. He has been described as soft left by a number of media outlets, including the *[Financial Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Times "Financial Times")*, the *[New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman "New Statesman")*, and [LabourList](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LabourList "LabourList"). Following his election as Labour Party leader in 2026, Burnham described [Neil Kinnock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Kinnock "Neil Kinnock"), Labour's leader when he joined the party, as his inspiration.

### Manchesterism

Burnham has used the term "Manchesterism" for the political philosophy underpinning his policy agenda as mayor of Greater Manchester – not to be confused with the 19th-century pro-free-trade [Manchester Liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Liberalism "Manchester Liberalism") associated with [Richard Cobden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Cobden "Richard Cobden") and [John Bright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bright "John Bright"), also known as Manchesterism. Burnham has characterised Manchesterism as "the end of [neo-liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-liberalism "Neo-liberalism")", "business-friendly [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism_in_the_United_Kingdom "Socialism in the United Kingdom")" and "a modern and functional response to the high-inequality, low-growth trap that came from the 1980s drive to privatise economic power and overcentralise political power in the Treasury". In a speech to the [Centre for Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_Cities "Centre for Cities"), Burnham described it as a perspective that aimed to combine economic development with social benefits, with policies designed to explicitly channel the proceeds of growth towards communities, rejecting [trickle-down economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trickle-down_economics "Trickle-down economics"). Described as being elements of [social democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democracy "Social democracy"), examples of such policies he cited included re-establishing public control of buses through the creation of the [Bee Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee_Network "Bee Network"), and the Good Growth Fund, which provided £1 billion for three projects in each of Greater Manchester's boroughs. He stressed that such initiatives were only possible due to [devolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution_in_the_United_Kingdom "Devolution in the United Kingdom"), countering historical trends of political centralisation, although he also advocated for increased devolution of fiscal powers.

Burnham has also argued that the [cost-of-living crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-of-living_crisis "Cost-of-living crisis") of the 2020s has roots in [neoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism "Neoliberalism"), suggesting that the [deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation "Deregulation") and privatisation of the 1980s led to increases in the costs of essential goods, noting the effects of [Right to Buy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_Buy "Right to Buy") on an unregulated rental sector resulting in increasing state expenditure on [Housing Benefit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_Benefit "Housing Benefit"). He contends that such problems were exacerbated by [government austerity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_government_austerity_programme "United Kingdom government austerity programme") and [Brexit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit "Brexit"). Manchesterism has also been characterised as embodying collaboration and partnership working between stakeholders, including cooperation between government, business and trade unions, different public services, community groups, and administrations of different political complexions among Greater Manchester's borough councils.

Writing for the *[Financial Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Times "Financial Times")* in May 2026, [Stephen Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Bush "Stephen Bush") argued that the policy offer Burnham was making as a prospective Labour Party leader was to the left of the policy he had actually implemented during his mayoralty, which in turn was to the right of the stance of the [Starmer ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starmer_ministry "Starmer ministry"). Bush suggested that this may have been due to the institutional constraints on, and weaknesses of, the metro mayor role. In a January 2026 piece for the *[London Review of Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Review_of_Books "London Review of Books")*, [Michael Chessum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Chessum "Michael Chessum") compared Manchesterism's emphasis on moving away from neoliberalism to the approaches to governance advocated by the socialist parties of [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Socialist_Workers%27_Party "Spanish Socialist Workers' Party") and [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_(Portugal) "Socialist Party (Portugal)").

### Economics

In his 2015 leadership bid, Burnham pledged to commit Labour to "a policy of progressive [renationalisation of the railway system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_of_the_privatisation_of_British_Rail#Political_positions_post-privatisation "Impact of the privatisation of British Rail")". Following the 2024 general election, Labour steadily progressed with the previous Conservative government's plan to nationalise the railway system, aiming for completion by 2027. Burnham also favours a universal [graduate tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graduate_tax "Graduate tax") to replace [student tuition fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuition_fees_in_the_United_Kingdom "Tuition fees in the United Kingdom"). He has advocated a [National Care Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Care_Service "National Care Service"), integrating care services into the National Health Service. In his leadership bid, Burnham's key economic policies included a new levy to fund social care, extending the [higher minimum wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Living_Wage "National Living Wage") to under-25s, and banning [zero-hour contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-hour_contract "Zero-hour contract"). Burnham described the [mansion tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansion_tax "Mansion tax") proposed by [Ed Miliband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Miliband "Ed Miliband") as "the politics of envy", saying he knew it would lose votes when his mother phoned and told him it represented a return to the 1970s.

In December 2016 during his initial mayoral campaign Burnham argued the UK needed to leave the European Single Market to allow greater scope for government intervention in Northern industries.

In May 2026, Burnham vowed to put energy, housing, water and transport under "stronger public control". He has said that [Thames Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_Water "Thames Water") should be nationalised.

The Office for Budget Responsibility cited the UK's triple lock pension system as a large factor to the increased instability in public financials. The system mandates pensions rise by 2.5%, average earnings, or inflation whichever is higher. The cost was expected to rise from 5% to 9% of GDP in the next 50 years. Burnham has proposed ending the triple lock system in 2030, which is estimated to save £50 million by 2050. The Institute for Fiscal Studies called it a "welcomed and substantial step" to creating stability in the pension system.

### Devolution

Burnham is a strong supporter of [devolving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution_to_the_North_of_England "Devolution to the North of England") power and, in his 2015 leadership campaign, criticised the "[Westminster Bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Bubble "Westminster Bubble")" – the London-centric focus in British politics and perceived detachment from life outside Westminster. Some opponents and political commentators accused him of being a part of the same bubble he criticises. He views devolution of powers to Greater Manchester (including its elected mayor) as an opportunity for [urban regeneration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_regeneration "Urban regeneration"). He also called for a focus on [Northern identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_England#Culture_and_identity "Northern England"). After he was elected as mayor of Greater Manchester, he described the new powers for northern cities as "the dawn of a new era". Burnham feels the government does not invest enough money in the North of England, saying: "Almost five years after the government promised us a [northern powerhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Powerhouse "Northern Powerhouse"), we learn that public spending in the north has fallen while rising in the south. This has got to stop and it is time that the north came to the front of the queue for public investment".

In June 2026, Burnham announced [Number 10 North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_10_North "Number 10 North") which he described as being to "oversee the biggest [rebalancing of power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralization "Decentralization") our country has ever seen". He said that Number 10 North would be the "nerve centre of a rewired Britain". Burnham set out three "clear tasks" for Number 10 North; to increase [public ownership of essential utilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_utilities "Public utilities") such as water, energy and housing, to [re-industrialise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reindustrialization "Reindustrialization") swathes of the country, and to [regenerate towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_Regeneration "Urban Regeneration"), prioritising places that had been left behind. Upon establishment, Number 10 North additionally assumed responsibility for [English devolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_devolution "English devolution") and local economic growth strategy from the [Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Housing,_Communities_and_Local_Government "Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government") (MHCLG), and local economic growth policy from [HM Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury "HM Treasury"). After Burnham became prime minister on 20 July 2026, it was reported that Number 10 North was already up and running with Heron House, an existing government building in [Manchester city centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_city_centre "Manchester city centre"), which is partially occupied by [GCHQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCHQ "GCHQ"), serving as its interim headquarters. Its first meeting will be reviving the [National Economic Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Economic_Council_(United_Kingdom) "National Economic Council (United Kingdom)") on 24 July 2026, bringing together government ministers and [regional mayors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_mayor "Regional mayor").

### Immigration

In 2016, as Shadow Home Secretary, Burnham opposed migration caps. Burnham also opposed a bill requiring landlords to conduct immigration checks for illegal immigrants.

In 2017, Burnham argued ending freedom of movement must be prioritised over the economic benefits of single market membership during the UK's [Brexit negotiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit_negotiations "Brexit negotiations").

It was reported in May 2026 that Burnham supports Home Secretary [Shabana Mahmood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabana_Mahmood "Shabana Mahmood")'s efforts to limit [legal and illegal immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_immigration_to_the_United_Kingdom "Modern immigration to the United Kingdom"). The same month, he stood back from past calls to scrap the "no recourse to public funds" rule, which prevents immigrants getting access to benefits or public housing before being granted [settled status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settled_status "Settled status").

### Foreign policy

#### Iraq War (2003)

Burnham voted in favour of British military intervention in the [2003 invasion of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_invasion_of_Iraq "2003 invasion of Iraq"), aligning with the stance of then-Prime Minister [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair "Tony Blair") and US President [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush "George W. Bush"). He has since described the vote as "traumatising on every level" and his "worst experience" in Parliament. In his 2024 book *Head North*, Burnham expressed deep regret for his decision, stating, "If I had my time again, I wouldn't have voted for it". He criticised the "rage rhetoric and haste" driven by the Blair and Bush administrations, while highlighting the catastrophic absence of a viable post-war stabilisation plan.[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

#### United States

Burnham's stance on the United States mixes political criticism with practical diplomacy. His economic and trade strategy takes cues from [Joe Biden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biden "Joe Biden")'s policies on manufacturing and workers' rights. He also has long-standing ties to US Democrats; his Director of Political Strategy, Matthew McGregor, worked on [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama")'s [2012 campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama_2012_presidential_campaign "Barack Obama 2012 presidential campaign"). By contrast, Burnham has been highly critical of [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump"), once urging UK voters to reject the "divided, dark politics" seen in America.

Since becoming Prime Minister, Burnham has tried to build a working relationship with the Trump administration. In their first phone call, Burnham pointed to his plan to raise UK defence spending to 3.5% of GDP and his decision to let US forces use British bases for strikes against Iran. Trump's public response to Burnham has shifted over time. He initially dismissed Burnham as "the mayor of a town" and "extremely liberal", but later praised Burnham's North Sea oil policies, saying they would make the UK "one of the richest countries anywhere." Tensions remain, however, because Burnham appointed Ed Miliband—a vocal Trump critic—as Foreign Secretary, which angered White House officials.

#### NATO

In 2015, Burnham drew a hard line on national security during the Labour leadership race, warning that he would quit the Shadow Cabinet if the party ever backed leaving [NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO "NATO"). This was a direct challenge to Corbyn, who had questioned the military alliance and suggesting Britain should withdraw. Burnham's ultimatum put the spotlight on a massive ideological rift within Labour, forcing a tense debate over the party's future foreign policy and global standing.

#### China

Burnham has viewed [relations with China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_Kingdom_relations "China–United Kingdom relations") primarily through the lens of economic development. As Mayor of Greater Manchester, he visited China in 2018, where he said China's [high-speed rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-speed_rail_in_China "High-speed rail in China") and infrastructure made a strong impression. During a meeting with the Chinese consul Tang Rui in April 2026, Burnham supported closer cooperation "in areas such as green development, electric buses, advanced manufacturing and life sciences".

#### Ukraine and Russia

Burnham has supported [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine") since the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine "2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine") amidst the wider [Russo-Ukrainian war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war "Russo-Ukrainian war"), stating, "We must not allow Russia to prevail... It is essential that we continue to support Ukraine in every possible way". In 2014, as the shadow health secretary, he condemned the [2014 Russian annexation of Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Russian_annexation_of_Crimea "2014 Russian annexation of Crimea") and argued Russia should not be allowed to host the [2018 FIFA World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_FIFA_World_Cup "2018 FIFA World Cup").

As Mayor of Greater Manchester, he practised this stance by establishing the Unbroken Cities Network alongside the [mayors of Lviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Lviv "Mayor of Lviv") and [Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Liverpool "Mayor of Liverpool") to support municipal recovery and rehabilitation programs for injured Ukrainians.

Upon succeeding Starmer as Prime Minister on 20 July 2026, Burnham immediately elevated his support to the state level. In one of his first official actions on his first day in office, he held a phone call with Zelenskyy. During the conversation, Burnham explicitly reaffirmed that the UK's "steadfast and resolute" commitment to Ukraine would remain completely unchanged under his leadership, promising to stand with him "100%".

#### Israel and Palestine

During the 2015 leadership campaign, Burnham pledged that his first overseas trip would be to Israel and joined [Labour Friends of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Friends_of_Israel "Labour Friends of Israel"). He also described the [Boycott, Divestment and Sanctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boycott,_Divestment_and_Sanctions "Boycott, Divestment and Sanctions") (BDS) movement as "spiteful." However, following the outbreak of the [Gaza war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_war "Gaza war") in October 2023, he became one of the first high-profile Labour figures to break ranks with then-leader Keir Starmer by publicly calling for a ceasefire. While condemning the [October 7 attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_7_attacks "October 7 attacks"), Burnham cited profound concerns over civilian casualties and the humanitarian blockade. He has consistently backed a [two-state solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-state_solution "Two-state solution"), denounced illegal [West Bank settlements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bank_settlement "West Bank settlement"), and co-signed a June 2025 letter demanding the protection of [Palestinian statehood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_statehood "Palestinian statehood")—which the previous Starmer government [subsequently recognised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine%E2%80%93United_Kingdom_relations "Palestine–United Kingdom relations") that September. Speaking on 9 July 2026, Burnham apologised for the initial stance Labour took toward the conflict, stating that the UK had been too slow to call for a ceasefire and needed to apply more pressure on the Israeli government. He also called for accountability through the international courts.

#### Iran

Burnham has been highly critical of direct Western military engagement in the [2026 Iran war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war "2026 Iran war"). In March 2026, following a joint US-Israeli offensive ordered by US President [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump") and Israeli Prime Minister [Benjamin Netanyahu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Netanyahu "Benjamin Netanyahu") that killed Iran's Supreme Leader [Ali Khamenei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Khamenei "Ali Khamenei"), Burnham warned that the conflict directly paralleled the strategic failures of the Iraq War. He challenged the simplicity of Western intervention models, arguing that the idea that you can "take out a leader and then the whole of the population there just unites behind that change... is simplistic". Burnham strongly advocated for diplomatic care and caution, urging leaders like Trump and Netanyahu not to rush headlong into the conflict without a thorough, viable plan for the regional aftermath.

### Sexuality and gender

Burnham supported the use of [all-women shortlists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-women_shortlist "All-women shortlist") for parliamentary candidate selections. In an interview in *[The Daily Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph "The Daily Telegraph")* in October 2007, Burnham said: "I think it's better when children are in a home where their parents are married" and "it's not wrong that the tax system should recognise commitment and marriage".

Burnham voted in favour of [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_the_United_Kingdom "Same-sex marriage in the United Kingdom") [in 2013](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_(Same_Sex_Couples)_Act_2013 "Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013"). In 2026, *[Attitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attitude_(magazine) "Attitude (magazine)")* magazine wrote that he had overall "one of the most consistently pro-LGBTQ+ records in politics."

Burnham was criticised in 2015 for jokingly saying that Labour should have a woman leader "when the time is right", with the *[New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman "New Statesman")* saying that he had "tripped over his mouth again". He abstained on the government's welfare bill, despite having previously described the legislation as "unsupportable".

In February 2020, Burnham met with the [anti-transgender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-transgender_movement_in_the_United_Kingdom "Anti-transgender movement in the United Kingdom") UK organisation [LGB Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGB_Alliance "LGB Alliance") to discuss a letter he had written to the government concerning changes to the [Gender Recognition Act 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_Recognition_Act_2004 "Gender Recognition Act 2004"). When concerns were raised over it being an anti-transgender organisation, he replied that there "was no mention in the letter of the LGB Alliance" before the meeting, and his representatives replied that he had "made his support for the trans community very clear over the years".[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*] In May 2026, Burnham said that people need to "stop arguing" about [transgender rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgender_rights_in_the_United_Kingdom "Transgender rights in the United Kingdom"), and have a "live and let live" approach, while simultaneously retracting his prior support for trans people being allowed to use single-sex facilities, instead voicing support for the regulations enacted by the [Equality and Human Rights Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_and_Human_Rights_Commission "Equality and Human Rights Commission") after the court case *[For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_Women_Scotland_Ltd_v_The_Scottish_Ministers "For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers")*.

### Constitutional and electoral reform

Burnham is a supporter of replacing the [House of Lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords "House of Lords") with an [elected Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_of_the_House_of_Lords "Reform of the House of Lords") of the [nations and regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nations_and_Regions "Nations and Regions") and for switching elections to the House of Commons to a form of [proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_representation "Proportional representation"). He has also spoken in favour of the non-proportional [alternative vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_vote "Alternative vote") and advocated reform of the [whipping system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whip_(politics) "Whip (politics)") to make it easier for MPs to vote in their constituency's interests. In a May 2026 interview, as part of his campaign for the [2026 Makerfield by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Makerfield_by-election "2026 Makerfield by-election"), Burnham said that he would require electoral reform "to be in a manifesto and endorsed at a general election", rejecting the idea of introducing proportional representation in the current parliament.

### *Head North*

In 2024, Burnham co-authored *[Head North: A Rallying Cry for a More Equal Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_North "Head North")* with the [Liverpool City Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_City_Region "Liverpool City Region") Mayor [Steve Rotheram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Rotheram "Steve Rotheram"). The book was described by Benjamin Myers for *[The Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Observer "The Observer")* as "offering hope to the northern regions when it is most needed, and reminding us that those politicians who refuse to toe the party line are often those who history remembers most favourably." In contrast, it was described by Jonathan Ball in the *[New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman "New Statesman")* as simplifying the [North–South divide in England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North%E2%80%93South_divide_in_England "North–South divide in England") to "'London elite' vs 'neglected north' caricatures".

## Personal life

Burnham married American-born [Marie-France van Heel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie-France_van_Heel "Marie-France van Heel"), who is of Dutch descent, in October 2000; they have been in a relationship since their university days. The couple have a son and two daughters. The family reside in [Golborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golborne "Golborne"), Greater Manchester. Burnham has two brothers, both teachers; Nick is the principal of [Cardinal Newman College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Newman_College "Cardinal Newman College"), a Catholic [Sixth Form College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_Form_College "Sixth Form College") in Preston, and John is headteacher of [Ormskirk School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ormskirk_School "Ormskirk School").

Burnham was brought up [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic "Catholic"). In the 2015 leadership contest, he praised [Pope Francis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Francis "Pope Francis") but urged him to promote a progressive stance on gay rights. In a newspaper interview during the contest, he said that he had been repeatedly at odds with the Catholic Church while he was an MP, and that this had strained personal relationships.

Burnham has stated that his primary national identity is a [British identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_national_identity "British national identity") rather than English, which he has attributed to his [Irish heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_people_in_Great_Britain "Irish people in Great Britain"). He has said that he sees himself as British first, a [north-westerner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_England "North West England") second, Scouse third, and English fourth.

He has a tattoo of a bee on his right arm in reference to the [Manchester worker bee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbols_of_Manchester#Worker_bee "Symbols of Manchester").

Burnham is a fan of [indie music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indie_music "Indie music"), after becoming interested in the [Manchester indie music scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_music_of_Manchester "Popular music of Manchester") during his youth. He has played a number of [DJ sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DJ_set "DJ set") to raise money for charity. Burnham is a supporter of [rugby league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_league "Rugby league"), having previously served as the honorary chairman of [Leigh Centurions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_Centurions "Leigh Centurions") for a short time, and is now an honorary vice-president. He was also president of the [Rugby Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_Football_League "Rugby Football League") from July 2018 until the summer of 2019. Burnham was a talented junior cricketer (playing for [Lancashire CCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire_CCC "Lancashire CCC") Juniors) and a keen footballer, and competed at both sports for his college.

Burnham is a lifelong fan of [Premier League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_League "Premier League") football club [Everton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everton_F.C. "Everton F.C."), and had held a season ticket at the club's [Hill Dickinson Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_Dickinson_Stadium "Hill Dickinson Stadium"), until he was forced to move to the director's box for safety reasons. In July 2003, Burnham played for [Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_League_(division) "National League (division)") club [Leigh RMI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_RMI "Leigh RMI") in a pre-season [friendly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhibition_game#Association_football "Exhibition game") against Everton. He came on as an 88th minute [substitute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitute_(association_football) "Substitute (association football)") for [Neil Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Robinson_(footballer,_born_1979) "Neil Robinson (footballer, born 1979)") in the 1–1 draw at [Hilton Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilton_Park_(stadium) "Hilton Park (stadium)"). He has also played for Labour's "[Demon Eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Labour,_New_Danger "New Labour, New Danger") F.C." football team, and the [Bristol City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_City_F.C. "Bristol City F.C.") London Supporters' team. Burnham is a keen [runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Running "Running"), and he has participated in the [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Marathon "London Marathon") and [Boston marathons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Marathon "Boston Marathon") and the [Great Manchester Run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Manchester_Run "Great Manchester Run") 10K.

In March 2023, Burnham was ordered to pay £1,984 and given six [penalty points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_points "Penalty points") after admitting to driving at 78 mph (126 km/h) on a motorway where there was a temporarily reduced 40 mph (64 km/h) speed limit, though he claimed he was "not aware of any variable speed limit in place on the smart motorway at the time".

On 14 September 2026, Burnham's father, Roy, died from complications of [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer%27s_disease "Alzheimer's disease"); he was 85. Burnham's office immediately cancelled his public engagements for the two days that followed, including a planned speech to the [TUC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trades_Union_Congress "Trades Union Congress") annual conference in [Brighton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton "Brighton").
