# Achaemenid Empire

The **Achaemenid Empire** ([/əˈkiː.mə.nɪd/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [*ə-KEE-mə-nid*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian_language "Old Persian language"): [𐎧𐏁𐏂](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%90%8E%A7%F0%90%8F%81%F0%90%8F%82%F0%90%8E%B6 "wikt:𐎧𐏁𐏂𐎶"), *Xšāça*, lit. 'The Empire' or 'The Kingdom') was an [ancient Iranian empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Iran#1 "History of Iran") founded by [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great") of the [Achaemenid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_dynasty "Achaemenid dynasty") in 550 BC. At peak, its territorial extent was roughly 5.5 million square kilometres (2.1 million square miles), making it the [largest empire of its time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_empires#Timeline_of_largest_empires_at_the_time "List of largest empires"). Based in the [Iranian plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_plateau "Iranian plateau"), it stretched from the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skudra "Skudra") and [Cyrenaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putaya "Putaya") in the west to the [Indus Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_conquest_of_the_Indus_Valley "Achaemenid conquest of the Indus Valley") in the east, including [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia"), [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history_of_Cyprus#Persian_period "Ancient history of Cyprus"), [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"), the [South Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Caucasus "South Caucasus"), parts of [Eastern Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Arabia "Eastern Arabia"), and large parts of [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia").

By the 7th century BC, the region of [Persis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persis "Persis"), located in the southwestern part of the Iranian plateau, had been settled by [Persian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Iranian_peoples "Ancient Iranian peoples"). From Persis, Cyrus rose and defeated [Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_kingdom "Median kingdom"), [Lydia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia "Lydia"), and the [Neo-Babylonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire "Neo-Babylonian Empire"), thus marking the establishment of a new imperial polity in the [ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East "Ancient Near East"). The empire expanded rapidly, but was repulsed from Scythia in 513 BC and from Greece in 492–490 BC and 480–479 BC.

In 330 BC, amidst a [Macedonian military campaign that began in 336 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Alexander_the_Great "Wars of Alexander the Great"), the Achaemenid Empire was conquered in its entirety by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great"), who annexed it to his [Macedonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Empire "Macedonian Empire"). Upon Alexander's death, which sparked the beginning of the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period") in 323 BC, the majority of the former Achaemenid Empire's territories came under the rule of the [Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire "Seleucid Empire") and the [Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom "Ptolemaic Kingdom"). Almost a century later, Iranian elites in the central plateau successfully reclaimed power from the [Seleucids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_dynasty "Seleucid dynasty") and established the [Parthian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire"), which existed for nearly half a millennium before being succeeded by the Iranian [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire").

In the modern era, the Achaemenid Empire has been recognized for its centralized [bureaucracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy "Bureaucracy") and administration; its multicultural policy and religious tolerance, [especially under Cyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_tolerance#In_Antiquity "Religious tolerance"); its complex infrastructure projects, such as the [Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road "Royal Road") and an [organized postal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapar_Khaneh "Chapar Khaneh"); the use of official languages ([Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian "Old Persian") and [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Aramaic "Imperial Aramaic")) across its territories; and the development of a civil service and a large, professional [army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Persian_army "Achaemenid Persian army") and [navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_navy "Achaemenid navy"). Many of these systems were adopted and expanded upon by later empires in the [Greco-Roman world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world "Greco-Roman world") and beyond.

## Etymology

The Achaemenid Empire is named after [Achaemenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenes "Achaemenes"), who is credited as the progenitor of the Achaemenid dynasty. The term *Achaemenid* means "of the family of the Achaemenis/Achaemenes" ([Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian_language "Old Persian language"): [𐏃𐎧𐎠𐎶𐎴𐎡𐏁](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%90%8F%83%F0%90%8E%A7%F0%90%8E%A0%F0%90%8E%B6%F0%90%8E%B4%F0%90%8E%A1%F0%90%8F%81 "wikt:𐏃𐎧𐎠𐎶𐎴𐎡𐏁"), romanized: *Haxāmaniš*; a [bahuvrihi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahuvrihi "Bahuvrihi") compound translating to "having a friend's mind"). Achaemenes was himself a minor seventh-century ruler of the [Anshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anshan_(Persia) "Anshan (Persia)") in southwestern Iran, and a vassal of [Assyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria").

Around 850 BC the original [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad") people who began the empire called themselves the *Parsa* and their constantly shifting territory *Parsua*, for the most part localized around Persis. The name "Persia" is a Greek and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") pronunciation of the native word referring to the country of the people originating from [Persis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persis "Persis") ([Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian_language "Old Persian language"): [𐎱𐎠𐎼𐎿](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%90%8E%B1%F0%90%8E%A0%F0%90%8E%BC%F0%90%8E%BF "wikt:𐎱𐎠𐎼𐎿"), romanized: *Pārsa*). The [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian_language "Old Persian language") term 𐎧𐏁𐏂 *Xšāça*, literally meaning "The Kingdom", was used to refer to the Empire formed by their multinational state.

### Origin of the Achaemenid dynasty

> The Persian nation contains a number of tribes as listed here. ... : the [Pasargadae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasargadae_(tribe) "Pasargadae (tribe)"), [Maraphii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maraphii "Maraphii"), and [Maspii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maspii "Maspii"), upon which all the other tribes are dependent. Of these, the Pasargadae are the most distinguished; they contain the clan of the Achaemenids from which spring the Perseid kings. Other tribes are the Panthialaei, Derusiaei, [Germanii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerman_Province#History_and_culture "Kerman Province"), all of which are attached to the soil, the remainder—the [Dai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahae "Dahae"), [Mardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardi_(people) "Mardi (people)"), [Dropici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropici "Dropici"), [Sagarti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagarti "Sagarti"), being [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad").

— [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus"), [Histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_(Herodotus) "Histories (Herodotus)") 1.101 & 125

The Achaemenid Empire was founded by nomadic [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people "Persian people"). The Persians were an [Iranic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranic_peoples "Iranic peoples") who arrived in what is today [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran") c. 1000 BC and settled a region including north-western Iran, the [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains "Zagros Mountains") and [Persis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persis "Persis") alongside the native [Elamites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam "Elam"). The Persians were originally [nomadic pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_pastoralism "Nomadic pastoralism") in the western Iranian Plateau. The Achaemenid Empire may not have been the first Iranian empire, as the [Medes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medes "Medes"), another group of Iranian people, possibly established a short-lived empire when they played a major role in overthrowing the [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians "Assyrians").

The Achaemenids were initially rulers of the Elamite city of [Anshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anshan_(Persia) "Anshan (Persia)") near the modern city of [Marvdasht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvdasht "Marvdasht"); the title "King of Anshan" was an adaptation of the earlier [Elamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite_civilisation "Elamite civilisation") title "King of Susa and Anshan". There are conflicting accounts of the identities of the earliest Kings of Anshan. According to the [Cyrus Cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Cylinder "Cyrus Cylinder") (the oldest extant genealogy of the Achaemenids) the kings of Anshan were [Teispes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teispes "Teispes"), [Cyrus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_I "Cyrus I"), [Cambyses I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambyses_I "Cambyses I") and [Cyrus II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great"), also known as Cyrus the Great, who founded the empire.

The later [Behistun Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behistun_Inscription "Behistun Inscription"), written by [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great"), claims that Teispes was the son of [Achaemenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenes "Achaemenes") and that Darius is also descended from Teispes through a different line, but no earlier texts mention Achaemenes. In [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus")' [Histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_(Herodotus) "Histories (Herodotus)"), he writes that Cyrus the Great was the son of Cambyses I and [Mandane of Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandane_of_Media "Mandane of Media"), the daughter of [Astyages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astyages "Astyages"), the king of the Median Empire.

### Formation and expansion

#### 550s BC

Cyrus revolted against the [Median Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_kingdom "Median kingdom") in 553 BC, and in 550 BC succeeded in defeating the Medes, capturing Astyages and taking the Median capital city of [Ecbatana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecbatana "Ecbatana"). Once in control of Ecbatana, Cyrus styled himself as the successor to Astyages and assumed control of the entire empire. By inheriting Astyages' empire, he also inherited the territorial conflicts the Medes had with both [Lydia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia "Lydia") and the [Neo-Babylonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire "Neo-Babylonian Empire").

#### 540s BC

King [Croesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croesus "Croesus") of Lydia sought to take advantage of the new international situation by advancing into what had previously been Median territory in Asia Minor. Cyrus led a counterattack which not only fought off Croesus' armies, but also led to the capture of [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis") and the fall of the Lydian Kingdom in 546 BC. Cyrus placed [Pactyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pactyes "Pactyes") in charge of collecting tribute in Lydia and left, but once Cyrus had left Pactyes instigated a rebellion against Cyrus. Cyrus sent the Median general [Mazares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazares "Mazares") to deal with the rebellion, and Pactyes was captured. Mazares, and after his death [Harpagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpagus "Harpagus"), set about reducing all the cities which had taken part in the rebellion. The subjugation of Lydia took about four years in total.

When the power in Ecbatana changed hands from the Medes to the Persians, many tributaries to the Median Empire believed their situation had changed and revolted against Cyrus. This forced Cyrus to fight wars against [Bactria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria "Bactria") and the nomadic [Saka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka "Saka") in Central Asia. During these wars, Cyrus established several [garrison towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrison_town "Garrison town") in Central Asia, including the [Cyropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyropolis "Cyropolis").

#### 530s BC

Nothing is known of Persia–Babylon relations between 547 and 539 BC, but it is likely that there were hostilities between the two empires for several years leading up to the war of 540–539 BC and the [Fall of Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Babylon "Fall of Babylon"). In October 539 BC, Cyrus won a battle against the Babylonians at [Opis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opis "Opis"), then took [Sippar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sippar "Sippar") without a fight before finally capturing the city of [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon") on 12 October, where the Babylonian king [Nabonidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabonidus "Nabonidus") was taken prisoner.

Upon taking control of the city, Cyrus depicted himself in propaganda as restoring the divine order which had been disrupted by [Nabonidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabonidus "Nabonidus"), who had promoted the cult of [Sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin_(mythology) "Sin (mythology)") rather than [Marduk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marduk "Marduk"), and he also portrayed himself as restoring the heritage of the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire "Neo-Assyrian Empire") by comparing himself to the Assyrian king [Ashurbanipal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashurbanipal "Ashurbanipal"). Cyrus is credited with freeing the [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") from the [Babylonian captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity "Babylonian captivity"), permitting their return to [Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yehud_(Persian_province) "Yehud (Persian province)"), and authorizing the rebuilding of [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem"), including the [Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple "Second Temple").

#### 520s BC

In 530 BC, Cyrus died and was succeeded by his eldest son [Cambyses II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambyses_II "Cambyses II"), while his younger son [Bardiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardiya "Bardiya") received a large territory in Central Asia. By 525 BC, Cambyses had successfully subjugated [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") and [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") and was making preparations to invade Egypt with the newly created [Persian navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_navy "Achaemenid navy"). Pharaoh [Amasis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amasis_II "Amasis II") had died in 526, and had been succeeded by [Psamtik III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psamtik_III "Psamtik III"), resulting in the defection of key Egyptian allies to the Persians. Psamtik positioned his army at [Pelusium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelusium "Pelusium") in the [Nile Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_Delta "Nile Delta"). He was soundly defeated by the Persians in the [Battle of Pelusium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Pelusium "Battle of Pelusium") before fleeing to [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt "Memphis, Egypt"), where the Persians defeated him and took him prisoner. After attempting a failed revolt, Psamtik III promptly committed suicide.

Herodotus depicts Cambyses as openly antagonistic to the Egyptian people and their gods, cults, temples, and priests, in particular stressing the murder of the sacred bull [Apis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apis_(deity) "Apis (deity)"). He says that these actions led to a madness that caused him to kill his brother Bardiya (who Herodotus says was killed in secret), his own sister-wife and Croesus of Lydia. He then concludes that Cambyses completely lost his mind, and all later classical authors repeat the themes of Cambyses' impiety and madness. However, this is based on spurious information, as the epitaph of Apis from 524 BC shows that Cambyses participated in the funeral rites of Apis styling himself as pharaoh.

Following the conquest of Egypt, the [Libyans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Libya "Ancient Libya") and the Greeks of [Cyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrene,_Libya "Cyrene, Libya") and [Barca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barca_(ancient_city) "Barca (ancient city)") in present-day eastern Libya ([Cyrenaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica "Cyrenaica")) surrendered to Cambyses and sent tribute without a fight. Cambyses then planned invasions of [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage "Carthage"), the oasis of Ammon and [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aethiopia "Aethiopia"). Herodotus claims that the naval invasion of Carthage was canceled because the Phoenicians, who made up a large part of Cambyses' fleet, refused to take up arms against their own people, but modern historians doubt whether an invasion of Carthage was ever planned at all. However, Cambyses dedicated his efforts to the other two campaigns, aiming to improve the Empire's strategic position in Africa by conquering the [Kingdom of Meroë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush") and taking strategic positions in the western oases. To this end, he established a garrison at [Elephantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephantine "Elephantine") consisting mainly of Jewish soldiers, who remained stationed at Elephantine throughout Cambyses' reign. Herodotus claims that the invasion of Ethiopia was a failure due to the madness of Cambyses and the lack of supplies for his men, but archaeological evidence suggests that the expedition was not a failure, and a fortress at the [Second Cataract of the Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataracts_of_the_Nile "Cataracts of the Nile"), on the border between Egypt and Kush, remained in use throughout the Achaemenid period. Furthermore, in two inscriptions of Darius, Nubia is listed as one of the lands in the empire, and a Nubian delegation is depicted in Persepolis on the [Apadana reliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apadana "Apadana") which visually present the lands of Darius' empire. These suggest that Cambyses saw success in subduing Nubia.

The events surrounding Cambyses's death and Bardiya's succession are greatly debated as there are many conflicting accounts. According to Herodotus, as Bardiya's assassination had been committed in secret, the majority of Persians still believed him to be alive. This allowed two [Magi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magi "Magi") to rise up against Cambyses, with one of them sitting on the throne able to impersonate Bardiya because of their remarkable physical resemblance and shared name (Smerdis in Herodotus's accounts). [Ctesias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesias "Ctesias") writes that when Cambyses had Bardiya killed he immediately put the magus Sphendadates in his place as satrap of Bactria due to a remarkable physical resemblance. Two of Cambyses' confidants then conspired to usurp Cambyses and put Sphendadates on the throne under the guise of Bardiya. According to the [Behistun Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behistun_Inscription "Behistun Inscription"), written by the following king [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great"), a magus named Gaumata impersonated Bardiya and incited a revolution in Persia. Whatever the exact circumstances of the revolt, Cambyses heard news of it in the summer of 522 BC and began to return from Egypt, but he was wounded in the thigh in Syria and died of gangrene, so Bardiya's impersonator became king. Darius provides the earliest account; later historians concur that a magus impersonated Bardiya and seized the throne, but this may be a fabrication by Darius to legitimize his own usurpation. Iranologist [Pierre Briant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Briant "Pierre Briant") hypothesises that Bardiya was not killed by Cambyses, but waited until his death in the summer of 522 BC to claim his legitimate right to the throne as he was then the only male descendant of the royal family. Briant says that although the hypothesis of a deception by Darius is generally accepted today, "nothing has been established with certainty at the present time, given the available evidence".

According to the [Behistun inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behistun_inscription "Behistun inscription"), Gaumata ruled for seven months before being overthrown in 522 BC by [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great") (Old Persian *Dāryavuš*, "who holds firm the good", also known as *Darayarahush*). The Magi, though persecuted, continued to exist, and a year following the death of the first pseudo-Smerdis (*Gaumata*), saw a second pseudo-Smerdis (*Vahyazdāta*) attempt a coup. The coup, though initially successful, failed.

[Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") writes that the native leadership debated the best form of government for the empire.

#### 510s BC

The [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians "Ancient Macedonians") king [Amyntas I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyntas_I "Amyntas I") surrendered his country to the Persians in about 512–511. In 513, [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great") (521–486) initiated Persian military operations that included the subjugation of [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom) "Macedonia (ancient kingdom)"), as a large Achaemenid army invaded the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") and [tried to defeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Scythian_campaign_of_Darius_I "European Scythian campaign of Darius I") the European [Scythians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians "Scythians") north of the [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube "Danube"). Darius' army subjugated several [Thracian peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thracians "Thracians") and virtually all other regions that touch the European part of the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea"), such as parts of modern [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria "Bulgaria"), [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania "Romania"), [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine"), and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia"), before it returned to [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"). Darius left in Europe one of his commanders named [Megabazus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabazus "Megabazus") whose task was to accomplish conquests in the Balkans. The Persian troops subjugated gold-rich [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace"), the coastal Greek cities, and defeated and conquered the powerful [Paeonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paeonians "Paeonians"). Finally, Megabazus sent envoys to Amyntas, demanding acceptance of Persian domination, which the Macedonians did. The Balkans provided many soldiers for the multi-ethnic Achaemenid army. Many of the Macedonian and Persian elite intermarried, such as the Persian official [Bubares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubares "Bubares") who married Amyntas' daughter, Gygaea. Family ties that the Macedonian rulers Amyntas and Alexander enjoyed with Bubares ensured them good relations with the Persian kings Darius and [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_the_Great "Xerxes the Great"), who was also known as Xerxes the Great. The Persian invasion indirectly advanced Macedonia's power, as both shared Balkan interests; Persian aid let Macedonians gain at the expense of tribes like the Paeonians and Greeks. All in all, the Macedonians were "willing and useful Persian allies." Macedonian soldiers fought against Athens and [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta "Sparta") in Xerxes I's army. The Persians referred to both Greeks and Macedonians as *[Yauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yona "Yona")* ("[Ionians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Greeks "Ionian Greeks")", their term for "Greeks"), and to Macedonians specifically as *Yaunã Takabara* or "Greeks with hats that look like shields", possibly referring to the Macedonian [kausia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kausia "Kausia") hat.

#### 5th century BC

By the 5th century BC, the Kings of Persia were either ruling over or had subordinated territories encompassing not just all of the [Persian Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Plateau "Persian Plateau") and all of the territories formerly held by the [Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire "Neo-Assyrian Empire") ([Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"), [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Egypt "Persian Egypt")), but beyond this, all of [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") and [Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenia "Armenia"), as well as the [Southern Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Caucasus "Southern Caucasus") and parts of the [North Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Caucasus "North Caucasus"), [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan "Azerbaijan"), [Uzbekistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzbekistan "Uzbekistan"), [Tajikistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tajikistan "Tajikistan"), [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria "Bulgaria"), [Paeonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paeonia_(kingdom) "Paeonia (kingdom)"), [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace") and [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom) "Macedonia (ancient kingdom)") to the north and west, most of the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea") coastal regions, parts of [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia") as far as the [Aral Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aral_Sea "Aral Sea"), the [Oxus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amu_Darya "Amu Darya") and [Jaxartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syr_Darya "Syr Darya") to the north and north-east, the [Hindu Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush "Hindu Kush") and the western [Indus basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River "Indus River") (corresponding to modern Afghanistan and [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan")) to the far east, parts of northern [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia "Arabia") to the south, and parts of eastern [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Libya "Ancient Libya") ([Cyrenaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica "Cyrenaica")) to the south-west, and parts of [Oman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oman "Oman"), China, and the [UAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAE "UAE").

### Greco-Persian Wars

The [Ionian Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Revolt "Ionian Revolt") in 499 BC, and associated revolts in Aeolis, Doris, Cyprus, and Caria, were military rebellions by several regions of Asia Minor against Persian rule, lasting from 499 to 493 BC. At the heart of the rebellion was the dissatisfaction of the Greek cities of Asia Minor with the tyrants appointed by Persia to rule them, along with the individual actions of two Milesian tyrants, [Histiaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histiaeus "Histiaeus") and [Aristagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristagoras "Aristagoras"). In 499 BC, the then-tyrant of [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus "Miletus"), Aristagoras, launched a joint expedition with the Persian satrap [Artaphernes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaphernes "Artaphernes") to [conquer Naxos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Naxos_(499_BC) "Siege of Naxos (499 BC)"), in an attempt to bolster his position in Miletus, both financially and in terms of prestige. The mission was a debacle, and sensing his imminent removal as a tyrant, Aristagoras chose to incite the whole of Ionia into rebellion against the Persian king, Darius I, who was commonly known as Darius the Great.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The Persians continued to reduce the cities along the west coast that still held out against them, before finally imposing a peace settlement in 493 BC on Ionia that was generally considered to be both just and fair. The [Ionian Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Revolt "Ionian Revolt") constituted the first major conflict between Greece and the Achaemenid Empire, and as such represents the first phase of the Greco-Persian Wars. Asia Minor had been brought back into the Persian fold, but Darius had vowed to punish Athens and Eretria for their support of the revolt. Moreover, seeing that the political situation in Greece posed a continued threat to the stability of his Empire, he decided to embark on the conquest of all of Greece. The first campaign of the invasion was to bring the territories in the [Balkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan "Balkan") peninsula back within the empire. The Persian grip over these territories had loosened following the Ionian Revolt. In 492 BC, the Persian general [Mardonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardonius_(general) "Mardonius (general)") re-subjugated [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace") and made [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom) "Macedonia (ancient kingdom)") a fully [subordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subordinate "Subordinate") part of the empire; it had been a vassal as early as the late 6th century BC but retained a great deal of autonomy. However, in 490 BC the Persian forces were defeated by the Athenians at the [Battle of Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marathon "Battle of Marathon") and Darius I would die before having the chance to launch an invasion of Greece.

[Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I") (485–465 BC, Old Persian *Xšayārša* "Hero Among Kings"), son of [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great"), vowed to complete the job. He organized a massive invasion aiming to conquer [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece "Greece"). His army entered Greece from the north in the spring of 480 BC, meeting little or no resistance through [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(Greece) "Macedonia (Greece)") and [Thessaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaly "Thessaly"), but was delayed by a small Greek force for three days at [Thermopylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae "Battle of Thermopylae"). A simultaneous naval [battle of Artemisium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Artemisium "Battle of Artemisium") was tactically indecisive as large storms destroyed ships from both sides. The battle was stopped prematurely when the Greeks received news of the defeat at Thermopylae and retreated. The battle was a tactical victory for the Persians, giving them uncontested control of Artemisium and the Aegean Sea.

Following his victory at the [Battle of Thermopylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae "Battle of Thermopylae"), Xerxes sacked the evacuated city of [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens") and prepared to meet the Greeks at the strategic [Isthmus of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmus_of_Corinth "Isthmus of Corinth") and the [Saronic Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saronic_Gulf "Saronic Gulf"). In 480 BC the Greeks won a decisive victory over the Persian fleet at the [Battle of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis "Battle of Salamis") and forced Xerxes to retire to [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis"). The land army which he left in Greece under [Mardonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardonius_(general) "Mardonius (general)") retook Athens but was eventually destroyed in 479 BC at the [Battle of Plataea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Plataea "Battle of Plataea"). The final defeat of the Persians at [Mycale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mycale "Battle of Mycale") encouraged the Greek cities of Asia to revolt, and the Persians lost all of their territories in Europe with Macedonia once again becoming independent. Xerxes was assassinated in his bed, though ancient accounts conflict significantly. The assassination is attributed by Greek and Roman sources to Artabanus, though a Babylonian tablet attributes it to Xerxes' son.

### Cultural phase and Zoroastrian reforms

After [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I") was assassinated, he was succeeded by his eldest surviving son [Artaxerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_I "Artaxerxes I"). It was during his reign that [Elamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite "Elamite") ceased to be the language of government, and Aramaic gained in importance. It was probably during this reign that the solar calendar was introduced as the national calendar. Under Artaxerxes I, [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism") became the *de facto* religion of the empire.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

After Persia had been defeated at the [Battle of Eurymedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Eurymedon "Battle of Eurymedon") (469 or 466 BC), military action between Greece and Persia was halted. When Artaxerxes I took power, he introduced a new Persian strategy of weakening the Athenians by funding their enemies in Greece. This indirectly caused the Athenians to move the treasury of the [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League "Delian League") from the island of [Delos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delos "Delos") to the Athenian acropolis. This funding practice inevitably prompted renewed fighting in 450 BC, where the Greeks attacked at the [Battle of Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis_in_Cyprus_(450_BC) "Battle of Salamis in Cyprus (450 BC)"). After [Cimon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimon "Cimon")'s failure to attain much in this expedition, the [Peace of Callias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Callias "Peace of Callias") was agreed between [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"), [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argos,_Peloponnese "Argos, Peloponnese") and [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia "Persia") in 449 BC.

Artaxerxes offered [asylum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_asylum "Political asylum") to [Themistocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themistocles "Themistocles"), who was the winner of the [Battle of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis "Battle of Salamis"), after Themistocles was [ostracized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostracized "Ostracized") from [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"). Also, Artaxerxes gave him [Magnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesia_on_the_Maeander "Magnesia on the Maeander"), [Myus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myus "Myus"), and [Lampsacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampsacus "Lampsacus") to maintain him in bread, meat, and wine. In addition, Artaxerxes I gave him [Palaescepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaescepsis "Palaescepsis") to provide him with clothes, and he also gave him [Percote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percote "Percote") with bedding for his house.

When Artaxerxes died in 424 BC at [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa"), his body was taken to the tomb already built for him in the [Naqsh-e Rustam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naqsh-e_Rustam "Naqsh-e Rustam") Necropolis. It was Persian tradition that kings begin constructing their own tombs while they were still alive. Artaxerxes I was immediately succeeded by his eldest and only legitimate son, [Xerxes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_II "Xerxes II"). However, after a few days on the throne, he was assassinated while drunk by Pharnacyas and Menostanes on the orders of his illegitimate brother [Sogdianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdianus "Sogdianus"), who apparently had gained the support of his regions. Sogdianus reigned for six months and fifteen days before being captured by his half-brother, [Ochus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_II "Darius II"), who had rebelled against him. Sogdianus was executed by being [suffocated in ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffocation_in_ash "Suffocation in ash") because Ochus had promised he would not die by the sword, by poison or by hunger. Ochus then took the royal name Darius II. Darius' ability to defend his position on the throne ended the short power vacuum.

From 412 BC [Darius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_II_of_Persia "Darius II of Persia"), at the insistence of [Tissaphernes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tissaphernes "Tissaphernes"), gave support first to Athens, then to Sparta, but in 407 BC, Darius' son [Cyrus the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Younger "Cyrus the Younger") was appointed to replace Tissaphernes and aid was given entirely to Sparta which finally defeated Athens in 404 BC. In the same year, Darius fell ill and died in Babylon. His death gave an Egyptian rebel named [Amyrtaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyrtaeus "Amyrtaeus") the opportunity to throw off [Persian control over Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-seventh_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Twenty-seventh Dynasty of Egypt"). At his death bed, Darius' Babylonian wife [Parysatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parysatis "Parysatis") pleaded with him to have her second eldest son Cyrus (the Younger) crowned, but Darius refused. Queen Parysatis favoured Cyrus more than her eldest son [Artaxerxes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_II "Artaxerxes II"). [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch "Plutarch") relates (probably on the authority of [Ctesias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesias "Ctesias")) that the displaced Tissaphernes came to the new king on his coronation day to warn him that his younger brother Cyrus (the Younger) was preparing to assassinate him during the ceremony. Artaxerxes had Cyrus arrested and would have had him executed if their mother Parysatis had not intervened. Cyrus was then sent back as Satrap of Lydia, where he prepared an armed rebellion. Cyrus assembled a large army, including a contingent of [Ten Thousand Greek mercenaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Thousand_Greek_mercenaries "Ten Thousand Greek mercenaries"), and made his way deeper into Persia. The army of Cyrus was stopped by the royal Persian army of Artaxerxes II at [Cunaxa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cunaxa "Battle of Cunaxa") in 401 BC, where Cyrus was killed. The [Ten Thousand Greek Mercenaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Thousand_Greek_Mercenaries "Ten Thousand Greek Mercenaries") including [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon "Xenophon") were now deep in Persian territory and were at risk of attack. So they searched for others to offer their services to but eventually had to return to Greece.

[Artaxerxes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_II "Artaxerxes II") was the longest reigning of the Achaemenid kings and it was during this 45-year period of relative peace and stability that many of the monuments of the era were constructed. Artaxerxes moved the capital back to [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis"), which he greatly extended. Also, the summer capital at [Ecbatana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecbatana "Ecbatana") was lavishly extended with gilded columns and roof tiles of silver and copper. The extraordinary innovation of the Zoroastrian shrines can also be dated to his reign, and it was probably during this period that Zoroastrianism spread from [Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenia "Armenia") throughout [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor") and the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"). The construction of temples, though serving a religious purpose, was not a purely selfless act, as they also served as an important source of income. From the Babylonian kings, the Achaemenids adopted the concept of a mandatory temple tax, a one-tenth tithe which all inhabitants paid to the temple nearest to their land or another source of income.

Artaxerxes II became involved in a war with Persia's erstwhile allies, the [Spartans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta "Sparta"), who, under [Agesilaus II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agesilaus_II "Agesilaus II"), invaded [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"). To redirect the Spartans' attention to Greek affairs, Artaxerxes II subsidized their enemies: in particular the [Athenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"), [Thebans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece "Thebes, Greece") and [Corinthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth "Ancient Corinth"). These subsidies helped to engage the Spartans in what would become known as the [Corinthian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_War "Corinthian War"). In 387 BC, Artaxerxes II betrayed his allies and came to an arrangement with Sparta, and in the [Treaty of Antalcidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Antalcidas "Treaty of Antalcidas") he forced his erstwhile allies to come to terms. This treaty restored control of the Greek cities of [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia "Ionia") and [Aeolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolis "Aeolis") on the Anatolian coast to the Persians while giving Sparta dominance on the Greek mainland. In 385 BC he [campaigned against the Cadusians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes%27_II_Cadusian_Campaign "Artaxerxes' II Cadusian Campaign"). Although successful against the Greeks, Artaxerxes II had more trouble with the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt"), who had successfully revolted against him at the beginning of his reign. An attempt to reconquer Egypt in 373 BC was completely unsuccessful, but in his waning years the Persians did manage to defeat a joint Egyptian–Spartan effort to conquer [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Phoenicia "Achaemenid Phoenicia"). He quashed the [Revolt of the Satraps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolt_of_the_Satraps "Revolt of the Satraps") in 372–362 BC. He is reported to have had a number of wives. His main wife was [Stateira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stateira_(wife_of_Artaxerxes_II) "Stateira (wife of Artaxerxes II)"), until she was poisoned by Artaxerxes II's mother Parysatis in about 400 BC. Another chief wife was a Greek woman of [Phocaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phocaea "Phocaea") named Aspasia (not the same as the concubine of [Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles "Pericles")). Artaxerxes II is said to have had more than 115 sons from 350 wives.

In 358 BC Artaxerxes II died and was succeeded by his son [Artaxerxes III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_III "Artaxerxes III"). In 355 BC, Artaxerxes III forced [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens") to conclude a peace which required the city's forces to leave [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor") and to acknowledge the independence of its rebellious allies. Artaxerxes started a campaign against the rebellious [Cadusians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadusii "Cadusii"), but he managed to appease both of the Cadusian kings. One individual who successfully emerged from this campaign was Darius Codomannus, who later occupied the Persian throne as [Darius III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_III "Darius III").

Artaxerxes III then ordered the disbanding of all the satrapal armies of Asia Minor, as he felt that they could no longer guarantee peace in the west and was concerned that these armies equipped the western satraps with the means to revolt. The order was however ignored by [Artabazos II of Phrygia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artabazos_II_of_Phrygia "Artabazos II of Phrygia"), who asked for the help of Athens in a rebellion against the king. Athens sent assistance to [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis"). [Orontes of Mysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orontid_dynasty#Orontid_Kings_and_satraps_of_Armenia "Orontid dynasty") also supported Artabazos and the combined forces managed to defeat the forces sent by Artaxerxes III in 354 BC. However, in 353 BC, they were defeated by Artaxerxes III's army and were disbanded. Orontes was pardoned by the king, while Artabazos fled to the safety of the court of [Philip II of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon"). In c. 351 BC, Artaxerxes embarked on a campaign to recover Egypt, which had revolted under his father, Artaxerxes II. At the same time, a rebellion had broken out in Asia Minor, which, being supported by [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece "Thebes, Greece"), threatened to become serious. Levying a vast army, Artaxerxes [invaded Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Achaemenid_conquest_of_Egypt "Second Achaemenid conquest of Egypt") and engaged in fighting with [Nectanebo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectanebo_II "Nectanebo II"). After a year of fighting the Egyptian [Pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaoh "Pharaoh"), Nectanebo inflicted a crushing defeat on the Persians with the support of mercenaries led by the Greek generals Diophantus and Lamius. Artaxerxes was compelled to retreat and postpone his plans to reconquer Egypt. Soon after this defeat, there were rebellions in [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Phoenicia "Achaemenid Phoenicia"), [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Anatolia#Achaemenid_Empire "History of Anatolia") and [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history_of_Cyprus#Persian_period "Ancient history of Cyprus").

In 343 BC, Artaxerxes committed responsibility for the suppression of the Cyprian rebels to [Idrieus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idrieus "Idrieus"), prince of [Caria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caria "Caria"), who employed 8,000 Greek mercenaries and forty [triremes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme "Trireme"), commanded by [Phocion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phocion "Phocion") the Athenian, and [Evagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evagoras_II "Evagoras II"), son of the elder [Evagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evagoras_I "Evagoras I"), the Cypriot monarch. Idrieus succeeded in reducing Cyprus. Artaxerxes initiated a counter-offensive against [Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidon "Sidon") by commanding [Belesys II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belesys_II "Belesys II"), satrap of Syria, and [Mazaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazaeus "Mazaeus"), [satrap of Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia_(satrapy) "Cilicia (satrapy)"), to invade the city and to keep the [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") in check. Both satraps suffered crushing defeats at the hands of [Tennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennes "Tennes"), the Sidonese king, who was aided by 40,000 Greek mercenaries sent to him by [Nectanebo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectanebo_II "Nectanebo II") and commanded by [Mentor of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentor_of_Rhodes "Mentor of Rhodes"). As a result, the Persian forces were driven out of Phoenicia.

After this, Artaxerxes personally led an army of 330,000 men against [Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidon "Sidon"). Artaxerxes' army comprised 300,000-foot soldiers, 30,000 [cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalry "Cavalry"), 300 triremes, and 500 transports or provision ships. After gathering this army, he sought assistance from the Greeks. Though refused aid by [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens") and [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta "Sparta"), he succeeded in obtaining a thousand Theban heavy-armed hoplites under Lacrates, three thousand Argives under Nicostratus, and six thousand Æolians, [Ionians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionians "Ionians"), and Dorians from the Greek cities of Asia Minor. This Greek support was numerically small, amounting to no more than 10,000 men, but it formed, together with the Greek mercenaries from Egypt who went over to him afterward, the force on which he placed his chief reliance, and to which the ultimate success of his expedition was mainly due. The approach of Artaxerxes sufficiently weakened the resolution of Tennes that he endeavoured to purchase his own pardon by delivering up 100 principal citizens of Sidon into the hands of the Persian king and then admitting Artaxerxes within the defences of the town. Artaxerxes had the 100 citizens transfixed with javelins, and when 500 more came out as supplicants to seek his mercy, Artaxerxes consigned them to the same fate. Sidon was then burnt to the ground, either by Artaxerxes or by the Sidonian citizens. Forty thousand people died in the conflagration. Artaxerxes sold the ruins at a high price to speculators, who calculated on reimbursing themselves by the treasures which they hoped to dig out from among the ashes. Tennes was later put to death by Artaxerxes. Artaxerxes later sent Jews who supported the revolt to [Hyrcania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyrcania "Hyrcania") on the south coast of the [Caspian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspian_Sea "Caspian Sea").

### Second conquest of Egypt

The reduction of Sidon was followed closely by the invasion of Egypt. In 343 BC, Artaxerxes III, in addition to his 330,000 Persians, had now a force of 14,000 Greeks furnished by the Greek cities of Asia Minor: 4,000 under [Mentor of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentor_of_Rhodes "Mentor of Rhodes"), consisting of the troops that he had brought to the aid of Tennes from Egypt; 3,000 sent by Argos; and 1,000 from Thebes. He divided these troops into three bodies, and placed at the head of each a Persian and a Greek. The Greek commanders were Lacrates of Thebes, Mentor of Rhodes and Nicostratus of Argos while the Persians were led by Rhossaces, Aristazanes, and [Bagoas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagoas "Bagoas"), the chief of the eunuchs. [Nectanebo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectanebo_II "Nectanebo II") resisted with an army of 100,000 of whom 20,000 were Greek mercenaries. Nectanebo II occupied the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile") and its various branches with his navy.

The character of the country, intersected by numerous canals and full of strongly fortified towns, was in his favour and Nectanebo II might have been expected to offer a prolonged, if not even a successful resistance. However, he lacked good generals, and, over-confident in his own powers of command, he was out-maneuvered by the Greek mercenary generals, and his forces were eventually defeated by the combined Persian armies. After his defeat, Nectanebo hastily fled to [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt "Memphis, Egypt"), leaving the fortified towns to be defended by their garrisons. These garrisons consisted of partly [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks "Greeks") and partly Egyptian troops; between whom jealousies and suspicions were easily sown by the Persian leaders. As a result, the Persians were able to rapidly reduce numerous towns across Lower Egypt and were advancing upon Memphis when Nectanebo decided to quit the country and flee southwards to [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia"). The Persian army completely routed the Egyptians and occupied the Lower Delta of the Nile. Following Nectanebo fleeing to Ethiopia, all of Egypt submitted to Artaxerxes. The Jews in Egypt were sent either to [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon") or to Hyrcania, the region on the south coast of the [Caspian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspian_Sea "Caspian Sea").

After this victory over the Egyptians, Artaxerxes had the city walls destroyed, started a reign of terror, and set about looting all the temples. [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia "Persia") gained a significant amount of wealth from this looting. Artaxerxes also raised high taxes and attempted to weaken [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") enough that it could never revolt against Persia. For the 10 years that Persia controlled Egypt, believers in the native religion were persecuted and sacred books were stolen. Before Artaxerxes returned to Persia, he appointed Pherendares as [satrap of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Achaemenid_Egypt "History of Achaemenid Egypt"). With the wealth gained from his reconquering Egypt, Artaxerxes was able to amply reward his mercenaries. He then returned to his capital having successfully completed his invasion of Egypt.

After his success in Egypt, Artaxerxes returned to Persia and spent the next few years quelling insurrections in various parts of the Empire, after which it was firmly under his control. Egypt remained a part of the Persian Empire from then until [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great")'s conquest of Egypt.

After the conquest of Egypt, there were no more revolts or rebellions against Artaxerxes. Mentor and [Bagoas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagoas "Bagoas"), the two generals who had most distinguished themselves in the Egyptian campaign, were advanced to posts of the highest importance. Mentor, who was governor of the entire Asiatic seaboard, was successful in reducing to subjection many of the chiefs who during the recent troubles had rebelled against Persian rule. In the course of a few years, Mentor and his forces were able to bring the whole Asian Mediterranean coast into complete submission and dependence.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Bagoas went back to the Persian capital with Artaxerxes, where he took a leading role in the internal administration of the Empire and maintained tranquillity throughout the rest of the Empire. During the last six years of the reign of Artaxerxes III, the Persian Empire was governed by a vigorous and successful government.

The Persian forces in [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia "Ionia") and [Lycia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycia "Lycia") regained control of the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea "Aegean Sea") and the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea") and took over much of [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens")' former island empire. In response, [Isocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isocrates "Isocrates") of Athens started giving speeches calling for a 'crusade against the barbarians' but there was not enough strength left in any of the Greek city-states to answer his call.

Although there were no rebellions in the Persian Empire itself, the growing power and territory of [Philip II of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon") in [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon "Macedon") (against which [Demosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosthenes "Demosthenes") was in vain warning the Athenians) attracted the attention of Artaxerxes. In response, he ordered that Persian influence was to be used to check and constrain the rising power and influence of the Macedonian kingdom. In 340 BC, a Persian force was dispatched to assist the [Thracian prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace"), [Cersobleptes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cersobleptes "Cersobleptes"), to maintain his independence. Sufficient effective aid was given to the city of [Perinthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perinthus "Perinthus") that the numerous and well-appointed army with which Philip had commenced his siege of the city was compelled to give up the attempt. By the last year of Artaxerxes' rule, Philip II already had plans in place for an invasion of the Persian Empire, which would crown his career, but the Greeks would not unite with him.

In 338 BC Artaxerxes was poisoned by [Bagoas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagoas "Bagoas") with the assistance of a physician.

### Fall to Alexander III of Macedon

Artaxerxes III was succeeded by [Artaxerxes IV Arses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_IV_Arses "Artaxerxes IV Arses"), who before he could act was also poisoned by Bagoas. Bagoas is further said to have killed not only all Arses' children, but many of the other princes of the land. Bagoas then placed [Darius III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_III "Darius III"), a nephew of Artaxerxes IV, on the throne. Darius III, previously the [Satrap of Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orontid_Dynasty "Orontid Dynasty"), personally forced Bagoas to swallow poison. In 334 BC, when Darius was just succeeding in subduing Egypt again, Alexander and his battle-hardened troops [invaded Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Alexander_the_Great "Wars of Alexander the Great").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") (Alexander III of Macedon) defeated the Persian armies at [Granicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Granicus "Battle of Granicus") (334 BC), followed by [Issus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Issus "Battle of Issus") (333 BC), and lastly at [Gaugamela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gaugamela "Battle of Gaugamela") (331 BC). Afterwards, he marched on [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa") and [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis") which surrendered and was destroyed by fire in early 330 BC. From Persepolis, Alexander headed north to [Pasargadae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasargadae "Pasargadae"), where he visited the [tomb of Cyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Cyrus "Tomb of Cyrus"), the man whom he had heard of from the *[Cyropaedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyropaedia "Cyropaedia")*.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In the ensuing chaos created by Alexander's invasion of Persia, Cyrus's tomb was broken into and most of its luxuries were looted. When Alexander reached the tomb, he was horrified by the manner in which it had been treated, and questioned the Magi, putting them on trial. By some accounts, Alexander's decision to put the Magi on trial was more an attempt to undermine their influence and display his own power than a show of concern for Cyrus's tomb. Regardless, Alexander the Great ordered Aristobulus to improve the tomb's condition and restore its interior, showing respect for Cyrus. From there he headed to [Ecbatana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecbatana "Ecbatana"), where Darius III had sought refuge.

Darius III was taken prisoner by [Bessus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessus "Bessus"), his [Bactrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria "Bactria") [satrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrap "Satrap") and kinsman. As Alexander approached, Bessus had his men murder Darius III and then declared himself Darius' successor, as Artaxerxes V, before retreating into Central Asia leaving Darius' body in the road to delay Alexander, who brought it to Persepolis for an honourable funeral. Bessus would then create a coalition of his forces, to create an army to defend against Alexander. Before Bessus could fully unite with his confederates at the eastern part of the empire, Alexander, fearing the danger of Bessus gaining control, found him, put him on trial in a Persian court under his control, and ordered his execution in a "cruel and barbarous manner."

Alexander generally kept the original Achaemenid administrative structure, leading some scholars to dub him as "the last of the Achaemenids". Upon Alexander's death in 323 BC, his empire was divided among his generals, the [Diadochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi "Diadochi"), resulting in a number of smaller states. The largest of these, which held sway over the Iranian plateau, was the [Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire "Seleucid Empire"), ruled by Alexander's general [Seleucus I Nicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I_Nicator "Seleucus I Nicator"). Native Iranian rule would be restored by the [Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire") of northeastern Iran over the course of the 2nd century BC through the Parthian Empire.

### Descendants in later Persian dynasties

"Frataraka" of the Seleucid Empire

Several later Persian rulers, forming the *[Frataraka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frataraka "Frataraka")* dynasty, are known to have acted as representatives of the [Seleucids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucids "Seleucids") in the region of [Fārs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C4%81rs "Fārs"). They ruled from the end of the 3rd century BC to the beginning of the 2nd century BC, and [Vahbarz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vahbarz "Vahbarz") or [Vādfradād I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C4%81dfrad%C4%81d_I "Vādfradād I") obtained independence c. 150 BC, when Seleucid power waned in the areas of southwestern Persia and the Persian Gulf region.

Kings of Persis under the Parthian Empire

During an apparent transitional period, corresponding to the reigns of Vādfradād II and another uncertain king, no titles of authority appeared on the reverse of their coins. The earlier title *prtrk' zy alhaya* (Frataraka) had disappeared. Under [Dārēv I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%81r%C4%93v_I "Dārēv I") (Darios I) however, the new title of *mlk*, or king, appeared, sometimes with the mention of *prs* (Persis), suggesting that the kings of Persis had become independent rulers.

When the [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire") [Arsacid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsacid "Arsacid") king [Mithridates I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithridates_I_of_Parthia "Mithridates I of Parthia") (c. 171–138 BC) took control of [Persis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persis "Persis"), he left the Persian dynasts in office, known as the [Kings of Persis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_of_Persis "Kings of Persis"), and they were allowed to continue minting coins with the title of *mlk* ("King").

Sasanian Empire

With the reign of Šābuhr, the son of [Pāpag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81pag "Pāpag"), the kingdom of Persis then became a part of the [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire"). Šābuhr's brother and successor, Ardaxšir (Artaxerxes) V, defeated the last legitimate Parthian king, [Artabanos V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artabanus_V_of_Parthia "Artabanus V of Parthia") in 224 AD, and was crowned at [Ctesiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesiphon "Ctesiphon") as [Ardaxšir I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardax%C5%A1ir_I "Ardaxšir I") (Ardashir I), *šāhanšāh ī Ērān*, becoming the first king of the new Sasanian Empire.

Kingdom of Pontus

The Achaemenid line would also be carried on through the [Kingdom of Pontus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Pontus "Kingdom of Pontus"), based in the [Pontus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontus_(region) "Pontus (region)") region of northern [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"). This Pontic Kingdom, a state of [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people "Persian people") origin, may even have been directly related to [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I "Darius I") and the [Achaemenid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_dynasty "Achaemenid dynasty"). It was founded by [Mithridates I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithridates_I_of_Pontus "Mithridates I of Pontus") in 281 BC and lasted until its conquest by the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic") in 63 BC. The kingdom grew to its largest extent under [Mithridates VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithridates_VI "Mithridates VI") the Great, who conquered [Colchis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colchis "Colchis"), [Cappadocia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappadocia "Cappadocia"), [Bithynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bithynia "Bithynia"), the Greek colonies of the [Tauric Chersonesos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chersonesus_Taurica "Chersonesus Taurica") and for a brief time the Roman province of [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_(Roman_province) "Asia (Roman province)"). Thus, this Persian dynasty managed to survive and prosper in the [Hellenistic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_world "Hellenistic world") while the main Persian Empire had fallen.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Despite Greek influence on the Kingdom of Pontus, Pontics continued to maintain their Achaemenid lineage.

Both the later dynasties of the [Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire") and [Sasanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire") would on occasion claim Achaemenid descent. Recently there has been some corroboration for the Parthian claim to Achaemenid ancestry via the possibility of an inherited disease ([neurofibromatosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurofibromatosis "Neurofibromatosis")) demonstrated by the physical descriptions of rulers and from the evidence of familial disease on ancient coinage.

## Government

[Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great") founded the empire as a multi-[state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(polity) "State (polity)") empire, governed from four capital cities: [Pasargadae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasargadae "Pasargadae"), [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon"), [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa") and [Ecbatana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecbatana "Ecbatana"). The Achaemenids allowed a certain amount of regional autonomy in the form of the [satrapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrap "Satrap") system. A satrapy was an administrative unit, usually organized on a geographical basis. A 'satrap' (governor) was the governor who administered the region, a 'general' supervised military recruitment and ensured order, and a 'state secretary' kept the official records. The general and the state secretary reported directly to the satrap as well as the central government. At differing times, there were between 20 and 30 satrapies.

Cyrus the Great created an organized army including the [Immortals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Immortals "Persian Immortals") unit, consisting of 10,000 highly trained soldiers Cyrus also formed an innovative [postal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postal_system "Postal system") throughout the empire, based on several relay stations called [Chapar Khaneh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapar_Khaneh "Chapar Khaneh").

[Persepolis Administrative Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis_Administrative_Archives "Persepolis Administrative Archives") provide many insights into the Achaemenid government system. Found at [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis") in the 1930s, they are mostly in [Elamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Elamite "Achaemenid Elamite"); the remains of more than 10,000 of these cuneiform documents have been uncovered. [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Aramaic "Imperial Aramaic") is represented by about 1,000 or more original records. Only one tablet in [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian_language "Old Persian language") has been identified so far.

Also, many seals and seal impressions are found in these Persepolis archives. These documents represent administrative activity and flow of data in Persepolis over more than fifty consecutive years (509–457 BC).

### Coinage

The Persian [daric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daric "Daric") was the first [gold coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_coin "Gold coin") which, along with a similar silver coin, the [siglos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siglos "Siglos"), introduced the bimetallic [monetary standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_standard "Monetary standard") of the Achaemenids, which has continued until today. This was accomplished by [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I "Darius I"), who reinforced the empire and expanded [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis") as a ceremonial capital; he revolutionized the economy by placing it on the silver and gold coinage.

### Tax districts

Darius also introduced a regulated and sustainable tax system that was precisely tailored to each satrapy, based on their supposed productivity and their economic potential. For instance, [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon") was assessed for the highest amount and for a startling mixture of commodities – 1,000 [silver talents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talent_(measurement) "Talent (measurement)"), four months' supply of food for the army. [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindush "Hindush") was famed for its gold, while [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") was renowned for its agricultural wealth; destined as the Persian Empire's granary (later Rome's), Egypt had to supply 120,000 measures of grain plus 700 talents of silver. This was exclusively a tax levied on subject peoples. There is evidence that conquered and rebellious enemies could be sold into slavery. Alongside its other innovations in administration and taxation, the Achaemenids may have been the first government in the ancient Near East to register private slave sales and tax them using an early form of [sales tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_tax "Sales tax").

Other accomplishments of Darius' reign included the codification of the *dāta* (a universal legal system which would become the basis of later Iranian law), and the construction of a new capital at [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis").

### Transportation and communication

Under the Achaemenids, trade was extensive and there was an efficient infrastructure that facilitated the exchange of commodities in the far reaches of the empire. Tariffs on trade, along with agriculture and tribute, were major sources of revenue for the empire.

The satrapies were linked by a 2,500-kilometer highway, the most impressive stretch being the [Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road "Royal Road") from [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa") to [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis"), built under the command of [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great"). It connected the [Iranian Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_plateau "Iranian plateau") to [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia") and the Mediterranean. It featured stations and [caravanserais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai "Caravanserai") at specific intervals. The relays of mounted couriers (the [angarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angarium "Angarium")) could reach the remotest of areas in fifteen days. Herodotus observes that "there is nothing in the world that travels faster than these Persian couriers. Neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor gloom of night stays these courageous couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds." Despite the relative local independence given by the satrapy system, royal inspectors toured the empire and reported on local conditions.

Another highway of commerce was the [Great Khorasan Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khurasan_Road "Khurasan Road"), an informal mercantile route that originated in the fertile lowlands of Mesopotamia and snaked through the Zagros highlands, through the Iranian plateau and Afghanistan into the Central Asian regions of [Samarkand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarkand "Samarkand"), [Merv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merv "Merv") and [Ferghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fergana_Valley "Fergana Valley"), allowing for the construction of frontier cities like [Cyropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyropolis "Cyropolis"). After Alexander's conquests, the highway carried syncretic movements such as [Greco-Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhism "Greco-Buddhism") into Central Asia and China, and allowed the [Kushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushan_Empire "Kushan Empire"), [Indo-Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom "Indo-Greek Kingdom") and [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire") empires to profit from trade between East and West. This route was greatly rehabilitated and formalized during the [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate"), during which it developed into a major component of the famed [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road").

## Military

Despite its humble origins in Persis, the empire reached an enormous size under the leadership of [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great"). Cyrus created a multi-state empire where he allowed regional rulers, [satraps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrap "Satrap"), to rule as his proxy over a certain designated area of his empire called a [satrapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrapy "Satrapy"). The basic rule of governance was based upon loyalty and obedience of each satrapy to the central power, or the king, and compliance with tax laws. Due to the ethno-cultural diversity of the subject nations under the rule of Persia, its enormous geographic size, and the constant struggle for power by regional competitors, the creation of a professional army was necessary for both maintenance of the peace and to enforce the authority of the king in cases of rebellion and foreign threat. Cyrus managed to create a strong land army, using it to advance in his campaigns in [Babylonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_empire "Babylonian empire"), Lydia, and [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"), which after his death was used by his son [Cambyses II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambyses_II "Cambyses II"), in [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") against [Psamtik III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psamtik_III "Psamtik III"). Cyrus would die battling a local Iranian insurgency in the empire, before he could have a chance to develop a naval force. [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great") assumed that responsibility, establishing the Persians' first royal navy to confront foes across imperial waters such as the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea"), [Aegean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea "Aegean Sea"), [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf"), [Ionian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Sea "Ionian Sea"), and [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Infantry

The Achaemenid infantry consisted of three groups: the [Immortals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortals_(Persian_Empire) "Immortals (Persian Empire)"), the [Sparabara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparabara "Sparabara"), and the [Takabara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takabara "Takabara"), though in the later years of the Achaemenid Empire, a fourth group, the [Cardaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardaces "Cardaces"), were introduced.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The [Immortals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortals_(Persian_empire) "Immortals (Persian empire)") were described by [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") as being heavy [infantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infantry "Infantry"), led by [Hydarnes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydarnes_II "Hydarnes II"), that were kept constantly at a strength of exactly 10,000 men. He claimed that the unit's name stemmed from the custom that every killed, seriously wounded, or sick member was immediately replaced with a new one, maintaining the numbers and cohesion of the unit. They had wicker shields, short spears, swords or large daggers, and bow and arrows. Underneath their robes they wore [scale armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_armour "Scale armour") coats. The spear counterbalances of the common soldiery were of silver; to differentiate commanding ranks, the officers' spear butt-spikes were golden. Surviving Achaemenid colored glazed bricks and carved reliefs represent the Immortals as wearing elaborate robes, hoop earrings and gold jewellery, though these garments and accessories were most likely worn only for ceremonial occasions.

The [Sparabara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparabara "Sparabara") were usually the first to engage in hand-to-hand combat with the enemy. Though little survives about them, they are thought to have formed the Persian army's core, creating a [shield wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shield_wall "Shield wall") and wielding two-metre spears to shield vulnerable units such as [archers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archery "Archery"). The Sparabara were taken from the full members of Persian society, were trained from childhood to be soldiers and when not called out to fight on campaigns in distant lands they practised hunting on the vast plains of [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia "Persia"). However, when all was quiet and the *Pax Persica* held true, the Sparabara returned to normal life farming the land and grazing their herds. Because of this, they lacked true professional quality on the battlefield, yet they were well trained and courageous to the point of holding the line in most situations long enough for a counter-attack. They were armoured with quilted [linen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linen "Linen") and carried large rectangular [wicker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicker "Wicker") shields as a form of light maneuverable defence. This, however, left them at a severe disadvantage against heavily armoured opponents such as the [hoplite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoplite "Hoplite"), and their two-metre-long spear was not able to give the Sparabara ample range to plausibly engage a trained [phalanx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phalanx "Phalanx"). The wicker shields were able to effectively stop arrows but not strong enough to protect the soldier from spears. However, the Sparabara could deal with most other infantry, including trained units from the East.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The Achaemenids relied heavily on [archery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archery "Archery"). Major contributing nations were the [Scythians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians "Scythians"), [Medes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medes "Medes"), [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people "Persian people"), and the [Elamites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam "Elam"). The [composite bow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_bow "Composite bow") was used by the Persians and Medes, who adopted it from the Scythians and transmitted it to other nations, including the Greeks. Achaemenid armies typically used socketed, three-bladed (also known as trilobate or Scythian) [arrowheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowheads "Arrowheads"). These arrowheads were cast from leaded tin-bronze, which made them amenable to mass-production unlike the wrought iron arrowheads of the period that had to be individually forged.

The [Takabara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takabara "Takabara") were a rare unit who were a tough type of [peltasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peltasts "Peltasts"). They tended to fight with their own native weapons which would have included a crescent-shaped light wickerwork [shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shield "Shield") and [axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axe "Axe") as well as light linen cloth and [leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leather "Leather"). The Takabara were recruited from territories that incorporated modern Iran.

### Cavalry

The Persian cavalry was crucial for conquering nations and maintained its importance in the Achaemenid army to the last days of the Achaemenid Empire. The cavalry was separated into four groups. The Persian mounted forces comprised horse cavalry, chariotry (including scythed chariots), and units of camel-borne troops, though the chariots and camels were rarely effective against massed infantry.

In the later years of the Achaemenid Empire, the chariot archer had become merely a ceremonial part of the Persian army, yet in the early years of the Empire, their use was widespread. The chariot archers were armed with lances, bows, arrows, swords, and [scale armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_armour "Scale armour"). The horses were also suited with scale armour similar to scale armour of the [Sassanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanian "Sassanian") [cataphracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataphract "Cataphract"). The chariots would contain imperial symbols and decorations.

The horses used by the Achaemenids for cavalry were often suited with scale armour, like most cavalry units. The riders often had the same armour as Infantry units, wicker shields, short spears, swords or large daggers, bow and arrow, and scale armour coats. The camel cavalry was different, because the camels and sometimes the riders, were provided little protection against enemies, yet when they were offered protection, they would have lances, swords, bow, arrow, and scale armour. The camel cavalry was first introduced into the Persian army by [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great"), at the [Battle of Thymbra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thymbra "Battle of Thymbra"). The elephant was most likely introduced into the Persian army by [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I "Darius I") after his [conquest of the Indus Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_invasion_of_the_Indus_Valley "Achaemenid invasion of the Indus Valley"). Elephants may have been used in Greek campaigns by Darius and [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I"), but Greek accounts only mention 15 of them being used at the [Battle of Gaugamela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gaugamela "Battle of Gaugamela").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Navy

Since its foundation by Cyrus, the Persian empire had been primarily a land empire with a strong army but void of any actual naval forces. By the 5th century BC, this was to change, as the empire came across Greek and Egyptian forces, each with their own maritime traditions and capabilities. [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great") was the first Achaemenid king to invest in a Persian fleet. Even by then no true "imperial navy" had existed either in Greece or Egypt. Persia would become the first empire, under Darius, to inaugurate and deploy the first regular imperial navy. Despite this achievement, the personnel for the imperial navy would not come from Iran, but were often [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") (mostly from [Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidon "Sidon")), [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians "Egyptians") and [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_people "Greek people") chosen by Darius the Great to operate the empire's combat vessels.

At first the ships were built in Sidon by the Phoenicians; the first Achaemenid ships measured about 40 meters in length and 6 meters in width, able to transport up to 300 Persian [troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Immortals "Persian Immortals") at any one trip. Soon, other states of the empire were constructing their own ships, each incorporating slight local preferences. The ships eventually found their way to the Persian Gulf and Persian naval forces laid the foundation for a strong Persian maritime presence there. Persians also had ships often of a capacity 100 to 200 troops patrolling the empire's various rivers including the [Karun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun "Karun"), [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris") and [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile") in the west, as well as the [Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River "Indus River").

The Achaemenid navy established bases located along the Karun, and in Bahrain, Oman, and Yemen. The Persian fleet was not only used for peace-keeping purposes along the Karun but also opened the door to trade with India via the Persian Gulf. While Darius's navy ranked as a world power, [Artaxerxes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_II "Artaxerxes II") constructed a formidable fleet in summer 397 BC as part of a rearmament that culminated in his decisive victory at [Knidos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knidos "Knidos") in 394 BC, restoring Achaemenid control over [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia "Ionia"). Artaxerxes II would also use his navy to later on quell a rebellion in Egypt.

The construction material of choice was wood, but some armoured Achaemenid ships had metallic blades on the front, often meant to slice enemy ships using the ship's momentum. Naval ships were also equipped with hooks on the side to grab enemy ships, or to negotiate their position. The ships were propelled by sails or manpower. The ships the Persians created were unique. As far as maritime engagement, the ships were equipped with two [mangonels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangonel "Mangonel") that would launch projectiles such as stones, or flammable substances.

[Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon "Xenophon") describes his eyewitness account of a massive military bridge created by joining 37 Persian ships across the Tigris. The Persians used each boat's buoyancy to support a connected bridge above which supply could be transferred. [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") also gives many accounts of the Persians using ships to build bridges.

Darius I, in an attempt to subdue the [Scythian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythia "Scythia") horsemen north of the Black Sea, crossed over at the [Bosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosphorus "Bosphorus"), using an enormous bridge made by connecting Achaemenid boats, then marched up to the [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube "Danube"), crossing it by means of a second boat bridge. The bridge across the Bosporus joined the closest points of Asia and Europe, covering at least 1,000 meters of open water. Herodotus describes the spectacle, and calls it the "bridge of Darius":

> *Strait called Bosphorus, across which the bridge of Darius had been thrown, is hundred and twenty [furlongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furlong "Furlong") in length, reaching from the [Euxine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euxine "Euxine"), to the [Propontis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propontis "Propontis"). The Propontis is five hundred furlongs across and fourteen hundred long. Its waters flow into the [Hellespont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles "Dardanelles"), the length of which is four hundred furlongs ...*

Years later, a similar boat bridge would be constructed by [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I"), in his invasion of Greece. Although the Persians failed to capture the Greek city-states completely, the tradition of maritime involvement was carried down by the Persian kings, most notably Artaxerxes II. In spring 327 BC, during Alexander's invasion of Persia and his advance into India, he followed Persian military practice by having [Hephaestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestion "Hephaestion") and [Perdiccas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdiccas "Perdiccas") construct a similar boat-bridge at the Indus river.

## Culture

### Languages

During the reign of Cyrus II and Darius I, and as long as the seat of government was still at [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa") in [Elam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam "Elam"), the language of the chancellery was [Elamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite_language "Elamite language"). This is primarily attested in the [Persepolis fortification and treasury tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis_Fortification_Tablets "Persepolis Fortification Tablets") that reveal details of the day-to-day functioning of the empire. The Elamite texts in the kings' large rock-face inscriptions are invariably accompanied by [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_language "Akkadian language") (Babylonian dialect) and [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian "Old Persian") versions, and appear to be translations of the Old Persian originals. It is then likely that although Elamite was used by the capital government in Susa, it was not a standardized language.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Following the conquest of [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), the [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_Aramaic "Official Aramaic") language (as used in that territory) was adopted as a "vehicle for written communication between the different regions of the vast empire with its different peoples and languages. The use of a single official language, which modern scholarship has dubbed "Official Aramaic" or "Imperial Aramaic", can be assumed to have greatly contributed to the astonishing success of the Achaemenids in holding their far-flung empire together for as long as they did."

In 1955, Richard Frye questioned the classification of Imperial Aramaic as an [official language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_language "Official language"), noting that no surviving edict expressly and unambiguously accorded that status to any particular language. Frye reclassifies Imperial Aramaic as the *[lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca "Lingua franca")* of the Achaemenid empire, suggesting that the use of Aramaic language in Achaemenid empire was more widespread than generally thought. Many centuries after the fall of the empire, [Aramaic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_script "Aramaic script") and—as [ideograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huzvarishn "Huzvarishn")—Aramaic vocabulary would survive as the essential characteristics of the [Pahlavi writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahlavi_scripts "Pahlavi scripts").

Although Old Persian also appears on some seals and art objects, that language is attested primarily in the Achaemenid inscriptions of Western Iran, suggesting then that Old Persian was the common language of that region. However, by the reign of Artaxerxes II, the grammar and orthography of the inscriptions was so "far from perfect" that it has been suggested that the scribes who composed those texts had already largely forgotten the language, and had to rely on older inscriptions, which they to a great extent reproduced verbatim.

When the occasion demanded, Achaemenid administrative correspondence was conducted in [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Greek "Old Greek"), making it a widely used [bureaucratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucratic "Bureaucratic") language. Native Old Iranian sources show no Greek linguistic influence, despite extensive Achaemenid-Greek contact and the Achaemenid conquest of many Greek-speaking areas in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") and [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor") at various times. Besides Herodotus' accounts, evidence shows Greeks were deployed and employed in the empire's core regions and also lived and worked in its heartland, Iran. For example, Greeks were part of the various ethnicities that constructed [Darius' palace in Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius%27_palace_in_Susa "Darius' palace in Susa"), apart from the Greek inscriptions found nearby there, and one short Persepolis tablet written in Greek.

### Customs

[Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") mentions that the Persians were invited to great [birthday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birthday "Birthday") feasts (Herodotus, *[Histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_(Herodotus) "Histories (Herodotus)")* 8), which would be followed by many desserts, a treat which they reproached the Greeks for omitting from their meals. He also observed that the Persians drank wine in large quantities and used it even for counsel, deliberating on important affairs when drunk, and deciding the next day, when sober, whether to act on the decision or set it aside.

### Religion

[Religious toleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_tolerance "Religious tolerance") has been described as a "remarkable feature" of the Achaemenid Empire. The [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") reports that Persian king [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great") released the [Jewish people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") from the [Babylonian captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity "Babylonian captivity") in 539–530 BC and [permitted them to return to their homeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_to_Zion "Return to Zion"). Cyrus the Great assisted in the restoration of the sacred places of various cities, including to rebuild the [Israelites'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelites "Israelites") [Temple in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem "Temple in Jerusalem").

It was during the Achaemenid period that [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism") reached southwestern Iran, where it came to be accepted by the rulers and through them became a defining element of Persian culture. The religion was not only accompanied by a formalization of the concepts and divinities of the traditional Iranian [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_(gods) "Pantheon (gods)") but also introduced several novel ideas, including that of [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will_in_theology "Free will in theology").

[Mithra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithra "Mithra") was worshipped in the Empire; his temples and symbols were the most widespread, most people bore names related to him and most festivals were dedicated to him.

During the reign of [Artaxerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_I "Artaxerxes I") and [Darius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_II "Darius II"), the Greek historian [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") wrote: "[the Persians] have no images of the gods, no temples nor altars, and consider the use of them a sign of folly. This comes, I think, from their not believing the gods to have the same nature with men, [as the Greeks imagine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_religion "Ancient Greek religion")." He claims the Persians offer sacrifice to: "the sun and moon, to the earth, to fire, to water, and to the winds. These are the only gods whose worship has come down to them from ancient times. At a later period, they began the worship of [Urania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urania "Urania"), which they borrowed from the [Arabians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia "Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia") and [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria"). [Mylitta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullissu "Mullissu") is the name by which the Assyrians know this goddess, to whom the Persians referred as [Anahita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anahita "Anahita")."

The Babylonian scholar and priest [Berosus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berossus "Berossus") records—although writing over seventy years after the reign of [Artaxerxes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_II "Artaxerxes II")—that the emperor had been the first to make cult statues of divinities and have them placed in temples in many of the major cities of the empire. Berosus also substantiates Herodotus when he says the Persians knew of no images of gods until Artaxerxes II erected those images. On the means of sacrifice, Herodotus adds "they raise no altar, light no fire, pour no libations." Herodotus also observed that "no prayer or offering can be made without a [magus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magi "Magi") present".

### Women

The position of women in the Achaemenid Empire varied depending on the culture to which they belonged and therefore differed by region. The position of Persian women in Persia proper has traditionally been reconstructed from mythological Biblical references and the sometimes biased Ancient Greek sources, neither fully reliable, while the most reliable evidence is the archeological Persepolis Fortification Tablets (PFT), which describe women connected to the royal court at Persepolis, from royal women to female laborers who received food rations there.

The hierarchy of royal women at the Persian court was ranked as follows: the king's mother, then the queen, the king's daughters, the king's concubines, and the other women of the royal palace. The king normally married a female member of the royal family or a Persian noblewoman related to a satrap or another important Persian man; it was permitted for members of the royal family to marry relatives, but there is no evidence for marriage between closer family members than half-siblings. The king's concubines were often either slaves, sometimes prisoners of war, or foreign princesses, whom the king did not marry because they were foreigners, and whose children did not have the right to inherit the throne.

Greek sources accuse the king of having hundreds of concubines secluded in a [harem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harem "Harem"). There is no archeological evidence supporting the existence of a harem, or the seclusion of women from contact with men, at the Persian court. The royal women joined the king at breakfast and dinner and accompanied him on his journeys. They may have participated in the royal hunt, as well as during the royal banquets. [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotos "Herodotos") relates how the Persian envoys at the Macedonian court demanded the presence of women during a banquet because it was the custom for women to participate in the banquets in their own country.

The queen may have attended the king's audience, and archeological evidence shows that she gave her own audiences, at least for female supplicants. Royal women and noblewomen at court could travel on their own, accompanied by both male and female staff, own and manage their own fortune, land, and business. Depictions of Persian women show them with long dresses and veils which did not cover their faces or their hair, only flowing down over their neck at the back of the head as an ornament.

Royal and aristocratic Achaemenid women were given an education in subjects that did not appear compatible with seclusions, such as horsemanship and archery. Royal and aristocratic women held and managed vast estates and workshops and employed large numbers of servants and professional laborers. Royal and aristocratic women do not seem to have lived in seclusion from men, since it is known that they appeared in public and traveled with their husbands, participated in hunting and in feasts. The chief wife of a royal or aristocratic man did not live in seclusion, as it is stated that wives customarily accompanied their husbands at dinner banquets. They left the banquet when the "women entertainers" came in and the men began "merrymaking".

No woman ever ruled the Achaemenid Empire, as [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch "Monarch") or as [regent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent "Regent"), but some queen's consorts are known to have had influence over the affairs of state, notably [Atossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atossa "Atossa") and [Parysatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parysatis "Parysatis").

There is not evidence of any women being employed as an official in the administration or within religious service; however, there are plenty of archeological evidence of women being employed as free labourers in Persepolis, where they worked alongside men. Women could be employed as the leaders of their workforce, known by the title *arraššara pašabena*, which were then given a higher salary than the male workers of their workforce. Female laborers were given less than men. Qualified workers within the crafts were given equal pay regardless of their sex. Women are abundant in religious context in the cuneiform sources from late Achaemenid and Hellenistic [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon").

### Architecture and art

Achaemenid architecture included large cities, temples, palaces, and [mausolea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausolea "Mausolea") such as the [Tomb of Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Cyrus_the_Great "Tomb of Cyrus the Great"). The quintessential feature of its architecture was its eclectic nature, with elements of Median, Assyrian, and Asiatic Greek all incorporated, yet maintaining a unique Persian identity seen in the finished products. Its influence pervades the regions ruled by the Achaemenids, from the Mediterranean shores to India, especially with its emphasis on monumental stone-cut design and gardens subdivided by water-courses.

Achaemenid art includes [frieze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frieze "Frieze") reliefs, metalwork such as the [Oxus Treasure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxus_Treasure "Oxus Treasure"), decoration of palaces, glazed brick masonry, fine craftsmanship (masonry, carpentry, etc.), and gardening. Although the Persians drew artists from all corners of their empire, incorporating their styles and techniques, they did not merely produce a combination of styles, but a synthesis of a new, unique Persian style.

One of the most remarkable examples of both Achaemenid architecture and art is the grand palace of [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis"), and its detailed workmanship, coupled with its grand scale. In describing the construction of the [Palace of Darius in Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Darius_in_Susa "Palace of Darius in Susa"), [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great") records:

> Yaka timber was brought from [Gandara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara "Gandhara") and from [Carmania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmania_(region) "Carmania (region)"). The gold was brought from [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis") and from [Bactria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria "Bactria") ... the precious stone lapis-lazuli and carnelian ... was brought from [Sogdiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdiana "Sogdiana"). The turquoise from [Chorasmia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorasmia "Chorasmia"), the silver and ebony from [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt"), the ornamentation from [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia "Ionia"), the ivory from [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia") and from [Sindh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sindh "Sindh") and from [Arachosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arachosia "Arachosia"). The stone-cutters who wrought the stone were Ionians and [Sardians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis"). The goldsmiths were [Medes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medes "Medes") and [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians "Egyptians"). The men who wrought the wood were Sardinians and Egyptians. The men who wrought the baked brick were Babylonians. The men who adorned the wall, those were Medes and Egyptians.

— [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great "Darius the Great"), [DSf inscription](http://www.avesta.org/op/op.htm#dsf)

### Tombs

Many Achaemenid rulers built tombs for themselves. [Naqsh-e Rostam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naqsh-e_Rostam "Naqsh-e Rostam"), the most famous, is an ancient necropolis about 12 km north-west of [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis"), where four of the dynasty's kings have tombs carved into the mountain: [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Darius_I "Tomb of Darius I"), [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I"), [Artaxerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_I "Artaxerxes I") and [Darius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_II "Darius II"). Other kings constructed their own tombs elsewhere.

[Artaxerxes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_II "Artaxerxes II") and [Artaxerxes III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_III "Artaxerxes III") preferred to carve their tombs beside their spring capital [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis"). The left tomb belongs to Artaxerxes II and the right tomb belongs to Artaxerxes III, the last Achaemenid king to have a tomb. The [tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Cyrus_the_Great "Tomb of Cyrus the Great") of the founder of the Achaemenid dynasty, [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great"), was built in [Pasargadae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasargadae "Pasargadae") (now a world heritage site).

## Legacy

The Achaemenid Empire has been referred to as the first "world empire" as it contained in the words of Persian kings "many people and many languages". The diverse groups which made up the empire enjoyed a great deal of legal and cultural autonomy. The Achaemenid Empire has also left a lasting impression on the heritage and cultural identity of Asia and the Middle East, and influenced the development and structure of future [empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire "Empire"). In fact, the Greeks, and later on the Romans, adopted the best features of the Persian method of governing an empire.

The Persian model of governance was particularly formative in the expansion and maintenance of the [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate"), whose rule is widely considered the period of the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age"). Similar to the ancient Persians, the Abbasid dynasty established its empire's center in Mesopotamia, founding the new cities of [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad") and [Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarra "Samarra") near the historic site of Babylon. The dynasty drew significant support from the Persian aristocracy and integrated Persian language and architecture extensively into Islamic culture.

The Achaemenid Empire is noted in Western history as the antagonist of the [Greek city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis "Polis") during the [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars "Greco-Persian Wars") and for the emancipation of the [Jewish exiles in Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity "Babylonian captivity"). The historical mark of the empire went far beyond its territorial and military influences and included cultural, social, technological and religious influences as well. For example, many [Athenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens") adopted Achaemenid customs in their daily lives in a reciprocal cultural exchange, some being employed by or allied to the Persian kings. The impact of the [Edict of Cyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Cyrus "Edict of Cyrus") is mentioned in Judeo-Christian texts, and the empire was instrumental in the spread of [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism") as far east as China. The empire also set the tone for the [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics "Politics"), heritage and [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History "History") of [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran") (also known as Persia).

Historian [Arnold Toynbee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_J._Toynbee "Arnold J. Toynbee") regarded Abbasid society as a "reintegration" or "reincarnation" of Achaemenid society, as the synthesis of Persian, Turkic and Islamic modes of governance and knowledge allowed for the spread of [Persianate culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persianate_society "Persianate society") over a wide swath of Eurasia through the Turkic-origin [Seljuq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire "Seljuk Empire"), [Ottoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire"), [Safavid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran") and [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") empires. Historian [Bernard Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Lewis "Bernard Lewis") writes,

> The work of Iranians can be seen in every field of cultural endeavor, including Arabic poetry, to which poets of Iranian origin composing their poems in Arabic made a very significant contribution. In a sense, Iranian Islam is a second advent of Islam itself, a new Islam sometimes referred to as Islam-i-Ajam. It was this Persian Islam, rather than the original Arab Islam, that was brought to new areas and new peoples: to the Turks, first in Central Asia and then in the Middle East in the country which came to be called Turkey, and of course to India. The Ottoman Turks brought a form of Iranian civilization to the walls of Vienna. [...] By the time of the great Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century, Iranian Islam had become not only an important component; it had become a dominant element in Islam itself, and for several centuries the main centers of the Islamic power and civilization were in countries that were, if not Iranian, at least marked by Iranian civilization ... The major centers of Islam in the late medieval and early modern periods, the centers of both political and cultural power, such as India, Central Asia, Iran, Turkey, were all part of this Iranian civilization.

[Georg W. F. Hegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_W._F._Hegel "Georg W. F. Hegel") in his work *[The Philosophy of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lectures_on_the_Philosophy_of_History "Lectures on the Philosophy of History")* introduces the Persian Empire as the "first empire that passed away" and its people as the "first historical people" in history. According to his account:

> The Persian Empire is an empire in the modern sense—like that which existed in Germany, and the great imperial realm under the sway of Napoleon; for we find it consisting of a number of states, which are indeed dependent, but which have retained their own individuality, their manners, and laws. The general enactments, binding upon all, did not infringe upon their political and social idiosyncrasies, but even protected and maintained them; so that each of the nations that constitute the whole, had its own form of constitution. As light illuminates everything—imparting to each object a peculiar vitality—so the Persian Empire extends over a multitude of nations, and leaves to each one its particular character. Some have even kings of their own; each one its distinct language, arms, way of life and customs. All this diversity coexists harmoniously under the impartial dominion of Light ... a combination of peoples—leaving each of them free. Thereby, a stop is put to that barbarism and ferocity with which the nations had been wont to carry on their destructive feuds.

[Will Durant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Durant "Will Durant"), the American historian and philosopher, during one of his speeches, "Persia in the History of Civilization", as an address before the *[Iran–America Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93America_Society "Iran–America Society")* in [Tehran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tehran "Tehran") on 21 April 1948, stated:

> For thousands of years Persians have been creating beauty. Sixteen centuries before Christ there went from these regions or near it ... You have been here a kind of watershed of civilization, pouring your blood and thought and art and religion eastward and westward into the world ... I need not rehearse for you again the achievements of your Achaemenid period. Then for the first time in known history an empire almost as extensive as the United States received an orderly government, a competence of administration, a web of swift communications, a security of movement by men and goods on majestic roads, equalled before our time only by the zenith of Imperial Rome.
