# Philosophy

**Philosophy** (from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") φιλοσοφία (*philosophía*), lit. 'love of wisdom') is a systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning topics like [existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence "Existence"), [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge "Knowledge"), [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind "Mind"), [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason"), [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language "Language"), and [value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_(ethics_and_social_sciences) "Value (ethics and social sciences)"). It is a rational and critical inquiry that reflects on its methods and assumptions.

Historically, many of the individual [sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science "Science"), such as [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology"), formed part of philosophy. However, they are considered separate academic disciplines in the modern sense of the term. Influential traditions in the [history of philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy "History of philosophy") include [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy "Western philosophy"), [Arabic–Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy "Islamic philosophy"), [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy "Indian philosophy"), and [Chinese philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_philosophy "Chinese philosophy"). Western philosophy originated in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") and covers a wide area of philosophical subfields. A central topic in Arabic–Persian philosophy is the [relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_(philosophy) "Relation (philosophy)") between reason and [revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation "Revelation"). Indian philosophy combines the [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") problem of how to reach [enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlightenment_in_Buddhism "Enlightenment in Buddhism") with the exploration of the nature of reality and the ways of arriving at knowledge. Chinese philosophy focuses principally on practical issues about right social conduct, government, and [self-cultivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-cultivation "Self-cultivation").

Major branches of philosophy are [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology"), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics"). Epistemology studies what knowledge is and how to acquire it. Ethics investigates moral principles and what constitutes right conduct. Logic is the study of [correct reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_reasoning "Logical reasoning") and explores how good [arguments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument "Argument") can be distinguished from bad ones. Metaphysics examines the most general features of [reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality "Reality"), existence, [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_(philosophy) "Object (philosophy)"), and [properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_(philosophy) "Property (philosophy)"). Other subfields are [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics"), [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language "Philosophy of language"), [philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind "Philosophy of mind"), [philosophy of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mathematics "Philosophy of mathematics"), [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science"), [philosophy of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion "Philosophy of religion"), [philosophy of history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_history "Philosophy of history"), and [political philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy "Political philosophy"). Within each branch, there are competing [schools of philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_philosophy "Schools of philosophy") that promote different principles, theories, or methods.

Philosophers use a great variety of methods to arrive at philosophical knowledge. They include [conceptual analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_analysis "Conceptual analysis"), reliance on [common sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_sense "Common sense") and [intuitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition "Intuition"), use of [thought experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_experiments "Thought experiments"), analysis of [ordinary language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_language "Ordinary language"), [description of experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_(philosophy) "Phenomenology (philosophy)"), and [critical questioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_questioning "Socratic questioning"). Many scientific academic disciplines have associated philosophical subfields, like [philosophy of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_physics "Philosophy of physics") and [philosophy of biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_biology "Philosophy of biology"), examining their fundamental concepts, methods, assumptions, and implications. Philosophy is related to and informs many other fields, such as [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law "Law"), [business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business "Business"), and [journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism "Journalism"), providing an [interdisciplinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary "Interdisciplinary") perspective and addressing their ethical questions.

## Etymology

The word *philosophy* comes from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") term φιλοσοφία (*philosophía*), meaning 'love of wisdom', from φίλος (*phílos*, 'loving, friend of') and σοφία ([*sophía*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_(wisdom)#Greek_and_Hellenistic_tradition "Sophia (wisdom)"), 'wisdom'). Some sources say that the term was coined by the [pre-Socratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratic "Pre-Socratic") philosopher [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras "Pythagoras"), but this is not certain.

The word entered the [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language") primarily from [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French "Old French") and [Anglo-Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_language "Anglo-Norman language") starting around 1175 CE. The French *philosophie* is itself a borrowing from the Latin *philosophia*. The term *philosophy* acquired the meanings of "advanced study of the speculative subjects ([logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics"))", "deep wisdom consisting of love of [truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth "Truth") and [virtuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtuous "Virtuous") living", "profound learning as transmitted by the ancient writers", and "the study of the fundamental nature of [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge "Knowledge"), [reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality "Reality"), and [existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence "Existence"), and the basic limits of human understanding".

Before the modern age, the term *philosophy* was used in a wide sense. It included most forms of [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational "Rational") inquiry, such as the individual [sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sciences "Sciences"), as its subdisciplines. For instance, [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") was a major branch of philosophy. This branch of philosophy encompassed a wide range of fields, including disciplines like physics, [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), and [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology"). An example of this usage is the 1687 book *[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica "Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica")* by [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"). This book referred to natural philosophy in its title, but it is today considered a book of physics.

The meaning of *philosophy* changed toward the end of the modern period when it acquired the narrower meaning common today. In this new sense, the term is mainly associated with disciplines like metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics. Among other topics, it covers the rational study of reality, knowledge, and values. It is distinguished from other disciplines of rational inquiry such as the empirical sciences and [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics").

## Conceptions of philosophy

### General conception

The practice of philosophy is characterized by several general features: it is a form of rational inquiry, it aims to be systematic, and it tends to critically reflect on its own methods and presuppositions. It requires thinking attentively, long and carefully, about the provocative, vexing, and enduring problems central to the [human condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_condition "Human condition").

The philosophical pursuit of wisdom involves asking general and fundamental questions. It often does not result in straightforward answers but may help a person to better understand the topic, examine their life, dispel confusion, and overcome prejudices and self-deceptive ideas associated with common sense. For example, [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates") stated that "[the unexamined life is not worth living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_unexamined_life_is_not_worth_living "The unexamined life is not worth living")" to highlight the role of philosophical inquiry in understanding one's own existence. And according to [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell "Bertrand Russell"),

> the man who has no tincture of philosophy goes through life imprisoned in the prejudices derived from common sense, from the habitual beliefs of his age or his nation, and from convictions which have grown up in his mind without the cooperation or consent of his deliberate reason.

### Academic definitions

Attempts to provide more precise definitions of philosophy are controversial and are studied in [metaphilosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphilosophy "Metaphilosophy"). Some approaches argue that there is a set of essential features shared by all parts of philosophy. Others see only weaker [family resemblances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_resemblance "Family resemblance") or contend that it is merely an empty blanket term. Precise definitions are often only accepted by theorists belonging to a certain [philosophical movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_movement "Philosophical movement") and are revisionist according to Søren Overgaard et al. in that many presumed parts of philosophy would not deserve the title "philosophy" if they were true.

Some definitions characterize philosophy in relation to its method, like pure reasoning. Others focus on its topic; for example, as the study of the biggest patterns of the world as a whole or as the attempt to answer the big questions. Such an approach is pursued by [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant"), who holds that the task of philosophy is united by four questions: "What can I know?", "What should I do?", "What may I hope?", and "What is the human being?" Both approaches have the problem that they are usually either too wide, by including non-philosophical disciplines, or too narrow, by excluding some philosophical subdisciplines.

Many definitions of philosophy emphasize its intimate relation to science. In this sense, philosophy is sometimes understood as a proper science in its own right. According to some [naturalistic philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(philosophy) "Naturalism (philosophy)"), such as [W. V. O. Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._O._Quine "W. V. O. Quine"), philosophy is an empirical yet abstract science that is concerned with wide-ranging empirical patterns instead of particular observations. Science-based definitions usually face the problem of explaining why philosophy in its long history has not progressed to the same extent or in the same way as the sciences. This problem is avoided by seeing philosophy as an immature or provisional science whose subdisciplines cease to be philosophy once they have fully developed. In this sense, philosophy is sometimes described as "the midwife of the sciences".

Other definitions focus on the contrast between science and philosophy. A common theme among many such conceptions is that philosophy is concerned with [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_(philosophy) "Meaning (philosophy)"), [understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding "Understanding"), or the clarification of language. According to one view, philosophy is [conceptual analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_analysis "Conceptual analysis"), which involves finding the [necessary and sufficient conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions "Necessary and sufficient conditions") for the application of concepts. Another definition characterizes philosophy as *[thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking "Thinking") about thinking* to emphasize its self-critical, reflective nature. A further approach presents philosophy as a [linguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic "Linguistic") therapy. According to [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Wittgenstein "Ludwig Wittgenstein"), for instance, philosophy aims at dispelling misunderstandings to which humans are susceptible due to the confusing structure of [ordinary language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language "Natural language").

[Phenomenologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_(philosophy) "Phenomenology (philosophy)"), such as [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl "Edmund Husserl"), characterize philosophy as a "rigorous science" investigating [essences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essence "Essence"). They practice a radical [suspension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoche "Epoche") of theoretical assumptions about reality to get back to the "things themselves"; that is, as originally given in experience. They contend that this base-level of experience provides the foundation for higher-order theoretical knowledge and that one needs to understand the former to understand the latter.

An early approach found in [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy") and [Roman philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_philosophy "Roman philosophy") is that philosophy is the spiritual practice of developing one's rational capacities. This practice is an expression of the philosopher's love of wisdom and has the aim of improving one's [well-being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-being "Well-being") by leading a reflective life. For example, the [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoics "Stoics") saw philosophy as an exercise to train the mind and thereby achieve [eudaimonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudaimonia "Eudaimonia") and flourish in life.

## History

As a discipline, the history of philosophy aims to provide a systematic and chronological exposition of philosophical concepts and doctrines. Some theorists see it as a part of [intellectual history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history "Intellectual history"), but it also investigates questions not covered by intellectual history, such as whether the theories of past philosophers are true and have remained philosophically relevant. The history of philosophy is primarily concerned with theories based on rational inquiry and argumentation; some historians understand it in a looser sense that includes [myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth "Myth"), [religious teachings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion"), and proverbial lore.

Influential traditions in the history of philosophy include [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy "Western philosophy"), [Arabic–Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy "Islamic philosophy"), [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy "Indian philosophy"), and [Chinese philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_philosophy "Chinese philosophy"). Other philosophical traditions are [Japanese philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_philosophy "Japanese philosophy"), [Latin American philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_philosophy "Latin American philosophy"), and [African philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_philosophy "African philosophy").

### Western

Western philosophy originated in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") in the 6th century BCE with the [pre-Socratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratic_philosophy "Pre-Socratic philosophy") philosophers, such as [Thales of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus "Thales of Miletus") (c. 626 – c. 545 BCE). They attempted to provide rational explanations of the [cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos "Cosmos") as a whole. The philosophy following them was shaped by [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates") (469–399 BCE), [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") (427–347 BCE), and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") (384–322 BCE). They expanded the range of topics to questions like [how people should act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), [how to arrive at knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology"), and what the [nature of reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") and [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind "Mind") is. The later part of the ancient period was marked by the emergence of philosophical movements, for example, [Epicureanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicureanism "Epicureanism"), [Stoicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism "Stoicism"), [Skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skepticism "Skepticism"), and [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism "Neoplatonism"). The medieval period started in the 5th century CE. Its focus was on religious topics, and many thinkers used ancient philosophy to explain and further elaborate [Christian doctrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_doctrine "Christian doctrine").

The [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") period started in the 14th century and saw a renewed interest in schools of ancient philosophy, in particular [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism "Platonism"). [Humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism "Renaissance humanism") also emerged in this period. The modern period started in the 17th century. One of its central concerns was how philosophical and scientific knowledge is created. Specific importance was given to the [role of reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism "Rationalism") and [sensory experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism "Empiricism"). Many of these innovations were used in the [Enlightenment movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment") to challenge traditional authorities. Several attempts to develop comprehensive systems of philosophy were made in the 19th century, for instance, by [German idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_idealism "German idealism") and [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism"). Influential developments in [20th-century philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_philosophy "Contemporary philosophy") were the emergence and application of [formal logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_logic "Formal logic"), the focus on the [role of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_turn "Linguistic turn") as well as [pragmatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatism "Pragmatism"), and movements in [continental philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_philosophy "Continental philosophy") like phenomenology, [existentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism"), and [post-structuralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-structuralism "Post-structuralism"). The 20th century saw a rapid expansion of academic philosophy in terms of the number of philosophical publications and philosophers working at [academic institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_institution "Academic institution"). There was also a noticeable growth in the number of [female philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Female_philosophers "Female philosophers"), but they still remained underrepresented.

### Arabic–Persian

Arabic–Persian philosophy arose in the early 9th century CE as a response to discussions in the [Islamic theological tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam "Kalam"). Its classical period lasted until the 12th century CE and was strongly influenced by ancient Greek philosophers. It employed their ideas to elaborate and interpret the teachings of the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran").

[Al-Kindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi "Al-Kindi") (801–873 CE) is usually regarded as the first philosopher of this tradition. He translated and interpreted many works of Aristotle and Neoplatonists in his attempt to show that there is a harmony between [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason") and [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith "Faith"). [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna") (980–1037 CE) also followed this goal and developed a comprehensive philosophical system to provide a rational understanding of reality encompassing science, religion, and mysticism. [Al-Ghazali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali "Al-Ghazali") (1058–1111 CE) was a strong critic of the idea that reason can arrive at a true understanding of reality and God. He formulated a detailed [critique of philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Incoherence_of_the_Philosophers "The Incoherence of the Philosophers") and tried to assign philosophy a more limited place besides the teachings of the Quran and mystical insight. Following Al-Ghazali and the end of the classical period, the influence of philosophical inquiry waned. [Mulla Sadra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulla_Sadra "Mulla Sadra") (1571–1636 CE) is often regarded as one of the most influential philosophers of the subsequent period. The increasing influence of Western thought and institutions in the 19th and 20th centuries gave rise to the intellectual movement of [Islamic modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_modernism "Islamic modernism"), which aims to understand the relation between traditional Islamic beliefs and modernity.

### Indian

One of the distinguishing features of Indian philosophy is that it integrates the exploration of the nature of reality, the ways of arriving at knowledge, and the [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") question of how to reach [enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlightenment_in_Buddhism "Enlightenment in Buddhism"). It started in the second and first millennia BCE, when the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") and [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads") were composed. They are the foundational scriptures of [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") and contemplate issues concerning the relation between the [self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_(Hinduism) "Ātman (Hinduism)") and [ultimate reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") as well as the question of how [souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiva "Jiva") are reborn based on their [past actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma "Karma"). This period also saw the emergence of non-Vedic teachings, like [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") and [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism "Jainism"). Buddhism was founded by [Gautama Siddhartha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Siddhartha "Gautama Siddhartha") (563–483 BCE), who challenged the Vedic idea of a [permanent self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anicca "Anicca") and proposed [a path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path "Noble Eightfold Path") to liberate oneself from [suffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du%E1%B8%A5kha "Duḥkha"). Jainism was founded by [Mahavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavira "Mahavira") (599–527 BCE), who emphasized [non-violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahi%E1%B9%83s%C4%81 "Ahiṃsā") as well as respect toward all forms of life.

The subsequent classical period started roughly 200 BCE and was characterized by the emergence of the six [orthodox schools of Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astika "Astika"): [Nyāyá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyaya "Nyaya"), [Vaiśeṣika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vai%C5%9Be%E1%B9%A3ika "Vaiśeṣika"), [Sāṃkhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C4%81%E1%B9%83khya "Sāṃkhya"), [Yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga "Yoga"), [Mīmāṃsā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%ABm%C4%81%E1%B9%83s%C4%81 "Mīmāṃsā"), and [Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanta "Vedanta"). The school of [Advaita Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advaita_Vedanta "Advaita Vedanta") developed later in this period. It was systematized by [Adi Shankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankara "Adi Shankara") (c. 700–750 CE), who held that [everything is one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism") and that the impression of a universe consisting of many distinct entities is an [illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_(religion) "Maya (religion)"). A slightly different perspective was defended by [Ramanuja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanuja "Ramanuja") (1017–1137 CE), who founded the school of [Vishishtadvaita Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishishtadvaita_Vedanta "Vishishtadvaita Vedanta") and argued that individual entities are real as aspects or parts of the underlying unity. He also helped to popularize the [Bhakti movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti_movement "Bhakti movement"), which taught [devotion toward the divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti "Bhakti") as a spiritual path and lasted until the 17th to 18th centuries CE. The modern period began roughly 1800 CE and was shaped by encounters with Western thought. Philosophers tried to formulate comprehensive systems to harmonize diverse philosophical and religious teachings. For example, [Swami Vivekananda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swami_Vivekananda "Swami Vivekananda") (1863–1902 CE) used the teachings of Advaita Vedanta to argue that all the different religions are valid paths toward the one divine.

### Chinese

Chinese philosophy is particularly interested in practical questions associated with right social conduct, government, and [self-cultivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-cultivation "Self-cultivation"). Many [schools of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Schools_of_Thought "Hundred Schools of Thought") emerged in the 6th century BCE in competing attempts to resolve the political turbulence of that period. The most prominent among them were [Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism "Confucianism") and [Daoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daoism "Daoism"). Confucianism was founded by [Confucius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucius "Confucius") (551–479 BCE). It focused on different forms of moral [virtues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue "Virtue") and explored how they lead to harmony in society. Daoism was founded by [Laozi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laozi "Laozi") (6th century BCE) and examined how humans can live in harmony with nature by following the [Dao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dao "Dao") or the natural order of the universe. Other influential early schools of thought were [Mohism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohism "Mohism"), which developed an early form of altruistic [consequentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism "Consequentialism"), and [Legalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legalism_(Chinese_philosophy) "Legalism (Chinese philosophy)"), which emphasized the importance of a strong state and strict laws.

Buddhism was introduced to China in the 1st century CE and diversified into [new forms of Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism "Chinese Buddhism"). Starting in the 3rd century CE, the school of [Xuanxue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanxue "Xuanxue") emerged. It interpreted earlier Daoist works with a specific emphasis on metaphysical explanations. [Neo-Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Confucianism "Neo-Confucianism") developed in the 11th century CE. It systematized previous Confucian teachings and sought a metaphysical foundation of ethics. The modern period in Chinese philosophy began in the early 20th century and was shaped by the influence of and reactions to Western philosophy. The emergence of [Chinese Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Marxism "Chinese Marxism")—which focused on [class struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_struggle "Class struggle"), [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism "Socialism"), and [communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism "Communism")—resulted in a significant transformation of the political landscape. Another development was the emergence of [New Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Confucianism "New Confucianism"), which aims to modernize and rethink Confucian teachings to explore their compatibility with democratic ideals and modern science.

### Other traditions

Traditional Japanese philosophy assimilated and synthesized ideas from different traditions, including the indigenous [Shinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto "Shinto") religion and Chinese and Indian thought in the forms of Confucianism and Buddhism, both of which entered Japan in the 6th and 7th centuries. Its practice is characterized by active interaction with reality rather than disengaged examination. Neo-Confucianism became an influential school of thought in the 16th century and the following [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period "Edo period") and prompted a greater focus on language and the natural world. The [Kyoto School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_School "Kyoto School") emerged in the 20th century and integrated Eastern spirituality with Western philosophy in its exploration of concepts like absolute nothingness (*zettai-mu*), place (*basho*), and the [self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self "Self").

Latin American philosophy in the [pre-colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era "Pre-Columbian era") was practiced by indigenous civilizations and explored questions concerning the nature of reality and the role of humans. It has similarities to [indigenous North American philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_American_philosophy "Indigenous American philosophy"), which covered themes such as the interconnectedness of all things. Latin American philosophy during the [colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas "European colonization of the Americas"), starting around 1550, was dominated by religious philosophy in the form of [scholasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism"). Influential topics in the post-colonial period were [positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivism "Positivism"), the [philosophy of liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_liberation "Philosophy of liberation"), and the exploration of identity and culture.

Early [African philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_philosophy "African philosophy") was primarily conducted and transmitted orally. It focused on community, morality, and ancestral ideas, encompassing folklore, wise sayings, religious ideas, and philosophical concepts like [Ubuntu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubuntu_philosophy "Ubuntu philosophy"). Systematic African philosophy emerged at the beginning of the 20th century. It discusses topics such as [ethnophilosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnophilosophy "Ethnophilosophy"), [négritude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A9gritude "Négritude"), [pan-Africanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Africanism "Pan-Africanism"), Marxism, [postcolonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postcolonialism "Postcolonialism"), the role of cultural identity, [relativism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativism "Relativism"), [African epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_philosophy#African_epistemology "African philosophy"), and the critique of [Eurocentrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism "Eurocentrism").

## Core branches

Philosophical questions can be grouped into several branches. These groupings allow philosophers to focus on a set of similar topics and interact with other thinkers who are interested in the same questions. Epistemology, ethics, logic, and metaphysics are sometimes listed as the main branches. There are many other subfields besides them, and the different divisions are neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive. For example, political philosophy, ethics, and [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics") are sometimes linked under the general heading of [value theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_theory "Value theory") as they investigate [normative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normative "Normative") or evaluative aspects. Furthermore, philosophical inquiry sometimes overlaps with other disciplines in the natural and social sciences, religion, and mathematics.

### Epistemology

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies knowledge. It is also known as the *theory of knowledge* and aims to understand what knowledge is, how it arises, what its limits are, and what value it has. It further examines the nature of [truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth "Truth"), [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief "Belief"), [justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justification_(epistemology) "Justification (epistemology)"), and [rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason"). Some of the questions addressed by epistemologists include "By what method(s) can one acquire knowledge?"; "How is truth established?"; and "Can we prove causal relations?"

Epistemology is primarily interested in [declarative knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declarative_knowledge "Declarative knowledge") or knowledge of facts, like knowing that Princess Diana died in 1997. But it also investigates [practical knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practical_knowledge "Practical knowledge"), such as knowing how to ride a bicycle, and [knowledge by acquaintance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_by_acquaintance "Knowledge by acquaintance"), for example, knowing a celebrity personally.

One area in epistemology is the *[analysis of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_knowledge "Analysis of knowledge")*. It assumes that declarative knowledge is a combination of different parts and attempts to identify what those parts are. An influential theory in this area claims that knowledge has three components: it is a *belief* that is *justified* and *true*. This theory is controversial, and the difficulties associated with it are known as the [Gettier problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettier_problem "Gettier problem"). Alternative views state that knowledge requires additional components, like the absence of luck; different components, like the manifestation of [cognitive virtues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic_virtue "Epistemic virtue") instead of justification; or they deny that knowledge can be analyzed in terms of other phenomena.

Another area in epistemology asks how people acquire knowledge. Often-discussed sources of knowledge are [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception "Perception"), [introspection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introspection "Introspection"), [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory "Memory"), [inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference "Inference"), and [testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimony "Testimony"). According to [empiricists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricists "Empiricists"), all knowledge is based on some form of experience. Rationalists reject this view and hold that some forms of knowledge, like [innate knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innate_knowledge "Innate knowledge"), are not acquired through experience. The [regress problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regress_problem "Regress problem") is a common issue in relation to the sources of knowledge and the justification they offer. It is based on the idea that beliefs require some kind of reason or evidence to be justified. The problem is that the source of justification may itself be in need of another source of justification. This leads to an [infinite regress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_regress "Infinite regress") or [circular reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_reasoning "Circular reasoning"). [Foundationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundationalist "Foundationalist") avoid this conclusion by arguing that some sources can provide justification without requiring justification themselves. Another solution is presented by [coherentists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherentist "Coherentist"), who state that a belief is justified if it coheres with other beliefs of the person.

Many discussions in epistemology touch on the topic of [philosophical skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_skepticism "Philosophical skepticism"), which raises doubts about some or all claims to knowledge. These doubts are often based on the idea that knowledge requires absolute certainty and that humans are unable to acquire it.

### Ethics

Ethics, also known as moral philosophy, studies what constitutes right [conduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_(philosophy) "Action (philosophy)"). It is also concerned with the moral [evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Values_(philosophy) "Values (philosophy)") of character traits and institutions. It explores what the standards of [morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality "Morality") are and how to live a good life. Philosophical ethics addresses such basic questions as "Are moral obligations relative?", "Which has priority: well-being or obligation?", and "What gives life meaning?"

The main branches of ethics are [metaethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-ethics "Meta-ethics"), [normative ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normative_ethics "Normative ethics"), and [applied ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_ethics "Applied ethics"). Metaethics asks abstract questions about the nature and sources of morality. It analyzes the meaning of ethical concepts, like *right action* and *[obligation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obligation "Obligation")*. It also investigates whether ethical theories can be [true in an absolute sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_relativism "Moral relativism") and how to acquire knowledge of them. Normative ethics encompasses general theories of how to distinguish between right and wrong conduct. It helps guide moral decisions by examining what moral obligations and rights people have. Applied ethics studies the consequences of the general theories developed by normative ethics in specific situations, for example, in the workplace or for medical treatments.

Within contemporary normative ethics, consequentialism, [deontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontology "Deontology"), and [virtue ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_ethics "Virtue ethics") are influential schools of thought. *Consequentialists* judge actions based on their consequences. One such view is [utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism "Utilitarianism"), which argues that actions should increase overall happiness while minimizing suffering. *Deontologists* judge actions based on whether they follow moral duties, such as abstaining from lying or killing. According to them, what matters is that actions are in tune with those duties and not what consequences they have. *Virtue theorists* judge actions based on how the moral character of the agent is expressed. According to this view, actions should conform to what an ideally virtuous agent would do by manifesting virtues like [generosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generosity "Generosity") and [honesty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honesty "Honesty").

### Logic

Logic is the study of [correct reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_reasoning "Logical reasoning"). It aims to understand how to distinguish good from bad [arguments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument "Argument"). It is usually divided into formal and [informal logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informal_logic "Informal logic"). Formal logic uses [artificial languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language "Formal language") with a precise symbolic representation to investigate arguments. In its search for exact criteria, it examines the structure of arguments to determine whether they are correct or incorrect. Informal logic uses non-formal criteria and standards to assess the correctness of arguments. It relies on additional factors such as content and context.

Logic examines a variety of arguments. [Deductive arguments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_arguments "Deductive arguments") are mainly studied by formal logic. An argument is deductively [valid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Validity_(logic) "Validity (logic)") if the truth of its [premises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premise "Premise") ensures the truth of its conclusion. Deductively valid arguments follow a [rule of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_inference "Rule of inference"), like *[modus ponens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modus_ponens "Modus ponens")*, which has the following [logical form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_form "Logical form"): "p; if p then q; therefore q". An example is the argument "today is Sunday; if today is Sunday then I don't have to go to work today; therefore I don't have to go to work today".

The premises of non-deductive arguments also support their conclusion, although this support does not guarantee that the conclusion is true. One form is [inductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning"). It starts from a set of individual cases and uses generalization to arrive at a universal law governing all cases. An example is the inference that "all ravens are black" based on observations of many individual black ravens. Another form is [abductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abductive_reasoning "Abductive reasoning"). It starts from an observation and concludes that the best explanation of this observation must be true. This happens, for example, when a doctor diagnoses a disease based on the observed symptoms.

Logic also investigates incorrect forms of reasoning. They are called *[fallacies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacies "Fallacies")* and are divided into [formal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_fallacy "Formal fallacy") and [informal fallacies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informal_fallacies "Informal fallacies") based on whether the source of the error lies only in the form of the argument or also in its content and context.

### Metaphysics

Metaphysics is the study of the most general features of [reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality "Reality"), such as existence, [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_(philosophy) "Object (philosophy)") and their [properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_(philosophy) "Property (philosophy)"), [wholes and their parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mereology "Mereology"), [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space "Space") and [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time "Time"), [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_(philosophy) "Event (philosophy)"), and [causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality"). There are disagreements about the precise definition of the term, and its meaning has changed throughout the ages. Metaphysicians attempt to answer basic questions including "[Why is there something rather than nothing?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_is_there_something_rather_than_nothing%3F "Why is there something rather than nothing?")", "Of what does reality ultimately consist?", and "Are humans free?"

Metaphysics is sometimes divided into general metaphysics and specific or special metaphysics. General metaphysics investigates being as such. It examines the features that all entities have in common. Specific metaphysics is interested in different kinds of being, the features they have, and how they differ from one another.

An important area in metaphysics is [ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology "Ontology"). Some theorists identify it with general metaphysics. Ontology investigates concepts like [being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being "Being"), [becoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Becoming_(philosophy) "Becoming (philosophy)"), and reality. It studies the [categories of being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categories_of_being "Categories of being") and asks what exists on the most fundamental level. Another subfield of metaphysics is [philosophical cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_cosmology "Philosophical cosmology"). It is interested in the essence of the world as a whole. It asks questions including whether the universe has a beginning and an end and whether it was created by something else.

A key topic in metaphysics concerns the question of whether reality only consists of physical things like matter and energy. Alternative suggestions are that mental entities (such as [souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul "Soul") and [experiences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience "Experience")) and [abstract entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete "Abstract and concrete") (such as numbers) exist apart from physical things. Another topic in metaphysics concerns the problem of [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_(philosophy) "Identity (philosophy)"). One question is how much an entity can change while still remaining the same entity. According to one view, entities have [essential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essence "Essence") and [accidental features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accident_(philosophy) "Accident (philosophy)"). They can change their accidental features, but they cease to be the same entity if they lose an essential feature. A central distinction in metaphysics is between [particulars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particular "Particular") and [universals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universals "Universals"). Universals, like the color red, can exist at different locations at the same time. This is not the case for particulars, including individual persons or specific objects. Other metaphysical questions are whether the past [fully determines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") the present and what implications this would have for the existence of [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will "Free will").

### Other major branches

There are many other subfields of philosophy besides its core branches. Some of the most prominent are aesthetics, philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, philosophy of religion, philosophy of science, and political philosophy.

[Aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics") in the philosophical sense is the field that studies the nature and appreciation of [beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beauty "Beauty") and other aesthetic properties, like [the sublime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_sublime "The sublime"). Although it is often treated together with the [philosophy of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics#Aesthetics_and_the_philosophy_of_art "Aesthetics"), aesthetics is a broader category that encompasses other aspects of experience, such as natural beauty. In a more general sense, aesthetics is "critical reflection on art, culture, and [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature "Nature")". A key question in aesthetics is whether beauty is an objective feature of entities or a subjective aspect of experience. Aesthetic philosophers also investigate the nature of aesthetic experiences and [judgments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic_judgment "Aesthetic judgment"). Further topics include the essence of [works of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Works_of_art "Works of art") and the processes involved in creating them.

The [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language "Philosophy of language") studies the nature and function of [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language "Language"). It examines the concepts of [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_(philosophy) "Meaning (philosophy)"), [reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference "Reference"), and truth. It aims to answer questions such as how words are related to things and how language affects human [thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought "Thought") and understanding. It is closely related to the disciplines of logic and linguistics. The philosophy of language rose to particular prominence in the early 20th century in [analytic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy "Analytic philosophy") due to the works of [Frege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frege "Frege") and Russell. One of its central topics is to understand how sentences get their meaning. There are two broad theoretical camps: those emphasizing the formal [truth conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_conditions "Truth conditions") of sentences and those investigating circumstances that determine when it is suitable to use a sentence, the latter of which is associated with [speech act theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_act_theory "Speech act theory").

The [philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind "Philosophy of mind") studies the nature of mental phenomena and how they are related to the physical world. It aims to understand different types of [conscious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscious "Conscious") and [unconscious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconscious_mind "Unconscious mind") [mental states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_states "Mental states"), like [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief "Belief"), [desires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desire "Desire"), [intentions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intention "Intention"), [feelings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feeling "Feeling"), [sensations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense "Sense"), and free will. An influential intuition in the philosophy of mind is that there is a distinction between the inner experience of objects and their existence in the external world. The [mind-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind-body_problem "Mind-body problem") is the problem of explaining how these two types of things—mind and matter—are related. The main traditional responses are [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism "Materialism"), which assumes that matter is more fundamental; [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism "Idealism"), which assumes that mind is more fundamental; and [dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind%E2%80%93body_dualism "Mind–body dualism"), which assumes that mind and matter are distinct types of entities. In contemporary philosophy, another common view is [functionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionalism_(philosophy_of_mind) "Functionalism (philosophy of mind)"), which understands mental states in terms of the functional or causal roles they play. The mind-body problem is closely related to the [hard problem of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_problem_of_consciousness "Hard problem of consciousness"), which asks how the physical brain can produce [qualitatively subjective experiences.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualia "Qualia")

The [philosophy of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion "Philosophy of religion") investigates the basic concepts, assumptions, and arguments associated with [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion"). It critically reflects on what religion is, how to define the [divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine "Divine"), and whether one or more gods exist. It also includes the discussion of [worldviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldview "Worldview") that reject religious doctrines. Further questions addressed by the philosophy of religion are: "How are we to interpret religious language, if not literally?"; "Is divine omniscience compatible with free will?"; and "Are the great variety of world religions in some way compatible in spite of their apparently contradictory theological claims?" It includes topics from nearly all branches of philosophy. It differs from [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") since theological debates typically take place within one religious tradition, whereas debates in the philosophy of religion transcend any particular set of theological assumptions.

The [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science") examines the fundamental concepts, assumptions, and problems associated with science. It reflects on what science is and how to distinguish it from [pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience"). It investigates the methods employed by scientists, how their application can result in knowledge, and on what assumptions they are based. It also studies the purpose and implications of science. Some of its questions are "What counts as an adequate explanation?", "Is a scientific law anything more than a description of a regularity?", and "Can some special sciences be explained entirely in the terms of a more general science?" It is a vast field that is commonly divided into the philosophy of the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences "Natural sciences") and the philosophy of the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences "Social sciences"), with further subdivisions for each of the individual sciences under these headings. How these branches are related to one another is also a question in the philosophy of science. Many of its philosophical issues overlap with the fields of metaphysics or epistemology.

[Political philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy "Political philosophy") is a branch of philosophy that aims to seek knowledge about the essence of politics. It examines the basic concepts, assumptions, and arguments in the field of [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics "Politics"). It investigates the nature and purpose of [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government "Government") and compares its different forms. It further asks under what circumstances the use of political power is [legitimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_legitimacy "Political legitimacy"), rather than a form of simple violence. In this regard, it is concerned with the distribution of political power, social and material goods, and [legal rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_rights "Legal rights"). Other topics are [justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice "Justice"), [liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty "Liberty"), [equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_equality "Social equality"), [sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereignty "Sovereignty"), and [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism"). Political philosophy involves a general inquiry into normative matters and differs in this respect from [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science "Political science"), which aims to provide empirical descriptions of actually existing states. Political philosophy is often treated as a subfield of ethics. Influential schools of thought in political philosophy are [liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism "Liberalism"), [conservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism "Conservatism"), [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism "Socialism"), and [anarchism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism "Anarchism").

## Methods

Methods of philosophy are ways of conducting philosophical inquiry. They include techniques for arriving at philosophical knowledge and justifying philosophical claims as well as principles used for choosing between competing theories. A great variety of methods have been employed throughout the history of philosophy. Many of them differ significantly from the methods used in the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences "Natural sciences") in that they do not use experimental data obtained through measuring equipment. The choice of one's method usually has important implications both for how philosophical theories are constructed and for the arguments cited for or against them. This choice is often guided by epistemological considerations about what constitutes philosophical [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence "Evidence").

[Methodological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodological "Methodological") disagreements can cause conflicts among philosophical theories or about the answers to philosophical questions. The discovery of new methods has often had important consequences both for how philosophers conduct their research and for what claims they defend. Some philosophers engage in most of their theorizing using one particular method while others employ a wider range of methods based on which one fits the specific problem investigated best.

[Conceptual analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_analysis "Conceptual analysis") is a common method in [analytic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy "Analytic philosophy"). It aims to clarify the meaning of concepts by analyzing them into their component parts. Another method often employed in analytic philosophy is based on [common sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_sense "Common sense"). It starts with commonly accepted beliefs and tries to draw unexpected conclusions from them, which it often employs in a negative sense to criticize philosophical theories that are too far removed from how the average person sees the issue. It is similar to how [ordinary language philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_language_philosophy "Ordinary language philosophy") approaches philosophical questions by investigating how ordinary language is used.

Various methods in philosophy give particular importance to [intuitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition "Intuition"), that is, non-inferential impressions about the correctness of specific claims or general principles. For example, they play an important role in [thought experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_experiment "Thought experiment"), which employ [counterfactual thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfactual_thinking "Counterfactual thinking") to evaluate the possible consequences of an imagined situation. These anticipated consequences can then be used to confirm or refute philosophical theories. The method of [reflective equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_equilibrium "Reflective equilibrium") also employs intuitions. It seeks to form a [coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherentism "Coherentism") position on a certain issue by examining all the relevant beliefs and intuitions, some of which often have to be deemphasized or reformulated to arrive at a coherent perspective.

[Pragmatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatism "Pragmatism") stress the significance of concrete practical consequences for assessing whether a philosophical theory is true. According to the [pragmatic maxim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatic_maxim "Pragmatic maxim") as formulated by [Charles Sanders Peirce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Sanders_Peirce "Charles Sanders Peirce"), the idea a person has of an object is nothing more than the totality of practical consequences they associate with this object. Pragmatists have also used this method to expose disagreements as merely verbal, that is, to show they make no genuine difference on the level of consequences.

[Phenomenologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_(philosophy) "Phenomenology (philosophy)") seek knowledge of the realm of appearance and the structure of human experience. They insist upon the first-person character of all experience and proceed by suspending theoretical judgments about the external world. This technique of phenomenological reduction is known as "bracketing" or [epoché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracketing_(phenomenology) "Bracketing (phenomenology)"). The goal is to give an unbiased description of the appearance of things.

[Methodological naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodological_naturalism "Methodological naturalism") places great emphasis on the empirical approach and the resulting theories found in the natural sciences. In this way, it contrasts with methodologies that give more weight to pure reasoning and introspection.

## Relation to other fields

Philosophy is closely related to many other fields. It is sometimes understood as a [meta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta_(prefix)#Epistemology "Meta (prefix)")-discipline that clarifies the nature and limits of other disciplines. It does this by critically examining their basic concepts, background assumptions, and methods. In this regard, it plays a key role in providing an [interdisciplinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary "Interdisciplinary") perspective. It bridges the gap between different disciplines by analyzing which concepts and problems they have in common. It shows how they overlap while also delimiting their scope. Historically, most of the individual sciences originated from philosophy.

The influence of philosophy is felt in several fields that require difficult practical decisions. In [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"), philosophical considerations related to [bioethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioethics "Bioethics") affect issues like whether an [embryo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryo "Embryo") is already a [person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personhood "Personhood") and under what conditions [abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion "Abortion") is morally permissible. A closely related philosophical problem is how humans should treat other animals, for instance, whether it is acceptable to use non-human animals as food or for [research experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_experiments "Animal experiments"). In relation to [business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business "Business") and professional life, philosophy has contributed by providing ethical frameworks. They contain guidelines on which business practices are morally acceptable and cover the issue of [corporate social responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_social_responsibility "Corporate social responsibility").

Philosophical inquiry is relevant to many fields that are concerned with what to believe and how to arrive at evidence for one's beliefs. This is a key issue for the sciences, which have as one of their prime objectives the creation of scientific knowledge. Scientific knowledge is based on [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence "Empirical evidence"), but it is often not clear whether empirical observations are neutral or already [include theoretical assumptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory-ladenness "Theory-ladenness"). A closely connected problem is whether the available [evidence is sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdetermination "Underdetermination") to decide between competing theories. Epistemological problems in relation to the [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law "Law") include what counts as evidence and how much evidence is required to find a person [guilty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilt_(law) "Guilt (law)") of a crime. A related issue in [journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism "Journalism") is how to ensure truth and [objectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalistic_objectivity "Journalistic objectivity") when reporting on events.

In the fields of [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") and religion, there are many doctrines associated with the existence and nature of God as well as rules governing correct behavior. A key issue is whether a rational person should believe these doctrines, for example, whether [revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation "Revelation") in the form of holy books and [religious experiences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_experience "Religious experience") of the divine are sufficient evidence for these beliefs.

Philosophy in the form of logic has been influential in the fields of mathematics and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science "Computer science"). Further fields influenced by philosophy include [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology"), [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology "Sociology"), linguistics, [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education "Education"), and [the arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_arts "The arts"). The close relation between philosophy and other fields in the contemporary period is reflected in the fact that many philosophy graduates go on to work in related fields rather than in philosophy itself.

In the field of politics, philosophy addresses issues such as how to assess whether a government policy is just. Philosophical ideas have prepared and shaped various political developments. For example, ideals formulated in [Enlightenment philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlightenment_philosophy "Enlightenment philosophy") laid the foundation for [constitutional democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_democracy "Constitutional democracy") and played a role in the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution "American Revolution") and the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution"). Marxist philosophy, with its exposition of communism, was one of the factors in the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution "Russian Revolution") and the [Chinese Communist Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Revolution "Chinese Communist Revolution"). In India, [Mahatma Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi "Mahatma Gandhi")'s [philosophy of non-violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyagraha#Ahimsa_and_satyagraha "Satyagraha") shaped the [Indian independence movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_independence_movement "Indian independence movement").

An example of the cultural and critical role of philosophy is found in its influence on the [feminist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist "Feminist") movement through philosophers such as [Mary Wollstonecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Wollstonecraft "Mary Wollstonecraft"), [Simone de Beauvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_de_Beauvoir "Simone de Beauvoir"), and [Judith Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Butler "Judith Butler"). It has shaped the understanding of key concepts in feminism, for instance, the meaning of [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender "Gender"), how it differs from [biological sex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_sex "Biological sex"), and what role it plays in the formation of [personal identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identity "Personal identity"). Philosophers have also investigated the concepts of justice and [equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_equality "Social equality") and their implications with respect to the [prejudicial treatment of women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexism "Sexism") in [male-dominated societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchy "Patriarchy").

The idea that philosophy is useful for many aspects of life and society is sometimes rejected. According to some academics, philosophy is mainly undertaken for its own sake and does not make significant contributions to existing practices or external goals.
