# History of mathematics

The **history of mathematics** deals with the origin of discoveries in [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") and the [mathematical methods and notation of the past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_mathematical_notation "History of mathematical notation"). Before the [modern age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_age "Modern age") and worldwide spread of knowledge, written examples of new mathematical developments have come to light only in a few locales. From 3000 BC the [Mesopotamian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") states of [Sumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer"), [Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_(region) "Akkad (region)") and [Assyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria"), followed closely by [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and the Levantine state of [Ebla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebla "Ebla") began using [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic"), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") and [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") for [taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation "Taxation"), [commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commerce "Commerce"), trade, and in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), to record time and formulate [calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars "Calendars").

The earliest mathematical texts available are from Mesopotamia and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") – *[Plimpton 322](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plimpton_322 "Plimpton 322")* ([Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics "Babylonian mathematics") c. 2000 – 1900 BC), the *[Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhind_Mathematical_Papyrus "Rhind Mathematical Papyrus")* ([Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics "Egyptian mathematics") c. 1800 BC) and the *[Moscow Mathematical Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Mathematical_Papyrus "Moscow Mathematical Papyrus")* (Egyptian c. 1890 BC). All these texts mention the so-called [Pythagorean triples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple "Pythagorean triple"), so, by inference, the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem") seems to be the most ancient and widespread mathematical development, after basic arithmetic and geometry.

The study of mathematics as a "demonstrative discipline" began in the 6th century BC with the [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans "Pythagoreans"), who coined the term "mathematics" from the ancient [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") *μάθημα* (*mathema*), meaning "subject of instruction". [Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics "Greek mathematics") greatly refined the methods (especially through the introduction of deductive reasoning and [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor "Mathematical rigor") in [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof "Mathematical proof")) and expanded the subject matter of mathematics. The [ancient Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Romans "Ancient Romans") used [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics") in [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying "Surveying"), [structural engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineering "Structural engineering"), [mechanical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineering "Mechanical engineering"), [bookkeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookkeeping "Bookkeeping"), creation of [lunar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_calendar "Lunar calendar") and [solar calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_calendar "Solar calendar"), and even [arts and crafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_art "Roman art"). [Chinese mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_mathematics "Chinese mathematics") made early contributions, including a [place value system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_value_system "Place value system") and the first use of [negative numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_numbers "Negative numbers"). The [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system "Hindu–Arabic numeral system") and the rules for the use of its operations, in use throughout the world today, evolved over the course of the first millennium AD in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematics "Indian mathematics") and were transmitted to the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world") via [Islamic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics "Islamic mathematics") through the work of [Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khw%C4%81rizm%C4%AB "Khwārizmī"). Islamic mathematics, in turn, developed and expanded the mathematics known to these civilizations. Contemporaneous with but independent of these traditions were the mathematics developed by the [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") of [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico") and [Central America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_America "Central America"), where the concept of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero "Zero") was given a standard symbol in [Maya numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_numerals "Maya numerals").
Many Greek and Arabic texts on mathematics were [translated into Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century "Latin translations of the 12th century") from the 12th century, leading to further development of mathematics in [Medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"). From ancient times through the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postclassical_age "Postclassical age"), periods of mathematical discovery were often followed by centuries of stagnation. Beginning in [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") Italy in the 15th century, new mathematical developments, interacting with new scientific discoveries, were made at an [increasing pace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_growth "Exponential growth") that continues through the present day. This includes the groundbreaking work of both [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") and [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz") in the development of infinitesimal [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") during the 17th century and subsequent discoveries of [German mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_German_mathematicians "List of German mathematicians") like [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") and [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert").

## Prehistoric

The origins of mathematical thought lie in the concepts of [number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number "Number"), [patterns in nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patterns_in_nature "Patterns in nature"), [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_(mathematics) "Magnitude (mathematics)"), and [form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Configuration_(geometry) "Configuration (geometry)"). Modern studies of animal cognition have shown that these concepts are not unique to humans. Such concepts would have been part of everyday life in [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") societies. The idea of the "number" concept evolving gradually over time is supported by the existence of languages that preserve the distinction between "one", "two", and "many", but not of numbers larger than two.

The use of yarn by [Neanderthals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neanderthal "Neanderthal") some 40,000 years ago at a site in Abri du Maras in the south of France suggests they knew basic concepts in mathematics. The [Ishango bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishango_bone "Ishango bone"), found near the headwaters of the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile") river (northeastern [Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo "Democratic Republic of the Congo")), may be more than [20,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic "Upper Paleolithic") years old and consists of a series of marks carved in three columns running the length of the bone. Common interpretations are that the Ishango bone shows either a *tally* of the earliest known demonstration of [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence "Sequence") of [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number "Prime number") or a six-month lunar calendar. Peter Rudman argues that the development of the concept of prime numbers could only have come about after the concept of division, which he dates to after 10,000 BC, with prime numbers probably not being understood until about 500 BC. He also writes that "no attempt has been made to explain why a tally of something should exhibit multiples of two, prime numbers between 10 and 20, and some numbers that are almost multiples of 10." The Ishango bone, according to scholar [Alexander Marshack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Marshack "Alexander Marshack"), may have influenced the later development of mathematics in Egypt as, like some entries on the Ishango bone, Egyptian arithmetic also made use of multiplication by 2; this however, is disputed.

[Predynastic Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predynastic_Egypt "Predynastic Egypt") of the 5th millennium BC pictorially represented geometric designs. It has been claimed that [megalithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalith "Megalith") monuments in [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England") and [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland "Scotland"), dating from the 3rd millennium BC, incorporate geometric ideas such as [circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle "Circle"), [ellipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse "Ellipse"), and [Pythagorean triples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple "Pythagorean triple") in their design. All of the above are disputed, however, and the currently oldest undisputed mathematical documents are from Babylonian and dynastic Egyptian sources.

## Babylonian

[Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia "Babylonia") mathematics refers to any mathematics of the peoples of [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") (modern [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq")) from the days of the early [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") through the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"), almost to the dawn of [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"). The majority of Babylonian mathematical work comes from two widely separated periods: The first few hundred years of the second millennium BC (Old Babylonian period) and the last few centuries of the first millennium BC ([Seleucid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid "Seleucid") period). It is named Babylonian mathematics due to the central role of [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon") as a place of study.

In contrast to the sparsity of sources in [Egyptian mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics "Egyptian mathematics"), knowledge of Babylonian mathematics is derived from more than 400 clay tablets unearthed since the 1850s. Written in [Cuneiform script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform_script "Cuneiform script"), tablets were inscribed whilst the clay was moist, and baked hard in an oven or by the heat of the sun. Some of these appear to be graded homework.

The earliest evidence of written mathematics dates back to the ancient [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer"), who built the earliest civilization in Mesopotamia. They developed a complex system of [metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrology "Metrology") from 3000 BC that was chiefly concerned with administrative/financial counting, such as grain allotments, workers, weights of silver, or even liquids, among other things. From around 2500 BC onward, the Sumerians wrote [multiplication tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_table "Multiplication table") on clay tablets and dealt with geometrical exercises and [division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_(mathematics) "Division (mathematics)") problems. The earliest traces of the Babylonian numerals also date back to this period.

Babylonian mathematics was written using a [sexagesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal "Sexagesimal") (base-60) [numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_system "Numeral system"). From this derives the modern-day usage of 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, and 360 (60 × 6) degrees in a circle, as well as the use of seconds and minutes of arc to denote fractions of a degree.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] It is thought the sexagesimal system was initially used by Sumerian scribes because 60 can be evenly divided by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, and for scribes (doling out the aforementioned grain allotments, recording weights of silver, etc.) being able to easily calculate by hand was essential, and so a sexagesimal system is pragmatically easier to calculate by hand with; however, there is the possibility that using a sexagesimal system was an ethno-linguistic phenomenon (that might not ever be known), and not a mathematical/practical decision. Also, unlike the Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans, the Babylonians had most of a place-value system, where digits written in the left column represented larger values, much as in the [decimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal "Decimal") system. The power of the Babylonian notational system lay in that it could be used to represent fractions as easily as whole numbers; thus, multiplying two numbers that contained fractions was no different from multiplying integers, similar to modern notation. The notational system of the Babylonians was the best of any civilization until the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance"), and its power allowed it to achieve remarkable computational accuracy; for example, the Babylonian tablet [YBC 7289](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YBC_7289 "YBC 7289") gives an approximation of √2 accurate to five decimal places. The Babylonians lacked, however, an equivalent of the decimal point, and so the place value of a symbol often had to be inferred from the context. By the Seleucid period, the Babylonians had developed a zero symbol as a placeholder for empty positions; however, it was only used for intermediate positions. This zero sign does not appear in terminal positions; thus, the Babylonians came close but did not develop a true place value system.

Other topics covered by Babylonian mathematics include fractions, algebra, quadratic and cubic equations, and the calculation of [regular numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_number "Regular number") and their [reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_inverse "Multiplicative inverse") [pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuple "Tuple"). The tablets also include multiplication tables and methods for solving [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation "Linear equation"), [quadratic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_equation "Quadratic equation"), and [cubic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_equation "Cubic equation"), a remarkable achievement for the time. Tablets from the Old Babylonian period also contain the earliest known statement of the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem"). However, as with Egyptian mathematics, Babylonian mathematics shows no awareness of the difference between exact and approximate solutions, or the solvability of a problem, and most importantly, no explicit statement of the need for [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof "Mathematical proof") or logical principles.

## Egyptian

[Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") mathematics refers to mathematics written in the [Egyptian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_language "Egyptian language"). From the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") replaced Egyptian as the written language of [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians "Egyptians") scholars. Archaeological evidence has suggested that the Ancient Egyptian counting system had origins in [Sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa"). Also, fractal geometry designs which are widespread among Sub-Saharan African cultures are also found in Egyptian architecture and cosmological signs. Megalithic structures located in [Nabta Playa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabta_Playa "Nabta Playa"), Upper Egypt featured [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), calendar arrangements in alignment with the heliacal rising of [Sirius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirius "Sirius") and supported calibration the yearly calendar for the annual Nile flood.

The most extensive Egyptian mathematical text is the [Rhind papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhind_papyrus "Rhind papyrus") (sometimes also called the Ahmes Papyrus after its author), dated to c. 1650 BC but likely a copy of an older document from the [Middle Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt "Middle Kingdom of Egypt") of about 2000–1800 BC. It is an instruction manual for students in arithmetic and geometry. In addition to giving area formulas and methods for multiplication, division and working with unit fractions, it also contains evidence of other mathematical knowledge, including [composite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_number "Composite number") and [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number "Prime number"); [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_mean "Arithmetic mean"), [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_mean "Geometric mean") and [harmonic means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_mean "Harmonic mean"); and simplistic understandings of both the [Sieve of Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sieve_of_Eratosthenes "Sieve of Eratosthenes") and [perfect number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_number "Perfect number") (namely, that of the number 6). It also shows how to solve first order [linear equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation "Linear equation") as well as [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_series "Arithmetic series") and [geometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series "Geometric series").

Another significant Egyptian mathematical text is the [Moscow papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_papyrus "Moscow papyrus"), also from the [Middle Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt "Middle Kingdom of Egypt") period, dated to c. 1890 BC. It consists of what are today called *word problems* or *story problems*, which were apparently intended as entertainment. One problem is considered to be of particular importance because it gives a method for finding the volume of a [frustum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frustum "Frustum") (truncated pyramid).

Finally, the [Berlin Papyrus 6619](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Papyrus_6619 "Berlin Papyrus 6619") (c. 1800 BC) shows that ancient Egyptians could solve a second-order [algebraic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_equation "Algebraic equation").

## Greek

Greek mathematics refers to the mathematics written in the [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") from the time of [Thales of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus "Thales of Miletus") (~600 BC) to the closure of the [Academy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy "Platonic Academy") in 529 AD. Greek mathematicians lived in cities spread over the entire Eastern Mediterranean, from Italy to North Africa, but were united by culture and language. Greek mathematics of the period following [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") is sometimes called [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period") mathematics.

Greek mathematics was much more sophisticated than the mathematics that had been developed by earlier cultures. All surviving records of pre-Greek mathematics show the use of [inductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning"), that is, repeated observations used to establish rules of thumb. Greek mathematicians, by contrast, used [deductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_reasoning "Deductive reasoning"). The Greeks used logic to derive conclusions from definitions and axioms, and used [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor "Mathematical rigor") to prove them.

Greek mathematics is thought to have begun with [Thales of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus "Thales of Miletus") (c. 624 – c. 546 BC) and [Pythagoras of Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras_of_Samos "Pythagoras of Samos") (c. 582 – c. 507 BC). Although the extent of the influence is disputed, they were probably inspired by [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics "Egyptian mathematics") and [Babylonian mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics "Babylonian mathematics"). According to legend, Pythagoras traveled to Egypt to learn mathematics, geometry, and astronomy from Egyptian priests.

Thales used [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") to solve problems such as calculating the height of [pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramids "Pyramids") and the distance of ships from the shore. He is credited with the first use of deductive reasoning applied to geometry, by deriving four corollaries to [Thales' Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales%27_Theorem "Thales' Theorem"). As a result, he has been hailed as the first true mathematician and the first known individual to whom a mathematical discovery has been attributed. Pythagoras established the [Pythagorean School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans "Pythagoreans"), whose doctrine it was that mathematics ruled the universe and whose motto was "All is number". It was the Pythagoreans who coined the term "mathematics", and with whom the study of mathematics for its own sake begins. The Pythagoreans are credited with the first proof of the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem"), though the statement of the theorem has a long history, and with the proof of the existence of [irrational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_numbers "Irrational numbers"). Although he was preceded by the [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics "Babylonian mathematics"), [Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematics "Indian mathematics") and the [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_mathematics "Chinese mathematics"), the [Neopythagorean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopythagorean "Neopythagorean") mathematician [Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus "Nicomachus") (60–120 AD) provided one of the earliest [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman "Greco-Roman") [multiplication tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_table "Multiplication table"), whereas the oldest extant Greek multiplication table is found on a wax tablet dated to the 1st century AD (now found in the [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum "British Museum")). The association of the Neopythagoreans with the Western invention of the multiplication table is evident in its later [Medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") name: the *mensa Pythagorica*.

[Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") (428/427 BC – 348/347 BC) is important in the history of mathematics for inspiring and guiding others. His [Platonic Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy "Platonic Academy"), in [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"), became the mathematical center of the world in the 4th century BC, and it was from this school that the leading mathematicians of the day, such as [Eudoxus of Cnidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudoxus_of_Cnidus "Eudoxus of Cnidus") (c. 390 – c. 340 BC), came. Plato also discussed the foundations of mathematics, clarified some of the definitions (e.g. that of a line as "breadthless length").

Eudoxus developed the [method of exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion "Method of exhaustion"), a precursor of modern [integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral "Integral") and a theory of ratios that avoided the problem of [incommensurable magnitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incommensurable_magnitudes "Incommensurable magnitudes"). The former allowed the calculations of areas and volumes of curvilinear figures, while the latter enabled subsequent geometers to make significant advances in geometry. Though he made no specific technical mathematical discoveries, [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") (384–c. 322 BC) contributed significantly to the development of mathematics by laying the foundations of [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic").

In the 3rd century BC, the premier center of mathematical education and research was the [Musaeum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musaeum "Musaeum") of [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"). It was there that [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid") (c. 300 BC) taught, and wrote the *[Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements "Euclid's Elements")*, widely considered the most successful and influential textbook of all time. The *Elements* introduced [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor "Mathematical rigor") through the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method "Axiomatic method") and is the earliest example of the format still used in mathematics today, that of definition, axiom, theorem, and proof. Although most of the contents of the *Elements* were already known, Euclid arranged them into a single, coherent logical framework. The *Elements* was known to all educated people in the West up through the middle of the 20th century and its contents are still taught in geometry classes today. In addition to the familiar theorems of [Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry "Euclidean geometry"), the *Elements* was meant as an introductory textbook to all mathematical subjects of the time, such as [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") and [solid geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_geometry "Solid geometry"), including proofs that the square root of two is irrational and that there are infinitely many prime numbers. Euclid also [wrote extensively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid#Other_works "Euclid") on other subjects, such as [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections "Conic sections"), [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics "Optics"), [spherical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_geometry "Spherical geometry"), and mechanics, but only half of his writings survive.

[Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes") (c. 287–212 BC) of [Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse,_Italy "Syracuse, Italy"), widely considered the greatest mathematician of antiquity, used the [method of exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion "Method of exhaustion") to calculate the [area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area "Area") under the arc of a [parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola "Parabola") with the [summation of an infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_(mathematics) "Series (mathematics)"), in a manner not too dissimilar from modern calculus. He also showed one could use the method of exhaustion to calculate the value of π with as much precision as desired, and obtained the most accurate value of π then known, 3+⁠10/71⁠ < π < 3+⁠10/70⁠. He also studied the [spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes_spiral "Archimedes spiral") bearing his name, obtained formulas for the [volumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume "Volume") of [surfaces of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_of_revolution "Surface of revolution") (paraboloid, ellipsoid, hyperboloid), and an ingenious method of [exponentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponentiation "Exponentiation") for expressing very large numbers. While he is also known for his contributions to physics and several advanced mechanical devices, Archimedes himself placed far greater value on the products of his thought and general mathematical principles. He regarded as his greatest achievement his finding of the surface area and volume of a sphere, which he obtained by proving these are 2/3 the surface area and volume of a cylinder circumscribing the sphere.

[Apollonius of Perga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonius_of_Perga "Apollonius of Perga") (c. 262–190 BC) made significant advances to the study of [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections "Conic sections"), showing that one can obtain all three varieties of conic section by varying the angle of the plane that cuts a double-napped cone. He also coined the terminology in use today for conic sections, namely [parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola "Parabola") ("place beside" or "comparison"), "ellipse" ("deficiency"), and "hyperbola" ("a throw beyond"). His work *Conics* is one of the best known and preserved mathematical works from antiquity, and in it he derives many theorems concerning conic sections that would prove invaluable to later mathematicians and astronomers studying planetary motion, such as Isaac Newton. While neither Apollonius nor any other Greek mathematicians made the leap to coordinate geometry, Apollonius' treatment of curves is in some ways similar to the modern treatment, and some of his work seems to anticipate the development of analytical geometry by Descartes some 1800 years later.

Around the same time, [Eratosthenes of Cyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes_of_Cyrene "Eratosthenes of Cyrene") (c. 276–194 BC) devised the [Sieve of Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sieve_of_Eratosthenes "Sieve of Eratosthenes") for finding [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_numbers "Prime numbers"). The 3rd century BC is generally regarded as the "Golden Age" of Greek mathematics, with advances in pure mathematics henceforth in relative decline. Nevertheless, in the centuries that followed significant advances were made in applied mathematics, most notably [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry"), largely to address the needs of astronomers. [Hipparchus of Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus_of_Nicaea "Hipparchus of Nicaea") (c. 190–120 BC) is considered the founder of trigonometry for compiling the first known trigonometric table, and to him is also due the systematic use of the 360 degree circle. [Heron of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron_of_Alexandria "Heron of Alexandria") (c. 10–70 AD) is credited with [Heron's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron%27s_formula "Heron's formula") for finding the area of a scalene triangle and with being the first to recognize the possibility of negative numbers possessing square roots. [Menelaus of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus_of_Alexandria "Menelaus of Alexandria") (c. 100 AD) pioneered [spherical trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_trigonometry "Spherical trigonometry") through [Menelaus' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus%27_theorem "Menelaus' theorem"). The most complete and influential trigonometric work of antiquity is the *[Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest "Almagest")* of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Ptolemy "Claudius Ptolemy") (c. AD 90–168), a landmark astronomical treatise whose trigonometric tables would be used by astronomers for the next thousand years. Ptolemy is also credited with [Ptolemy's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy%27s_theorem "Ptolemy's theorem") for deriving trigonometric quantities, and the most accurate value of π outside of China until the medieval period, 3.1416.

Following a period of stagnation after Ptolemy, the period between 250 and 350 AD is sometimes referred to as the "Silver Age" of Greek mathematics. During this period, [Diophantus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantus "Diophantus") made significant advances in algebra, particularly [indeterminate analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_equation "Indeterminate equation"), which is also known as "Diophantine analysis". The study of [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equations "Diophantine equations") and [Diophantine approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximations "Diophantine approximations") is a significant area of research to this day. His main work was the *Arithmetica*, a collection of 150 algebraic problems dealing with exact solutions to determinate and [indeterminate equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_equation "Indeterminate equation"). The *Arithmetica* had a significant influence on later mathematicians, such as [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat"), who arrived at his famous [Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem") after trying to generalize a problem he had read in the *Arithmetica* (that of dividing a square into two squares). Diophantus also made significant advances in notation, the *Arithmetica* being the first instance of algebraic symbolism and syncopation.

Among the last great Greek mathematicians is [Pappus of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_of_Alexandria "Pappus of Alexandria") (4th century AD). He is known for his [hexagon theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus%27s_hexagon_theorem "Pappus's hexagon theorem") and [centroid theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus%27s_centroid_theorem "Pappus's centroid theorem"), as well as the [Pappus configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_configuration "Pappus configuration") and [Pappus graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_graph "Pappus graph"). His *Collection* is a major source of knowledge on Greek mathematics as most of it has survived. Pappus is considered the last major innovator in Greek mathematics, with subsequent work consisting mostly of commentaries on earlier work.

The first woman mathematician recorded was [Hypatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypatia "Hypatia") of Alexandria (AD 350–415), who wrote many works on applied mathematics. Because of a political dispute, the [Christian community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Roman_Empire "Christianity in the Roman Empire") in Alexandria had her stripped publicly and executed. Her death is sometimes taken as the end of the era of the Alexandrian Greek mathematics, although work did continue in Athens for another century with figures such as [Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus "Proclus"), [Simplicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia "Simplicius of Cilicia") and [Eutocius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutocius "Eutocius"). Although Proclus and Simplicius were more philosophers than mathematicians, their commentaries on earlier works are valuable sources on Greek mathematics. The closure of the neo-Platonic [Academy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy "Platonic Academy") by the emperor [Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian "Justinian") in 529 AD is traditionally held as marking the end of the era of Greek mathematics, although the Greek tradition continued unbroken in the [Byzantine empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_empire "Byzantine empire") with mathematicians such as [Anthemius of Tralles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthemius_of_Tralles "Anthemius of Tralles") and [Isidore of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Miletus "Isidore of Miletus"), the architects of the [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia "Hagia Sophia"). Nevertheless, Byzantine mathematics consisted mostly of commentaries, with little in the way of innovation, and the centers of mathematical innovation were to be found elsewhere by this time.

## Roman

Although [ethnic Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks "Greeks") mathematicians continued under the rule of the late [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic") and subsequent [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"), there were no noteworthy [native Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latins_(Italic_tribe) "Latins (Italic tribe)") mathematicians in comparison. [Ancient Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") such as [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero "Cicero") (106–43 BC), an influential Roman statesman who studied mathematics in Greece, believed that Roman [surveyors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveyor "Surveyor") and [calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_calculator "Mental calculator") were far more interested in [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics") than the [theoretical mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_mathematics "Theoretical mathematics") and geometry that were prized by the Greeks. It is unclear if the Romans first derived [their numerical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numerals "Roman numerals") directly from [the Greek precedent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_numerals "Greek numerals") or from [Etruscan numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_numerals "Etruscan numerals") used by the [Etruscan civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization "Etruscan civilization") centered in what is now [Tuscany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscany "Tuscany"), [central Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Italy "Central Italy").

Using calculation, Romans were adept at both instigating and detecting financial [fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraud "Fraud"), as well as [managing taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Roman_taxes "List of Roman taxes") for the [treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury "Treasury"). [Siculus Flaccus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siculus_Flaccus "Siculus Flaccus"), one of the Roman *[gromatici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromatici "Gromatici")* (i.e. land surveyor), wrote the *Categories of Fields*, which aided Roman surveyors in measuring the [surface areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_area "Surface area") of allotted lands and territories. Aside from managing trade and taxes, the Romans also regularly applied mathematics to solve problems in [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_engineering "Roman engineering"), including the erection of [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_architecture "Roman architecture") such as [bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_bridge "Roman bridge"), [road-building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads "Roman roads"), and [preparation for military campaigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_military_engineering "Roman military engineering"). [Arts and crafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_art "Roman art") such as [Roman mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_mosaic "Roman mosaic"), inspired by previous [Greek designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaics_of_Delos "Mosaics of Delos"), created illusionist geometric patterns and rich, detailed scenes that required precise measurements for each [tessera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessera "Tessera") tile, the [opus tessellatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_tessellatum "Opus tessellatum") pieces on average measuring eight millimeters square and the finer [opus vermiculatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_vermiculatum "Opus vermiculatum") pieces having an average surface of four millimeters square.

The creation of the [Roman calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_calendar "Roman calendar") also necessitated basic mathematics. The first calendar allegedly dates back to 8th century BC during the [Roman Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Kingdom "Roman Kingdom") and included 356 days plus a [leap year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_year "Leap year") every other year. In contrast, the [lunar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_calendar "Lunar calendar") of the Republican era contained 355 days, roughly ten-and-one-fourth days shorter than the [solar year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_year "Solar year"), a discrepancy that was solved by adding an extra month into the calendar after the 23rd of February. This calendar was supplanted by the [Julian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_calendar "Julian calendar"), a [solar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_calendar "Solar calendar") organized by [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") (100–44 BC) and devised by [Sosigenes of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sosigenes_of_Alexandria "Sosigenes of Alexandria") to include a [leap day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_day "Leap day") every four years in a 365-day cycle. This calendar, which contained an error of 11 minutes and 14 seconds, was later corrected by the [Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar "Gregorian calendar") organized by [Pope Gregory XIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XIII "Pope Gregory XIII") (r. 1572–1585), virtually the same solar calendar used in modern times as the international standard calendar.

At roughly the same time, [the Han Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty "Science and technology of the Han dynasty") and the Romans both invented the wheeled [odometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odometer "Odometer") device for measuring [distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance "Distance") traveled, the Roman model first described by the Roman civil engineer and architect [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius "Vitruvius") (c. 80 BC – c. 15 BC). The device was used at least until the reign of emperor [Commodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodus "Commodus") (r. 177 – 192 AD), but its design seems to have been lost until experiments were made during the 15th century in Western Europe. Perhaps relying on similar gear-work and [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_technology "Roman technology") found in the [Antikythera mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_mechanism "Antikythera mechanism"), the odometer of Vitruvius featured chariot wheels measuring 4 feet (1.2 m) in diameter turning four-hundred times in one [Roman mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_mile "Roman mile") (roughly 4590 ft/1400 m). With each revolution, a pin-and-axle device engaged a 400-tooth [cogwheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cogwheel "Cogwheel") that turned a second gear responsible for dropping pebbles into a box, each pebble representing one mile traversed.

## Chinese

An analysis of early Chinese mathematics has demonstrated its unique development compared to other parts of the world, leading scholars to assume an entirely independent development. The oldest extant mathematical text from China is the *[Zhoubi Suanjing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhoubi_Suanjing "Zhoubi Suanjing")* (周髀算經), variously dated to between 1200 BC and 100 BC, though a date of about 300 BC during the [Warring States Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_Period "Warring States Period") appears reasonable. However, the [Tsinghua Bamboo Slips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsinghua_Bamboo_Slips "Tsinghua Bamboo Slips"), containing the earliest known [decimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal "Decimal") [multiplication table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_table "Multiplication table") (although ancient Babylonians had ones with a base of 60), is dated around 305 BC and is perhaps the oldest surviving mathematical text of China.

Of particular note is the use in Chinese mathematics of a decimal positional notation system, the so-called "rod numerals" in which distinct ciphers were used for numbers between 1 and 10, and additional ciphers for powers of ten. Thus, the number 123 would be written using the symbol for "1", followed by the symbol for "100", then the symbol for "2" followed by the symbol for "10", followed by the symbol for "3". This was the most advanced number system in the world at the time, apparently in use several centuries before the common era and well before the development of the Indian numeral system. [Rod numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counting_rods "Counting rods") allowed the representation of numbers as large as desired and allowed calculations to be carried out on the *[suan pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suanpan "Suanpan")*, or Chinese abacus. The date of the invention of the *suan pan* is not certain, but the earliest written mention dates from AD 190, in [Xu Yue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Yue_(mathematician) "Xu Yue (mathematician)")'s *Supplementary Notes on the Art of Figures*.

The oldest extant work on geometry in China comes from the philosophical [Mohist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohism "Mohism") canon c. 330 BC, compiled by the followers of [Mozi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozi "Mozi") (470–390 BC). The *Mo Jing* described various aspects of many fields associated with physical science, and provided a small number of geometrical theorems as well. It also defined the concepts of [circumference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumference "Circumference"), [diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diameter "Diameter"), [radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius "Radius"), and [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume "Volume").

In 212 BC, the Emperor [Qin Shi Huang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang "Qin Shi Huang") commanded all books in the [Qin Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_Empire "Qin Empire") other than officially sanctioned ones be burned. This decree was not universally obeyed, but as a consequence of this order little is known about ancient Chinese mathematics before this date. After the [book burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burning_of_books_and_burying_of_scholars "Burning of books and burying of scholars") of 212 BC, the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") (202 BC–220 AD) produced works of mathematics which presumably expanded on works that are now lost. The most important of these is *[The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nine_Chapters_on_the_Mathematical_Art "The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art")*, the full title of which appeared by AD 179, but existed in part under other titles beforehand. It consists of 246 word problems involving agriculture, business, employment of geometry to figure height spans and dimension ratios for [Chinese pagoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_pagoda "Chinese pagoda") towers, engineering, [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying "Surveying"), and includes material on [right triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_triangle "Right triangle"). It created mathematical proof for the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem"), and a mathematical formula for [Gaussian elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_elimination "Gaussian elimination"). The treatise also provides values of [π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi"), which Chinese mathematicians originally approximated as 3 until [Liu Xin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Xin_(scholar) "Liu Xin (scholar)") (d. 23 AD) provided a figure of 3.1457 and subsequently [Zhang Heng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhang_Heng "Zhang Heng") (78–139) approximated pi as 3.1724, as well as 3.162 by taking the [square root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root "Square root") of 10. [Liu Hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Hui "Liu Hui") commented on the *Nine Chapters* in the 3rd century AD and [gave a value of π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Hui%27s_%CF%80_algorithm "Liu Hui's π algorithm") accurate to 5 decimal places (i.e. 3.14159). Though more of a matter of computational stamina than theoretical insight, in the 5th century AD [Zu Chongzhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zu_Chongzhi "Zu Chongzhi") computed [the value of π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mil%C3%BC "Milü") to seven decimal places (between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927), which remained the most accurate value of π for almost the next 1000 years. He also established a method which would later be called [Cavalieri's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalieri%27s_principle "Cavalieri's principle") to find the volume of a [sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere "Sphere").

The high-water mark of Chinese mathematics occurred in the 13th century during the latter half of the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty "Song dynasty") (960–1279), with the development of Chinese algebra. The most important text from that period is the *[Precious Mirror of the Four Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jade_Mirror_of_the_Four_Unknowns "Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns")* by [Zhu Shijie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Shijie "Zhu Shijie") (1249–1314), dealing with the solution of simultaneous higher order algebraic equations using a method similar to [Horner's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horner%27s_method "Horner's method"). The *Precious Mirror* also contains a diagram of [Pascal's triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_triangle "Pascal's triangle") with coefficients of binomial expansions through the eighth power, though both appear in Chinese works as early as 1100. The Chinese also made use of the complex combinatorial diagram known as the [magic square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_square "Magic square") and [magic circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_circle_(mathematics) "Magic circle (mathematics)"), described in ancient times and perfected by [Yang Hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Hui "Yang Hui") (AD 1238–1298).

Even after European mathematics began to flourish during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance"), European and Chinese mathematics were separate traditions, with significant Chinese mathematical output in decline from the 13th century onwards. [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit "Jesuit") missionaries such as [Matteo Ricci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteo_Ricci "Matteo Ricci") carried mathematical ideas back and forth between the two cultures from the 16th to 18th centuries, though at this point far more mathematical ideas were entering China than leaving.

[Japanese mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_mathematics "Japanese mathematics"), [Korean mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_numerals "Korean numerals"), and [Vietnamese mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_numerals "Vietnamese numerals") are traditionally viewed as stemming from Chinese mathematics and belonging to the [Confucian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucian "Confucian")-based [East Asian cultural sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_cultural_sphere "East Asian cultural sphere"). Korean and Japanese mathematics were heavily influenced by the algebraic works produced during China's Song dynasty, whereas Vietnamese mathematics was heavily indebted to popular works of China's [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty "Ming dynasty") (1368–1644). For instance, although Vietnamese mathematical treatises were written in either [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters "Chinese characters") or the native Vietnamese [Chữ Nôm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%AF_N%C3%B4m "Chữ Nôm") script, all of them followed the Chinese format of presenting a collection of problems with [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm") for solving them, followed by numerical answers. Mathematics in Vietnam and Korea were mostly associated with the professional court bureaucracy of [mathematicians and astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_astronomy "History of astronomy"), whereas in Japan it was more prevalent in the realm of [private schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_school "Private school").

## Indian

The earliest civilization on the Indian subcontinent is the [Indus Valley civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilization "Indus Valley civilization") (mature second phase: 2600 to 1900 BC) that flourished in the [Indus river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_river "Indus river") basin. Their cities were laid out with geometric regularity, but no known mathematical documents survive from this civilization.

The oldest extant mathematical records from India are the [Sulba Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulba_Sutras "Sulba Sutras") (dated variously between the 8th century BC and the 2nd century AD), appendices to religious texts which give simple rules for constructing altars of various shapes, such as squares, rectangles, parallelograms, and others. As with Egypt, the preoccupation with temple functions points to an origin of mathematics in religious ritual. The Sulba Sutras give methods for constructing a [circle with approximately the same area as a given square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squaring_the_circle "Squaring the circle"), which imply several different approximations of the value of π. In addition, they compute the [square root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root "Square root") of 2 to several decimal places, list Pythagorean triples, and give a statement of the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem"). All of these results are present in Babylonian mathematics, indicating Mesopotamian influence. It is not known to what extent the Sulba Sutras influenced later Indian mathematicians. As in China, there is a lack of continuity in Indian mathematics; significant advances are separated by long periods of inactivity.

[Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini "Pāṇini") (c. 5th century BC) formulated the rules for [Sanskrit grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_grammar "Sanskrit grammar"). His notation was similar to modern mathematical notation, and used metarules, [transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_(geometry) "Transformation (geometry)"), and [recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion "Recursion"). [Pingala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingala "Pingala") (roughly 3rd–1st centuries BC) in his treatise of [prosody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosody_(poetry) "Prosody (poetry)") uses a device corresponding to a [binary numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system "Binary numeral system"). His discussion of the [combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics "Combinatorics") of [meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(music) "Metre (music)") corresponds to an elementary version of the [binomial theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_theorem "Binomial theorem"). Pingala's work also contains the basic ideas of [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number "Fibonacci number") (called *mātrāmeru*).

The next significant mathematical documents from India after the *Sulba Sutras* are the *Siddhantas*, astronomical treatises from the 4th and 5th centuries AD ([Gupta period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_period "Gupta period")) showing strong Hellenistic influence. They are significant in that they contain the first instance of trigonometric relations based on the half-chord, as is the case in modern trigonometry, rather than the full chord, as was the case in Ptolemaic trigonometry. Through a series of translation errors, the words "sine" and "cosine" derive from the Sanskrit "jiya" and "kojiya".

Around 500 AD, [Aryabhata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata "Aryabhata") wrote the *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya "Aryabhatiya")*, a slim volume, written in verse, intended to supplement the rules of calculation used in astronomy and mathematical mensuration, though with no feeling for logic or deductive methodology. It is in the *Aryabhatiya* that the decimal place-value system first appears. Several centuries later, the [Muslim mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics "Islamic mathematics") [Abu Rayhan Biruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Rayhan_Biruni "Abu Rayhan Biruni") described the *Aryabhatiya* as a "mix of common pebbles and costly crystals".

In the 7th century, [Brahmagupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta "Brahmagupta") identified the [Brahmagupta theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta_theorem "Brahmagupta theorem"), [Brahmagupta's identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta%27s_identity "Brahmagupta's identity") and [Brahmagupta's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta%27s_formula "Brahmagupta's formula"), and for the first time, in *[Brahma-sphuta-siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasphutasiddhanta "Brahmasphutasiddhanta")*, he lucidly explained the use of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_(number) "0 (number)") as both a placeholder and [decimal digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_digit "Decimal digit"), and explained the [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system "Hindu–Arabic numeral system"). It was from a translation of this Indian text on mathematics (c. 770) that Islamic mathematicians were introduced to this numeral system, which they adapted as [Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_numerals "Arabic numerals"). Islamic scholars carried knowledge of this number system to Europe by the 12th century, and it has now displaced all older number systems throughout the world. Various symbol sets are used to represent numbers in the Hindu–Arabic numeral system, all of which evolved from the [Brahmi numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmi_numeral "Brahmi numeral"). Each of the roughly dozen major scripts of India has its own numeral glyphs. In the 10th century, [Halayudha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halayudha "Halayudha")'s commentary on [Pingala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingala "Pingala")'s work contains a study of the [Fibonacci sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_sequence "Fibonacci sequence") and [Pascal's triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_triangle "Pascal's triangle").

In the 12th century, [Bhāskara II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C4%81skara_II "Bhāskara II"), who lived in southern India, wrote extensively on the mathematical knowledge of his time. In his astronomical work, he described results that have been interpreted by later scholars as resembling early infinitesimal methods. His writings include ideas approximately equivalent to infinitesimals and a special case of the [mean value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_value_theorem "Mean value theorem") in inverse interpolation of sine.He also made significant contributions to algebra, including methods for solving indeterminate equations of the type later known as Pell equations, which he treated using cyclic procedures such as the chakravāla method. In the 14th century, [Narayana Pandita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narayana_Pandita_(mathematician) "Narayana Pandita (mathematician)") completed his *[Ganita Kaumudi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganita_Kaumudi "Ganita Kaumudi")*.

Also in the 14th century, [Madhava of Sangamagrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhava_of_Sangamagrama "Madhava of Sangamagrama"), the founder of the [Kerala School of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_School_of_Astronomy_and_Mathematics "Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics"), found the [Madhava–Leibniz series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_formula_for_pi "Leibniz formula for pi") and obtained from it a [transformed series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximations_of_%CF%80#Middle_Ages "Approximations of π"), whose first 21 terms he used to compute the value of π as 3.14159265359. Madhava also found [the Madhava-Gregory series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory%27s_series "Gregory's series") to determine the arctangent, the Madhava-Newton [power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series "Power series") to determine sine and cosine and [the Taylor approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series "Taylor series") for sine and cosine functions. In the 16th century, [Jyesthadeva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyesthadeva "Jyesthadeva") consolidated many of the Kerala School's developments and theorems in the *Yukti-bhāṣā*. has been argued that certain ideas of calculus like infinite series and taylor series of some trigonometry functions, were transmitted to Europe in the 16th century via [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit "Jesuit") missionaries and traders who were active around the ancient port of [Muziris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muziris "Muziris") at the time and, as a result, directly influenced later European developments in analysis and calculus. However, other scholars argue that the Kerala School did not formulate a systematic theory of [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative "Derivative") and [integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral "Integral"), and that there is not any direct evidence of their results being transmitted outside Kerala.

## Islamic empires

The [Islamic Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate") established across the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"), [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"), [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa"), [Iberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula "Iberian Peninsula"), and in parts of [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India "History of India") in the 8th century made significant contributions towards mathematics. Although most Islamic texts on mathematics were written in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language"), they were not all written by [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab "Arab"), since much like the status of Greek in the Hellenistic world, Arabic was used as the written language of non-Arab scholars throughout the Islamic world at the time.

In the 9th century, the Persian mathematician [Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%E1%B8%A5ammad_ibn_M%C5%ABs%C4%81_al-Khw%C4%81rizm%C4%AB "Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī") wrote an important book on the [Hindu–Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numerals "Hindu–Arabic numerals") and one on methods for solving equations. His book *On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals*, written about 825, along with the work of [Al-Kindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi "Al-Kindi"), were instrumental in spreading [Indian mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematics "Indian mathematics") and [Indian numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system "Hindu–Arabic numeral system") to the West. The word *[algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm")* is derived from the Latinization of his name, Algoritmi, and the word *algebra* from the title of one of his works, *[Al-Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī hīsāb al-ğabr wa'l-muqābala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing "The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing")* (*The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing*). He gave an exhaustive explanation for the algebraic solution of quadratic equations with positive roots, and he was the first to teach algebra in an [elementary form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_algebra "Elementary algebra") and for its own sake. He also discussed the fundamental method of "[reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_(mathematics) "Reduction (mathematics)")" and "balancing", referring to the transposition of subtracted terms to the other side of an equation, that is, the cancellation of like terms on opposite sides of the equation. This is the operation which al-Khwārizmī originally described as *al-jabr*. His algebra was also no longer concerned "with a series of problems to be resolved, but an [exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expository_writing "Expository writing") which starts with primitive terms in which the combinations must give all possible prototypes for equations, which henceforward explicitly constitute the true object of study." He also studied an equation for its own sake and "in a generic manner, insofar as it does not simply emerge in the course of solving a problem, but is specifically called on to define an infinite class of problems."

In Egypt, [Abu Kamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Kamil "Abu Kamil") extended algebra to the set of [irrational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_numbers "Irrational numbers"), accepting square roots and fourth roots as solutions and coefficients to quadratic equations. He also developed techniques used to solve three non-linear simultaneous equations with three unknown variables. One unique feature of his works was trying to find all the possible solutions to some of his problems, including one where he found 2676 solutions. His works formed an important foundation for the development of algebra and influenced later mathematicians, such as al-Karaji and Fibonacci.

Further developments in algebra were made by [Al-Karaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Karaji "Al-Karaji") in his treatise *al-Fakhri*, where he extends the methodology to incorporate integer powers and integer roots of unknown quantities. Something close to a [proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof "Mathematical proof") by [mathematical induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_induction "Mathematical induction") appears in a book written by Al-Karaji around 1000 AD, who used it to prove the [binomial theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_theorem "Binomial theorem"), [Pascal's triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_triangle "Pascal's triangle"), and the sum of integral [cubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_(algebra) "Cube (algebra)"). The [historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian "Historian") of mathematics, F. Woepcke, praised Al-Karaji for being "the first who introduced the [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory "Theory") of [algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus")." Also in the 10th century, [Abul Wafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_Wafa "Abul Wafa") translated the works of [Diophantus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantus "Diophantus") into Arabic. [Ibn al-Haytham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham") was the first mathematician to derive the formula for the sum of the fourth powers, using a method that is readily generalizable for determining the general formula for the sum of any integral powers. He performed an integration in order to find the volume of a [paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraboloid "Paraboloid"), and was able to generalize his result for the integrals of [polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial "Polynomial") up to the [fourth degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_polynomial "Quartic polynomial"). He thus came close to finding a general formula for the integrals of polynomials, but he was not concerned with any polynomials higher than the fourth degree.

In the late 11th century, [Omar Khayyam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyam "Omar Khayyam") wrote *Discussions of the Difficulties in Euclid*, a book about what he perceived as flaws in [Euclid's *Elements*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements "Euclid's Elements"), especially the [parallel postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate "Parallel postulate"). He was also the first to find the general geometric solution to [cubic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_equation "Cubic equation"). He was also very influential in [calendar reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_reform "Calendar reform").

In the 13th century, [Nasir al-Din Tusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Din_Tusi "Nasir al-Din Tusi") (Nasireddin) made advances in [spherical trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_trigonometry "Spherical trigonometry"). He also wrote influential work on Euclid's [parallel postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate "Parallel postulate"). In the 15th century, [Ghiyath al-Kashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghiyath_al-Kashi "Ghiyath al-Kashi") computed the value of π to the 16th decimal place. Kashi also had an algorithm for calculating *n*th roots, which was a special case of the methods given many centuries later by [Ruffini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Ruffini_(mathematician) "Paolo Ruffini (mathematician)") and [Horner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_George_Horner "William George Horner").

Other achievements of Muslim mathematicians during this period include the addition of the decimal point notation to the Arabic numerals, the discovery of all the modern trigonometric functions besides the sine, al-Kindi's introduction of cryptanalysis and frequency analysis, the development of analytic geometry by Ibn al-Haytham, the beginning of algebraic geometry by Omar Khayyam, and the development of an algebraic notation by al-Qalasādī.

During the time of the Ottoman Empire and Safavid Empire from the 15th century, the development of Islamic mathematics became stagnant.

## Maya

In the [Pre-Columbian Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_Americas "Pre-Columbian Americas"), the [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") that flourished in [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico") and [Central America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_America "Central America") during the 1st millennium AD developed a unique tradition of mathematics that, due to its geographic isolation, was entirely independent of existing European, Egyptian, and Asian mathematics. [Maya numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_numerals "Maya numerals") used a [base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radix "Radix") of twenty, the [vigesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigesimal "Vigesimal") system, instead of a base of ten that forms the basis of the [decimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal "Decimal") system used by most modern cultures. The Maya used mathematics to create the [Maya calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_calendar "Maya calendar") as well as to predict astronomical phenomena in their native [Maya astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_astronomy "Maya astronomy"). While the concept of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero "Zero") had to be inferred in the mathematics of many contemporary cultures, the Maya developed a standard symbol for it.

## Medieval European

Medieval European interest in mathematics was driven by concerns quite different from those of modern mathematicians. One driving element was the belief that mathematics provided the key to understanding the created order of nature, frequently justified by [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato")'s *[Timaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue) "Timaeus (dialogue)")* and the biblical passage (in the *[Book of Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Wisdom "Book of Wisdom")*) that God had *ordered all things in measure, and number, and weight*.

[Boethius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boethius "Boethius") provided a place for mathematics in the curriculum in the 6th century when he coined the term *[quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium "Quadrivium")* to describe the study of arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music. He wrote *De institutione arithmetica*, a free translation from the Greek of [Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus "Nicomachus")'s *Introduction to Arithmetic*; *De institutione musica*, also derived from Greek sources; and a series of excerpts from Euclid's *Elements*. His works were theoretical, rather than practical, and were the basis of mathematical study until the recovery of Greek and Arabic mathematical works.

In the 12th century, European scholars traveled to Spain and Sicily [seeking scientific Arabic texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century "Latin translations of the 12th century"), including [al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khw%C4%81rizm%C4%AB "Al-Khwārizmī")'s *[The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing "The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing")*, translated into Latin by [Robert of Chester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_of_Chester "Robert of Chester"), and the complete text of Euclid's *Elements*, translated in various versions by [Adelard of Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelard_of_Bath "Adelard of Bath"), [Herman of Carinthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_of_Carinthia "Herman of Carinthia"), and [Gerard of Cremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_of_Cremona "Gerard of Cremona"). These and other new sources sparked a renewal of mathematics.

Leonardo of Pisa, now known as [Fibonacci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci "Fibonacci"), serendipitously learned about the [Hindu–Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numerals "Hindu–Arabic numerals") on a trip to what is now [Béjaïa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9ja%C3%AFa "Béjaïa"), [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algeria "Algeria") with his merchant father. (Europe was still using [Roman numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numerals "Roman numerals").) There, he observed a system of [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic") (specifically [algorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorism "Algorism")) which due to the [positional notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positional_notation "Positional notation") of Hindu–Arabic numerals was much more efficient and greatly facilitated commerce. Leonardo wrote *[Liber Abaci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liber_Abaci "Liber Abaci")* in 1202 (updated in 1254) introducing the technique to Europe and beginning a long period of popularizing it. The book also brought to Europe what is now known as the [Fibonacci sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_sequence "Fibonacci sequence") (known to Indian mathematicians for hundreds of years before that) which Fibonacci used as an unremarkable example.

The 14th century saw the development of new mathematical concepts to investigate a wide range of problems. One important contribution was development of mathematics of local motion. [Thomas Bradwardine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bradwardine "Thomas Bradwardine") proposed that speed (V) increases in arithmetic proportion as the ratio of force (F) to resistance (R) increases in geometric proportion. Bradwardine expressed this by a series of specific examples, but although the logarithm had not yet been conceived, we can express his conclusion anachronistically by writing:
V = log (F/R). Bradwardine's analysis is an example of transferring a mathematical technique used by [al-Kindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi "Al-Kindi") and [Arnald of Villanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnald_of_Villanova "Arnald of Villanova") to quantify the nature of compound medicines to a different physical problem.

One of the 14th-century [Oxford Calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Calculators "Oxford Calculators"), [William of Heytesbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Heytesbury "William of Heytesbury"), lacking [differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_calculus "Differential calculus") and the concept of [limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_of_a_function "Limit of a function"), proposed to measure instantaneous speed "by the path that *would* be described by [a body] *if*... it were moved uniformly at the same degree of speed with which it is moved in that given instant".

Heytesbury and others mathematically determined the distance covered by a body undergoing uniformly accelerated motion (today solved by integration), stating that "a moving body uniformly acquiring or losing that increment [of speed] will traverse in some given time a [distance] completely equal to that which it would traverse if it were moving continuously through the same time with the mean degree [of speed]".

[Nicole Oresme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_Oresme "Nicole Oresme") at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") and the Italian [Giovanni di Casali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_di_Casali "Giovanni di Casali") independently provided graphical demonstrations of this relationship, asserting that the area under the line depicting the constant acceleration, represented the total distance traveled. In a later mathematical commentary on Euclid's *Elements*, Oresme made a more detailed general analysis in which he demonstrated that a body will acquire in each successive increment of time an increment of any quality that increases as the odd numbers. Since Euclid had demonstrated the sum of the odd numbers are the square numbers, the total quality acquired by the body increases as the square of the time.

## Renaissance

During the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance"), the development of mathematics and of [accounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accounting "Accounting") were intertwined. While there is no direct relationship between algebra and accounting, the teaching of the subjects and the books published often intended for the children of merchants who were sent to reckoning schools (in [Flanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanders "Flanders") and [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany")) or [abacus schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abacus_school "Abacus school") (known as *abbaco* in Italy), where they learned the skills useful for trade and commerce. There is probably no need for algebra in performing [bookkeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookkeeping "Bookkeeping") operations, but for complex bartering operations or the calculation of [compound interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_interest "Compound interest"), a basic knowledge of arithmetic was mandatory and knowledge of algebra was very useful.

[Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca "Piero della Francesca") (c. 1415 – c. 1492) wrote books on [solid geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_geometry "Solid geometry") and [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_(graphical) "Perspective (graphical)"), including *[De Prospectiva Pingendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Prospectiva_Pingendi "De Prospectiva Pingendi") (On Perspective for Painting)*, *Trattato d'Abaco (Abacus Treatise)*, and *[De quinque corporibus regularibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_quinque_corporibus_regularibus "De quinque corporibus regularibus") (On the Five Regular Solids)*.

[Luca Pacioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luca_Pacioli "Luca Pacioli")'s *[Summa de Arithmetica, Geometria, Proportioni et Proportionalità](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_de_arithmetica "Summa de arithmetica")* (Italian: "Review of [Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic"), [Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), [Ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio "Ratio") and [Proportion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_(mathematics) "Proportionality (mathematics)")") was first printed and published in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice "Venice") in 1494. It included a 27-page treatise on bookkeeping, *"Particularis de Computis et Scripturis"* (Italian: "Details of Calculation and Recording"). It was written primarily for, and sold mainly to, merchants who used the book as a reference text, as a source of pleasure from the [mathematical puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_puzzles "Mathematical puzzles") it contained, and to aid the education of their sons. In *Summa Arithmetica*, Pacioli introduced symbols for [plus and minus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_and_minus "Plus and minus") for the first time in a printed book, symbols that became standard notation in Italian Renaissance mathematics. *Summa Arithmetica* was also the first known book printed in Italy to contain algebra. Pacioli obtained many of his ideas from Piero Della Francesca whom he plagiarized.

In Italy, during the first half of the 16th century, [Scipione del Ferro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scipione_del_Ferro "Scipione del Ferro") and [Niccolò Fontana Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Fontana_Tartaglia "Niccolò Fontana Tartaglia") discovered solutions for [cubic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_equation "Cubic equation"). [Gerolamo Cardano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerolamo_Cardano "Gerolamo Cardano") published them in his 1545 book *[Ars Magna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Magna_(Gerolamo_Cardano) "Ars Magna (Gerolamo Cardano)")*, together with a solution for the [quartic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_equation "Quartic equation"), discovered by his student [Lodovico Ferrari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lodovico_Ferrari "Lodovico Ferrari"). In 1572 [Rafael Bombelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Bombelli "Rafael Bombelli") published his *L'Algebra* in which he showed how to deal with the [imaginary quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_number "Imaginary number") that could appear in Cardano's formula for solving cubic equations.

[Simon Stevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Stevin "Simon Stevin")'s *[De Thiende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Thiende "De Thiende")* ('the art of tenths'), first published in Dutch in 1585, contained the first systematic treatment of [decimal notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_notation "Decimal notation") in Europe, which influenced all later work on the [real number system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_system "Real number system").

Driven by the demands of navigation and the growing need for accurate maps of large areas, [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry") grew to be a major branch of mathematics. [Bartholomaeus Pitiscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomaeus_Pitiscus "Bartholomaeus Pitiscus") was the first to use the word, publishing his *Trigonometria* in 1595. Regiomontanus's table of sines and cosines was published in 1533.

During the Renaissance the desire of artists to represent the natural world realistically, together with the rediscovered philosophy of the Greeks, led artists to study mathematics. They were also the engineers and architects of that time, and so had need of mathematics in any case. The art of painting in perspective, and the developments in geometry that were involved, were studied intensely.

## Mathematics during the Scientific Revolution

### 16th century

In the 16th century, [Viète](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vi%C3%A8te "Viète") laid down the foundations of [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") in 1591. This was foundational for the mathematics of [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes "Descartes").

### 17th century

The 17th century saw an unprecedented increase of mathematical and scientific ideas across Europe. [Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_Brahe "Tycho Brahe") had gathered a large quantity of mathematical data describing the positions of the planets in the sky. By his position as Brahe's assistant, [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler "Johannes Kepler") was first exposed to and seriously interacted with the topic of planetary motion. Kepler's calculations were made simpler by the contemporaneous invention of [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm "Logarithm") by [John Napier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Napier "John Napier") and [Jost Bürgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jost_B%C3%BCrgi "Jost Bürgi"). Kepler succeeded in formulating mathematical laws of planetary motion.
The [analytic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry "Analytic geometry") developed by [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes "René Descartes") (1596–1650) allowed those orbits to be plotted on a graph, in [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates "Cartesian coordinates").

Building on earlier work by many predecessors, [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") discovered the laws of physics that explain [Kepler's Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_Laws "Kepler's Laws"), and brought together the concepts now known as [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus"). Independently, [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz"), developed calculus and much of the calculus notation still in use today. He also refined the [binary number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_number "Binary number") system, which is the foundation of nearly all digital ([electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_calculator "Scientific calculator"), [solid-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_electronics "Solid-state electronics"), [discrete logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate "Logic gate")) [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer "Computer").

Science and mathematics had become an international endeavor, which would soon spread over the entire world.

In addition to the application of mathematics to the studies of the heavens, [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics") began to expand into new areas, with the correspondence of [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") and [Blaise Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Pascal "Blaise Pascal"). Pascal and Fermat set the groundwork for the investigations of [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") and the corresponding rules of [combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics "Combinatorics") in their discussions over a game of [gambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambling "Gambling"). Pascal, with his [wager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_Wager "Pascal's Wager"), attempted to use the newly developing probability theory to argue for a life devoted to religion, on the grounds that even if the probability of success was small, the rewards were infinite. In some sense, this foreshadowed the development of [utility theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_theory "Utility theory") in the 18th and 19th centuries.

### 18th century

The most influential mathematician of the 18th century was arguably [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler") (1707–83). His contributions range from founding the study of [graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory "Graph theory") with the [Seven Bridges of Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Bridges_of_K%C3%B6nigsberg "Seven Bridges of Königsberg") problem to standardizing many modern mathematical terms and notations. For example, he named the square root of minus 1 with the symbol [*i*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit "Imaginary unit"), and he popularized the use of the Greek letter

π
{\displaystyle \pi }
 to stand for the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. He made numerous contributions to the study of topology, graph theory, calculus, combinatorics, and complex analysis, as evidenced by the multitude of theorems and notations named for him.

Other important European mathematicians of the 18th century included [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange "Joseph Louis Lagrange"), who did pioneering work in number theory, algebra, differential calculus, and the calculus of variations, and [Pierre-Simon Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Simon_Laplace "Pierre-Simon Laplace"), who, in the age of [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon"), did important work on the foundations of [celestial mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics "Celestial mechanics") and on [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics "Statistics").

## Modern

### 19th century

Throughout the 19th century mathematics became increasingly abstract. [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") (1777–1855) did revolutionary work on [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics) "Function (mathematics)") of [complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable "Complex variable"), in [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), and on the convergence of [series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_(mathematics) "Series (mathematics)"), leaving aside his many contributions to science. He also gave the first satisfactory proofs of the [fundamental theorem of algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_algebra "Fundamental theorem of algebra") and [quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity "Quadratic reciprocity") law.

This century saw the development of the two forms of [non-Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry "Non-Euclidean geometry"), where the [parallel postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate "Parallel postulate") of Euclidean geometry no longer holds.
The Russian mathematician [Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Ivanovich_Lobachevsky "Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky") and his rival, the Hungarian mathematician [János Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A1nos_Bolyai "János Bolyai"), independently defined and studied [hyperbolic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_geometry "Hyperbolic geometry"), where uniqueness of parallels no longer holds. In this geometry the sum of angles in a triangle add up to less than 180°. [Elliptic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_geometry "Elliptic geometry") was developed later in the 19th century by the German mathematician [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann "Bernhard Riemann"); here no parallel can be found and the angles in a triangle add up to more than 180°. Riemann also developed [Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_geometry "Riemannian geometry"), which unifies and vastly generalizes the three types of geometry, and he defined the concept of a [manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold"), which generalizes the ideas of [curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve "Curve") and [surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_(topology) "Surface (topology)"), and set the mathematical foundations for the [theory of general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity").

The 19th century saw the beginning of a great deal of [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"). [Hermann Grassmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Grassmann "Hermann Grassmann") in Germany gave a first version of [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space "Vector space"), [William Rowan Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rowan_Hamilton "William Rowan Hamilton") in Ireland developed [noncommutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra "Noncommutative algebra"). The British mathematician [George Boole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Boole "George Boole") devised an algebra that soon evolved into what is now called [Boolean algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra "Boolean algebra"), in which the only numbers were 0 and 1. Boolean algebra is the starting point of [mathematical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_logic "Mathematical logic") and has important applications in [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering") and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science "Computer science").
[Augustin-Louis Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin-Louis_Cauchy "Augustin-Louis Cauchy"), [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann "Bernhard Riemann"), and [Karl Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Weierstrass "Karl Weierstrass") reformulated the calculus in a more rigorous fashion.

Also, for the first time, the limits of mathematics were explored. [Paolo Ruffini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Ruffini "Paolo Ruffini"), [Niels Henrik Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Henrik_Abel "Niels Henrik Abel"), and [Évariste Galois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89variste_Galois "Évariste Galois") proved there is no general algebraic method for solving polynomial equations of degree greater than four ([Abel–Ruffini theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel%E2%80%93Ruffini_theorem "Abel–Ruffini theorem")). Other 19th-century mathematicians used this in their proofs that straight edge and compass alone are not sufficient to [trisect an arbitrary angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trisect_an_arbitrary_angle "Trisect an arbitrary angle"), to construct the side of a cube twice the volume of a given cube, [nor to construct a square equal in area to a given circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squaring_the_circle "Squaring the circle"). Mathematicians had vainly attempted to solve these problems since the ancient Greeks. On the other hand, the limitation of three [dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension "Dimension") in geometry was surpassed in the 19th century through considerations of [parameter space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_space "Parameter space") and [hypercomplex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_number "Hypercomplex number").

Abel and Galois's investigations into the solutions of various polynomial equations laid the groundwork for further developments of [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory"), and the associated fields of [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"). In the 20th century physicists and other scientists have seen group theory as the ideal way to study [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry "Symmetry").

In the later 19th century, [Georg Cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Cantor "Georg Cantor") established the first foundations of [set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory "Set theory"), which enabled the rigorous treatment of the notion of infinity and has become the common language of nearly all mathematics. Cantor's set theory, and the rise of [mathematical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_logic "Mathematical logic") in the hands of [Peano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano "Peano"), [L.E.J. Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L.E.J._Brouwer "L.E.J. Brouwer"), [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert"), [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell "Bertrand Russell"), and [A.N. Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.N._Whitehead "A.N. Whitehead"), initiated a long running debate on the [foundations of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_mathematics "Foundations of mathematics").

The 19th century saw the founding of a number of national mathematical societies: the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") in 1865, the [Société mathématique de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_math%C3%A9matique_de_France "Société mathématique de France") in 1872, the [Circolo Matematico di Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circolo_Matematico_di_Palermo "Circolo Matematico di Palermo") in 1884, the [Edinburgh Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_Mathematical_Society "Edinburgh Mathematical Society") in 1883, and the [American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society") in 1888. The first international, special-interest society, the [Quaternion Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternion_Association "Quaternion Association"), was formed in 1899, in the context of a [vector controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_quaternion#Historical_review "Hyperbolic quaternion"). In 1897, [Kurt Hensel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Hensel "Kurt Hensel") introduced [p-adic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_number "P-adic number").

### 20th century

The 20th century saw mathematics become a major profession. By the end of the century, thousands of new Ph.D.s in mathematics were being awarded every year, and jobs were available in both teaching and industry. An effort to catalogue the areas and applications of mathematics was undertaken in [Klein's encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%27s_encyclopedia "Klein's encyclopedia").

In a 1900 speech to the [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians"), [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert") set out a list of [23 unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert%27s_problems "Hilbert's problems"). These problems, spanning many areas of mathematics, formed a central focus for much of 20th-century mathematics. 10 have been solved, 7 partially solved, and 2 are still open. The remaining 4 are too loosely formulated to be stated as solved or not.

Notable historical conjectures were finally proven. In 1976, [Wolfgang Haken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Haken "Wolfgang Haken") and [Kenneth Appel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Appel "Kenneth Appel") proved the [four color theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_color_theorem "Four color theorem"), controversial at the time for the use of a computer to do so. [Andrew Wiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wiles "Andrew Wiles"), building on the work of others, proved [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem") in 1995. [Paul Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Cohen "Paul Cohen") and [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del "Kurt Gödel") proved that the [continuum hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_hypothesis "Continuum hypothesis") is [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_independence "Logical independence") of (could neither be proved nor disproved from) the [standard axioms of set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC "ZFC"). In 1998, [Thomas Callister Hales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Callister_Hales "Thomas Callister Hales") proved the [Kepler conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler_conjecture "Kepler conjecture"), also using a computer.

Mathematical collaborations of unprecedented size and scope took place. An example is the [classification of finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups "Classification of finite simple groups") (also called the "enormous theorem"), whose proof between 1955 and 2004 required 500-odd journal articles by about 100 authors, and filling tens of thousands of pages. A group of French mathematicians, including [Jean Dieudonné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Dieudonn%C3%A9 "Jean Dieudonné") and [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Weil "André Weil"), publishing under the [pseudonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudonym "Pseudonym") "[Nicolas Bourbaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Bourbaki "Nicolas Bourbaki")", attempted to exposit all of known mathematics as a coherent rigorous whole. The resulting several dozen volumes has had a controversial influence on mathematical education.

[Differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry") came into its own when [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") used it in [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"). Entirely new areas of mathematics such as [mathematical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_logic "Mathematical logic"), [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), and [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann")'s [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory "Game theory") changed the kinds of questions that could be answered by mathematical methods.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] All kinds of [structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_structure "Mathematical structure") were abstracted using axioms and given names like [metric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_space "Metric space"), [topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space "Topological space") etc. The concept of an abstract structure was itself abstracted and led to [category theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_theory "Category theory"). [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck "Grothendieck") and [Serre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Serre "Jean-Pierre Serre") recast [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry") using [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_(mathematics) "Sheaf (mathematics)"). Large advances were made in the qualitative study of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems_theory "Dynamical systems theory") that [Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9 "Henri Poincaré") had begun in the 1890s.
[Measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory "Measure theory") was developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Applications of measures include the [Lebesgue integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integral "Lebesgue integral"), [Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov "Kolmogorov")'s axiomatisation of [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory"), and [ergodic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theory "Ergodic theory"). [Knot theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_theory "Knot theory") greatly expanded. [Quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") aided the development of [functional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_analysis "Functional analysis"), a branch of mathematics developed by [Stefan Banach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefan_Banach "Stefan Banach") and his collaborators who formed the [Lwów School of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w_School_of_Mathematics "Lwów School of Mathematics"). Other new areas include [Laurent Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_Schwartz "Laurent Schwartz")'s [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_(mathematics) "Distribution (mathematics)"), [fixed point theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-point_theorem "Fixed-point theorem"), [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory") and [René Thom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Thom "René Thom")'s [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory"), [model theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_theory "Model theory"), and [Mandelbrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoit_Mandelbrot "Benoit Mandelbrot")'s [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals "Fractals"). [Lie theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_theory "Lie theory") with its [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group "Lie group") and [Lie algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra "Lie algebra") became one of the major areas of study.

[Nonstandard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonstandard_analysis "Nonstandard analysis"), introduced by [Abraham Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Robinson "Abraham Robinson"), rehabilitated the [infinitesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal "Infinitesimal") approach to calculus, which had fallen into disrepute in favour of the theory of [limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_of_a_function "Limit of a function"), by extending the field of real numbers to the [Hyperreal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperreal_number "Hyperreal number") which include infinitesimal and infinite quantities. An even larger number system, the [surreal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surreal_number "Surreal number") were discovered by [John Horton Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horton_Conway "John Horton Conway") in connection with [combinatorial games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_game "Combinatorial game").

The development and continual improvement of [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer "Computer"), at first mechanical analog machines and then digital electronic machines, allowed [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_industry "Private industry") to deal with larger and larger amounts of data to facilitate mass production and distribution and communication, and new areas of mathematics were developed to deal with this: [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing "Alan Turing")'s [computability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability_theory "Computability theory"); [complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory "Computational complexity theory"); [Derrick Henry Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer "Derrick Henry Lehmer")'s use of [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC "ENIAC") to further number theory and the [Lucas–Lehmer primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas%E2%80%93Lehmer_primality_test "Lucas–Lehmer primality test"); [Rózsa Péter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B3zsa_P%C3%A9ter "Rózsa Péter")'s [recursive function theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_function_theory "Recursive function theory"); [Claude Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Shannon "Claude Shannon")'s [information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory "Information theory"); [signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_processing "Signal processing"); [data analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_analysis "Data analysis"); [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_optimization "Mathematical optimization") and other areas of [operations research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operations_research "Operations research").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] In the preceding centuries much mathematical focus was on calculus and continuous functions, but the rise of computing and communication networks led to an increasing importance of [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics "Discrete mathematics") concepts and the expansion of [combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics "Combinatorics") including [graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory "Graph theory"). The speed and data processing abilities of computers also enabled the handling of mathematical problems that were too time-consuming to deal with by pencil and paper calculations, leading to areas such as [numerical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_analysis "Numerical analysis") and [computer algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_algebra "Computer algebra"). Some of the most important methods and [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm") of the 20th century are: the [simplex algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplex_algorithm "Simplex algorithm"), the [fast Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform "Fast Fourier transform"), [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code "Error-correcting code"), the [Kalman filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalman_filter "Kalman filter") from [control theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_theory "Control theory") and the [RSA algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_algorithm "RSA algorithm") of [public-key cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptography "Public-key cryptography").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

At the same time, deep insights were made about the limitations to mathematics. In 1929 and 1930, it was proved by [Mojżesz Presburger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moj%C5%BCesz_Presburger "Mojżesz Presburger"), that the truth or falsity of all statements formulated about the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number "Natural number") plus either addition or multiplication (but not both), was [decidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidability_(logic) "Decidability (logic)"), i.e. could be determined by some algorithm. In 1931, [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del "Kurt Gödel") found that this was not the case for the natural numbers plus both addition and multiplication; this system, known as [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic "Peano arithmetic"), was in fact [incomplete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompleteness_theorem "Incompleteness theorem"). (Peano arithmetic is adequate for a good deal of [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), including the notion of [prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number "Prime number").) A consequence of Gödel's two [incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompleteness_theorem "Incompleteness theorem") is that in any mathematical system that includes Peano arithmetic (including all of [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis") and geometry), truth necessarily outruns proof, i.e. there are true statements that [cannot be proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompleteness_theorem "Incompleteness theorem") within the system. Hence mathematics cannot be reduced to mathematical logic, and [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert")'s dream of making all of mathematics complete and consistent needed to be reformulated.

One of the more colorful figures in 20th-century mathematics was [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan "Srinivasa Ramanujan") (1887–1920), an Indian [autodidact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autodidact "Autodidact") who conjectured or proved over 3000 theorems, including properties of [highly composite numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly_composite_number "Highly composite number"), the [partition function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_function_(number_theory) "Partition function (number theory)") and its [asymptotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotics "Asymptotics"), and [mock theta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan_theta_function "Ramanujan theta function"). He also made major investigations in the areas of [gamma functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_function "Gamma function"), [modular forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_form "Modular form"), [divergent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divergent_series "Divergent series"), [hypergeometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_hypergeometric_function "General hypergeometric function") and prime number theory.

[Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s "Paul Erdős") published more papers than any other mathematician in history, working with hundreds of collaborators. Mathematicians have a game equivalent to the [Kevin Bacon Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Bacon_Game "Kevin Bacon Game"), which leads to the [Erdős number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erd%C5%91s_number "Erdős number") of a mathematician. This describes the "collaborative distance" between a person and Erdős, as measured by joint authorship of mathematical papers.

[Emmy Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmy_Noether "Emmy Noether") has been described by many as the most important woman in the history of mathematics. She studied the theories of [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)"), [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)"), and [algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_field "Algebra over a field").

As in most areas of study, the explosion of knowledge in the scientific age has led to specialization: by the end of the century, there were hundreds of specialized areas in mathematics, and the [Mathematics Subject Classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_Subject_Classification "Mathematics Subject Classification") was dozens of pages long. More and more [mathematical journals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_journal "Mathematical journal") were published and, by the end of the century, the development of the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web") led to online publishing.

### 21st century

In 2000, the [Clay Mathematics Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_Mathematics_Institute "Clay Mathematics Institute") announced the seven [Millennium Prize Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems "Millennium Prize Problems"). In 2003 the [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_conjecture "Poincaré conjecture") was solved by [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman "Grigori Perelman") (who declined to accept an award, as he was critical of the mathematics establishment).

Most mathematical journals now have online versions as well as print versions, and many online-only journals are launched. There is an increasing drive toward [open access publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_access_(publishing) "Open access (publishing)"), first made popular by [arXiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArXiv "ArXiv").

Many other important problems have been solved in this century. Examples include the [Green–Tao theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green%E2%80%93Tao_theorem "Green–Tao theorem") (2004), [existence of bounded gaps between arbitrarily large primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yitang_Zhang#Research "Yitang Zhang") (2013), and the [modularity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modularity_theorem "Modularity theorem") (2001). The [AKS primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AKS_primality_test "AKS primality test") was published in 2002, which is the first algorithm that can determine whether a number is prime or composite in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_complexity "Time complexity"). A proof of [Goldbach's weak conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach%27s_weak_conjecture "Goldbach's weak conjecture") was published by [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott "Harald Helfgott") in 2013; as of 2025, the proof has not yet been fully reviewed. The first [einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_problem "Einstein problem") was discovered in 2023.

In addition, a lot of work has been done toward long-lasting projects which began in the twentieth century. For example, the [classification of finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups "Classification of finite simple groups") was completed in 2008. Similarly, work on the [Langlands program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_program "Langlands program") has progressed significantly, and there have been proofs of the [fundamental lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_lemma_(Langlands_program) "Fundamental lemma (Langlands program)") (2008), as well as a proposed proof of the [geometric Langlands correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_Langlands_correspondence "Geometric Langlands correspondence") in 2024.

## Future

The future trajectory of mathematics is anticipated to be significantly influenced by the integration of advanced computational methodologies, though the exact epistemological consequences remain a subject of ongoing academic debate. The utilization of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") (AI) and automated theorem provers, such as [Rocq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocq "Rocq") and [Lean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_(proof_assistant) "Lean (proof assistant)"), is increasingly prominent in the formal verification of complex theorems. While proponents suggest this integration may enhance logical rigor, some scholars argue it could potentially obscure fundamental human comprehension if proofs become too computationally dense for independent human verification. Furthermore, the application of [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning") algorithms to identify heuristic patterns in fields like [knot theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_theory "Knot theory") is hypothesized to accelerate mathematical discovery; however, the reliance on algorithmic processes necessitates cautious epistemological scrutiny regarding verifiability.

In the realm of applied mathematics, the theoretical development of [quantum computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computing "Quantum computing") is widely projected to necessitate a paradigm shift in [cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography "Cryptography"). Algorithms such as [Shor's algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shor%27s_algorithm "Shor's algorithm") possess the theoretical capacity to efficiently factorize large prime numbers, which would ostensibly compromise widely used public-key cryptosystems like [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_(cryptosystem) "RSA (cryptosystem)"). Consequently, researchers are actively developing [post-quantum cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-quantum_cryptography "Post-quantum cryptography"), an area heavily reliant on [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry") and lattice-based structures. Nevertheless, the timeline for the practical engineering of scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computers remains highly uncertain, making the immediate urgency of this cryptographic transition a topic of theoretical speculation.

Additionally, the expanding availability of massive empirical datasets, commonly termed [Big data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_data "Big data"), has stimulated the development of [topological data analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_data_analysis "Topological data analysis") (TDA). This interdisciplinary field attempts to apply abstract geometric and topological concepts to identify structural features in noisy, high-dimensional data. Despite its potential, the application of deterministic mathematical models to inherently stochastic biological or sociological systems must be approached with critical caution to avoid conflating statistical anomalies with actual causal mechanisms.
