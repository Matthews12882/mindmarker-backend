# History of agriculture

[Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture") began independently in different parts of the globe, and included a diverse range of [taxa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxon "Taxon"). At least eleven separate regions of the [Old](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World "Old World") and [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World "New World") were involved as independent [centers of origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centers_of_origin "Centers of origin").
The development of agriculture about 12,000 years ago changed the way humans lived. They switched from nomadic hunter-gatherer lifestyles to permanent settlements and farming.

Wild [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal "Cereal") were collected and eaten from at least 104,000 years ago. However, domestication did not occur until much later. The earliest evidence of small-scale cultivation of edible grasses is from around 21,000 BC with the [Ohalo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohalo_II "Ohalo II") people on the shores of the [Sea of Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Galilee "Sea of Galilee"). By around 9500 BC, the eight [Neolithic founder crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_founder_crops "Neolithic founder crops") – [emmer wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer_wheat "Emmer wheat"), [einkorn wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einkorn_wheat "Einkorn wheat"), [hulled barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley"), [peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea "Pea"), [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil "Lentil"), [bitter vetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicia_ervilia "Vicia ervilia"), [chickpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickpea "Chickpea"), and [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax "Flax") – were cultivated in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"). [Rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye "Rye") may have been cultivated earlier, but this claim remains controversial. Regardless, rye's spread from Southwest Asia to the Atlantic was independent of the Neolithic founder crop package. [Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice "Rice") was domesticated in China by 6200 BC with earliest known cultivation from 5700 BC, followed by [mung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mung_bean "Mung bean"), [soy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soy "Soy") and [azuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azuki_bean "Azuki bean") beans. Rice was also independently domesticated in West Africa and cultivated by 1000 BC. [Pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig "Pig") were domesticated in Mesopotamia around 11,000 years ago, followed by [sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep "Sheep"). [Cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle "Cattle") were domesticated from the wild [aurochs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurochs "Aurochs") in the areas of modern Turkey and India around 8500 BC. [Camels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel "Camel") were domesticated late, perhaps around 3000 BC.

In [sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa"), [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum") was domesticated in the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") region of Africa by 3000 BC, along with [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet "Pearl millet") by 2000 BC. [Yams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_(vegetable) "Yam (vegetable)") were domesticated in several distinct locations, including West Africa (unknown date), and [cowpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowpea "Cowpea") by 2500 BC. [Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice "Rice") ([African rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oryza_glaberrima "Oryza glaberrima")) was also independently domesticated in West Africa and cultivated by 1000 BC. [Teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff "Teff") and likely [finger millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finger_millet "Finger millet") were domesticated in [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia") by 3000 BC, along with [noog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guizotia_abyssinica "Guizotia abyssinica"), [ensete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensete "Ensete"), and [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_bean "Coffee bean"). Other plant foods domesticated in Africa include [watermelon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon "Watermelon"), [okra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra "Okra"), [tamarind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamarind "Tamarind") and [black eyed peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_eyed_peas "Black eyed peas"), along with tree crops such as the [kola nut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_nut "Kola nut") and [oil palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaeis "Elaeis"). [Plantains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_plantains "True plantains") were cultivated in Africa by 3000 BC and [bananas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana "Banana") by 1500 BC. The helmeted [guineafowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guineafowl "Guineafowl") was domesticated in West Africa. [Sanga cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanga_cattle "Sanga cattle") was likely also domesticated in North-East Africa, around 7000 BC, and later crossbred with other species.

In South America, agriculture began as early as 9000 BC, starting with the cultivation of several species of plants that later became only minor crops. In the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes "Andes") of South America, the [potato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato "Potato") was domesticated between 8000 BC and 5000 BC, along with [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaseolus "Phaseolus"), [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_(plant) "Squash (plant)"), [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomatoes "Tomatoes"), [peanuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peanut "Peanut"), [coca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca "Coca"), [llamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llama "Llama"), [alpacas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpaca "Alpaca"), and [guinea pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_pig "Guinea pig"). [Cassava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassava "Cassava") was domesticated in the Amazon Basin no later than 7000 BC. [Maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") (*Zea mays*) found its way to South America from [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"), where wild [teosinte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teosinte "Teosinte") was domesticated about 7000 BC and [selectively bred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding "Selective breeding") to become domestic maize. [Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton "Cotton") was domesticated in [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru") by 4200 BC; another species of cotton was domesticated in Mesoamerica and became by far the most important species of cotton in the textile industry in modern times. Evidence of agriculture in the [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Agricultural_Complex "Eastern Agricultural Complex") United States dates to about 3000 BCE. Several plants were cultivated, later to be replaced by the [Three Sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Sisters_(agriculture) "Three Sisters (agriculture)") cultivation of maize, squash, and beans.

[Sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane "Sugarcane") and some [root vegetables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_root_vegetables "List of root vegetables") were domesticated in [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea") around 7000 BC. [Bananas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana "Banana") were cultivated and [hybridized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_(biology) "Hybrid (biology)") in the same period in [Papua New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea "Papua New Guinea"). In [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia "Australia"), agriculture was invented at a currently unspecified period, with the oldest eel traps of [Budj Bim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budj_Bim "Budj Bim") dating to 6,600 BC and the deployment of several crops ranging from [murnong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murnong "Murnong") to bananas.

The [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age"), from c. 3300 BC, witnessed the intensification of agriculture in civilizations such as [Mesopotamian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") [Sumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer"), [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"), [ancient Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerma "Kerma"), the [Indus Valley civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilisation "Indus Valley civilisation") of the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"), [ancient China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_China "Ancient China"), and [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"). From 100 BC to 1600 AD, world population continued to grow along with land use, as evidenced by the rapid increase in [methane emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_emissions "Methane emissions") from cattle and the cultivation of rice. During the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age "Iron Age") and era of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), the expansion of [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome"), both [the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic") and then [the Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"), throughout the [ancient Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Mediterranean "Ancient Mediterranean") and [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe "Western Europe") built upon existing systems of agriculture while also establishing the [manorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorial "Manorial") system that became a bedrock of medieval agriculture. In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), both in Europe and [in the Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Agricultural_Revolution "Arab Agricultural Revolution"), agriculture was transformed with improved techniques and the diffusion of crop plants, including the introduction of sugar, rice, cotton and fruit trees such as the [orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_(fruit) "Orange (fruit)") to Europe by way of [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus "Al-Andalus"). After the [voyages of Christopher Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyages_of_Christopher_Columbus "Voyages of Christopher Columbus") in 1492, the [Columbian exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange "Columbian exchange") brought New World crops such as [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize"), [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato "Potato"), [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato "Tomato"), [sweet potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato "Sweet potato"), and [manioc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manioc "Manioc") to Europe, and Old World crops such as wheat, barley, rice, and [turnips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnip "Turnip"), and livestock including horses, cattle, sheep, and goats to the Americas.

[Irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation"), [crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation "Crop rotation"), and [fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer "Fertilizer") were introduced soon after the [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution") and developed much further in the past 200 years, starting with the [British Agricultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Agricultural_Revolution "British Agricultural Revolution"). Since 1900, agriculture in the developed nations, and to a lesser extent in the developing world, has seen large rises in productivity as human labour has been replaced by [mechanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanized_farming "Mechanized farming"), and assisted by synthetic fertilizers, [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide "Pesticide"), and [selective breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding "Selective breeding"). The [Haber-Bosch process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process "Haber process") allowed the synthesis of [ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate "Ammonium nitrate") fertilizer on an industrial scale, greatly increasing [crop yields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yields "Crop yields"). Modern agriculture has raised social, political, and environmental issues including [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution "Water pollution"), [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel "Biofuel"), [genetically modified organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetically_modified_organism "Genetically modified organism"), [tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariff "Tariff") and [farm subsidies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_subsidies "Farm subsidies"). In response, [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming "Organic farming") developed in the twentieth century as an alternative to the use of synthetic pesticides.

## Origins

### Origin hypotheses

Scholars have developed a number of hypotheses to explain the historical origins of agriculture. Studies of the transition from [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") to agricultural societies indicate an antecedent period of intensification and increasing [sedentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedentism "Sedentism"); examples are the [Natufian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natufian_culture "Natufian culture") in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") and the Early Chinese Neolithic in China. Current models indicate that wild stands that had been harvested previously started to be planted, but were not immediately domesticated.

Localised climate change is the favoured explanation for the [origins of agriculture in the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origins_of_agriculture_in_West_Asia "Origins of agriculture in West Asia"). When major climate change took place after the last [ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age "Ice age") (c. 11,000 BC), much of the earth became subject to long dry seasons. These conditions favoured [annual plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") which die off in the long dry season, leaving a [dormant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormancy "Dormancy") [seed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed "Seed") or [tuber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuber "Tuber"). An abundance of readily storable wild grains and [pulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legume "Legume") enabled hunter-gatherers in some areas to form the first settled villages at this time.

Across Western Eurasia it was not until approximately 4,000 BC that farming societies completely replaced hunter-gatherers. These technologically advanced societies expanded faster in areas with less forest, pushing hunter-gatherers into denser woodlands. Only the middle-late Bronze Age and Iron Age societies were able to fully replace hunter-gatherers in their final stronghold located in the most densely forested areas. Unlike their Bronze and Iron Age counterparts, Neolithic societies could not establish themselves in dense forests, and Copper Age societies had only limited success.

### Early development

Early people began altering communities of [flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora "Flora") and [fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna "Fauna") for their own benefit through means such as [fire-stick farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-stick_farming "Fire-stick farming") and [forest gardening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_gardening "Forest gardening") very early. Wild [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal "Cereal") have been collected and eaten from at least 105,000 years ago, and possibly much longer. Exact dates are hard to determine, as people collected and ate seeds before domesticating them, and plant characteristics may have changed during this period without human selection. An example is the semi-tough [rachis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachis "Rachis") and larger seeds of [cereals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal "Cereal") from just after the [Younger Dryas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Dryas "Younger Dryas") (about 9500 BC) in the early [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene "Holocene") in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") region of the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent"). [Monophyletic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophyletic "Monophyletic") characteristics were attained without any human intervention, implying that apparent domestication of the cereal [rachis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachis "Rachis") could have occurred quite naturally.

Agriculture began independently in different parts of the globe and included a diverse range of taxa. At least 11 separate regions of the Old and New World were involved as independent [centers of origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centers_of_origin "Centers of origin"). Some of the earliest known domestications were of animals. [Domestic pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_pig "Domestic pig") had multiple centres of origin in Eurasia, including Europe, East Asia and Southwest Asia, where [wild boar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_boar "Wild boar") were first domesticated about 10,500 years ago. [Sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep "Sheep") were domesticated in Mesopotamia between 11,000 BC and 9000 BC. [Cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle "Cattle") were domesticated from the wild [aurochs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurochs "Aurochs") in the areas of modern Turkey and India around 8500 BC. [Camels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camels "Camels") were domesticated relatively late, perhaps around 3000 BC.

It was not until after 9500 BC that the eight so-called [founder crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_founder_crops "Neolithic founder crops") of agriculture appear: first [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer_wheat "Emmer wheat") and [einkorn wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einkorn_wheat "Einkorn wheat"), then hulled [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley"), [peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea "Pea"), [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil "Lentil"), [bitter vetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicia_ervilia "Vicia ervilia"), [chick peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chick_pea "Chick pea") and [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax "Flax"). These eight crops occur more or less simultaneously on Pre-Pottery Neolithic B ([PPNB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_B "Pre-Pottery Neolithic B")) sites in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"), although [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat "Wheat") may have been the first to be grown and harvested on a significant scale. At around the same time (9400 BC), [parthenocarpic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenocarpic "Parthenocarpic") [fig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ficus "Ficus") trees were domesticated.

Domesticated [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye "Rye") occurs in small quantities at some [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") sites in (Asia Minor) Turkey, such as the [Pre-Pottery Neolithic B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_B "Pre-Pottery Neolithic B") (c. 7600 – c. 6000 BC) Can Hasan III near [Çatalhöyük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk "Çatalhöyük"), but is otherwise absent until the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") of central Europe, c. 1800–1500 BC. Claims of much earlier cultivation of rye, at the [Epipalaeolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipalaeolithic "Epipalaeolithic") site of [Tell Abu Hureyra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Abu_Hureyra "Tell Abu Hureyra") in the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates") valley of northern [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria"), remain controversial. Critics point to inconsistencies in the [radiocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon "Radiocarbon") dates, and identifications based solely on grain, rather than on [chaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaff "Chaff").

By 8000 BC, farming was entrenched on the banks of the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile"). About this time, agriculture was developed independently in the Far East, probably in China, with rice rather than wheat as the primary crop. Maize was domesticated from the wild grass [teosinte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teosinte "Teosinte") in southern Mexico by 6700 BC.
The [potato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato "Potato") (8000 BC), [tomato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato "Tomato"), [pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsicum "Capsicum"), [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_(fruit) "Squash (fruit)"), and some varieties of [bean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legume "Legume") were domesticated in the New World.

Agriculture was independently developed on the island of [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea"). [Banana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana "Banana") cultivation of *[Musa acuminata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musa_acuminata "Musa acuminata")*, including [hybridization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_(biology) "Hybrid (biology)"), dates back to 5000 BC, and possibly to 8000 BC, in [Papua New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea "Papua New Guinea").

[Bees were kept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beekeeping "Beekeeping") for honey in the Middle East around 7000 BC. Archaeological evidence from various sites on the [Iberian peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_peninsula "Iberian peninsula") suggest the domestication of plants and animals between 6000 and 4500 BC. Ireland's [Céide Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A9ide_Fields "Céide Fields") consist of tracts of land enclosed by stone walls built around 3500 BC, representing Europe's oldest field systems. The [horse was domesticated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse "Domestication of the horse") in the [Pontic steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontic_steppe "Pontic steppe") around 4000 BC In [Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberia "Siberia"). [Cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis "Cannabis") was in use in China in Neolithic times and may have been domesticated there; it was in use both as a fibre for ropemaking and as a medicine in Ancient Egypt by about 2350 BC.

In northern [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China"), [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet "Millet") was domesticated by early [Sino-Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan "Sino-Tibetan") speakers at around 8000 to 6000 BC, becoming the main crop of the [Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River "Yellow River") basin by 5500 BC.

In southern China, rice was domesticated in the [Yangtze River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze_River "Yangtze River") basin at around 11,500 to 6200 BC, along with the development of [wetland agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland_agriculture "Wetland agriculture"), by early [Austronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_peoples "Austronesian peoples") and [Hmong-Mien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong-Mien_languages "Hmong-Mien languages")-speakers. Other food plants were also harvested, including [acorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acorn "Acorn"), [water chestnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleocharis_dulcis "Eleocharis dulcis"), and [foxnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxnut "Foxnut"). Rice cultivation was later spread to [Maritime Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_Southeast_Asia "Island Southeast Asia") by the [Austronesian expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_expansion "Austronesian expansion"), starting at around 3,500 to 2,000 BC. This migration event also saw the introduction of cultivated and domesticated food plants from [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan"), [Maritime Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_Southeast_Asia "Island Southeast Asia"), and [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea") into the [Pacific Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Islands "Pacific Islands") as [canoe plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_plants "Canoe plants"). Contact with [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") and [Southern India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_India "Southern India") by Austronesian sailors also led to an exchange of food plants which later became the origin of the valuable [spice trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_trade "Spice trade"). In the 1st millennium AD, Austronesian sailors also settled [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar "Madagascar") and the [Comoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoros "Comoros"), bringing Southeast Asian and South Asian food plants with them to the [East African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa") coast, including bananas and rice. Rice was also spread southwards into [Mainland Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia "Mainland Southeast Asia") by around 2000 to 1500 BC by the migrations of the early [Austroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages "Austroasiatic languages") and [Kra-Dai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kra-Dai "Kra-Dai")-speakers.

In the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") region of [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"), [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum") was domesticated by 3000 BC in Sudan and [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet "Pearl millet") by 2500 BC in Mali. [Kola nut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_nut "Kola nut") and [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee "Coffee") were also domesticated in Africa. In [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea"), ancient [Papuan peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_people_of_New_Guinea "Indigenous people of New Guinea") began practicing agriculture around 7000 BC, domesticating [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane "Sugarcane") and [taro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taro "Taro"). In the [Indus Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley "Indus Valley") from the eighth millennium BC onwards at [Mehrgarh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrgarh "Mehrgarh"), 2-row and 6-row barley were cultivated, along with einkorn, emmer, and durum wheats, and dates. In the earliest levels of Merhgarh, wild game such as [gazelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazelle "Gazelle"), [swamp deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swamp_deer "Swamp deer"), [blackbuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbuck "Blackbuck"), [chital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chital "Chital"), [wild ass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onager "Onager"), wild goat, wild sheep, [boar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boar "Boar"), and [nilgai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilgai "Nilgai") were all hunted for food. These are successively replaced by domesticated sheep, goats, and humped [zebu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebu "Zebu") cattle by the fifth millennium BC, indicating the gradual transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture.

[Maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") and [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_(plant) "Squash (plant)") were domesticated in [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"); [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato "Potato") in South America, and [sunflowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower "Sunflower") in the Eastern Woodlands of North America.

## Civilizations

### Sumer

[Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") farmers grew the cereals [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley") and [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat "Wheat"), starting to live in villages from about 8000 BC. Given the low rainfall of the region, agriculture relied on the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris") and [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates") rivers. Irrigation canals leading from the rivers permitted the growth of cereals in large enough quantities to support cities. The first ploughs appear in [pictographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictograph "Pictograph") from Uruk around 3000 BC; seed-ploughs that funneled seed into the ploughed furrow appear on [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_(emblem) "Seal (emblem)") around 2300 BC. Vegetable crops included [chickpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickpea "Chickpea"), [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil "Lentil"), peas, beans, [onions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onion "Onion"), [garlic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garlic "Garlic"), [lettuce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lettuce "Lettuce"), [leeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leek "Leek") and [mustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustard_plant "Mustard plant"). They grew fruits including [dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_(fruit) "Date (fruit)"), grapes, apples, melons, and figs. Alongside their farming, Sumerians also caught fish and hunted [fowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fowl "Fowl") and [gazelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazelle "Gazelle"). The meat of sheep, goats, cows and poultry was eaten, mainly by the elite. Fish was preserved by drying, salting and smoking.

### Ancient Egypt

The civilization of [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") was indebted to the [Nile River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_River "Nile River") and its dependable seasonal flooding. The river's predictability and the fertile soil allowed the Egyptians to build an empire on the basis of great agricultural wealth. Egyptians were among the first peoples to practice agriculture on a large scale, starting in the pre-dynastic period from the end of the Paleolithic into the Neolithic, between around 10,000 BC and 4000 BC. This was made possible with the development of basin irrigation. Their staple food crops were grains such as wheat and barley, alongside industrial crops such as [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax "Flax") and [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus "Papyrus"). Archaeological evidence also suggests that the spread of agriculture in Egypt was facilitated by farming communities associated with the playa lakes of the Sahara some 6,500 years ago.

### Indian Subcontinent

[Jujube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jujube "Jujube") was domesticated in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent") by 9000 BC. Barley and wheat cultivation – along with the domestication of cattle, primarily sheep and goats – followed in [Mehrgarh culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrgarh "Mehrgarh") by 8000–6000 BC. This period also saw the first domestication of the [elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant "Elephant"). [Pastoral farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral_farming "Pastoral farming") in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_(Herodotus) "India (Herodotus)") included threshing, planting crops in rows – either of two or of six – and storing grain in [granaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granary "Granary"). [Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton "Cotton") was cultivated by the 5th–4th millennium BC. By the 5th millennium BC, agricultural communities became widespread in [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir "Kashmir"). Irrigation was developed in the [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation "Indus Valley Civilisation") by around 4500 BC. The size and prosperity of the Indus civilization grew as a result of this innovation, leading to more thoroughly planned settlements which used [drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage "Drainage") and [sewers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitary_sewer "Sanitary sewer"). Archeological evidence of an animal-drawn [plough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough "Plough") dates back to 2500 BC in the Indus Valley Civilization.

### Ancient China

Records from the [Warring States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period "Warring States period"), [Qin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_dynasty "Qin dynasty"), and [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") provide a picture of early [Chinese agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_China "Agriculture in China") from the 5th century BC to 2nd century AD which included a nationwide [granary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granary "Granary") system and widespread use of [sericulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sericulture "Sericulture"). An important early Chinese book on agriculture is the [Qimin Yaoshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qimin_Yaoshu "Qimin Yaoshu") of AD 535, written by Jia Sixie. Jia's writing style was straightforward and lucid relative to the elaborate and allusive writing typical of the time. Jia's book was also very long, with over one hundred thousand written [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters "Chinese characters"), and it quoted many other Chinese books that were written previously, but no longer survive. The contents of Jia's 6th century book include sections on land preparation, seeding, cultivation, orchard management, forestry, and animal husbandry. The book also includes peripherally related content covering trade and culinary uses for crops. The work and the style in which it was written proved influential on later Chinese [agronomists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agronomist "Agronomist"), such as [Wang Zhen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Zhen_(official) "Wang Zhen (official)") and his groundbreaking *Nong Shu* of 1313.

For agricultural purposes, the Chinese had innovated the [hydraulic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic "Hydraulic")-powered [trip hammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trip_hammer "Trip hammer") by the 1st century BC. Although it found other purposes, its main function to pound, decorticate, and polish grain that otherwise would have been done manually. The Chinese also began using the square-pallet [chain pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_pump "Chain pump") by the 1st century AD, powered by a [waterwheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterwheel "Waterwheel") or [oxen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxen "Oxen") pulling an on a system of mechanical wheels. Although the chain pump found use in [public works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_works "Public works") of providing water for urban and palatial [pipe systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport "Pipeline transport"), it was used largely to lift water from a lower to higher elevation in filling [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation") [canals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal "Canal") and [channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_(geography) "Channel (geography)") for [farmland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land "Arable land"). By the end of the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") in the late 2nd century, [heavy ploughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_plough "Heavy plough") had been developed with iron ploughshares and [mouldboards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouldboard "Mouldboard"). These slowly spread west, revolutionizing farming in Northern Europe by the 10th century. ([Thomas Glick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Glick "Thomas Glick"), however, argues for a development of the Chinese plough as late as the 9th century, implying its spread east from similar designs known in Italy by the 7th century.)

Asian rice was domesticated 13,500 to 8,200 years ago in China, with a single genetic origin from the wild rice *[Oryza rufipogon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oryza_rufipogon "Oryza rufipogon")*, in the [Pearl River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_River_(China) "Pearl River (China)") valley region of China. Rice cultivation then spread to South and Southeast Asia.

### Ancient Greece and Hellenistic world

The major cereal crops of the [ancient Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Mediterranean "Ancient Mediterranean") region were wheat, [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer"), and barley, while common vegetables included peas, beans, [fava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicia_faba "Vicia faba"), and olives, dairy products came mostly from sheep and goats, and meat, which was consumed on rare occasion for most people, usually consisted of pork, beef, and lamb. [Agriculture in ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_ancient_Greece "Agriculture in ancient Greece") was hindered by the [topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography "Topography") of [mainland Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Greece "Mainland Greece") that only allowed for roughly 10% of the land to be cultivated properly, necessitating the specialised exportation of [oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking_oil "Cooking oil") and [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_wine "History of wine") and importation of grains from [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace") (centered in what is now [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Bulgaria "History of Bulgaria")) and the [Greek colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonies_in_antiquity "Colonies in antiquity") of [Pontic Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontic_Greeks "Pontic Greeks") near the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea"). During the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"), the [Ptolemaic Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty "Ptolemaic dynasty") controlled [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom "Ptolemaic Kingdom"), [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus"), [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia"), and [Cyrenaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica "Cyrenaica"), major grain-producing regions that mainland [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") depended on for subsistence, while the Ptolemaic [grain market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_trade "Grain trade") also played a critical role in [the rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_of_Rome "Rise of Rome") of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic"). In the [Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire "Seleucid Empire"), Mesopotamia was a crucial area for the production of wheat, while [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad") [animal husbandry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_husbandry "Animal husbandry") was also practiced in other parts.

### Roman Empire

In the [Greco-Roman world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world "Greco-Roman world") of [Classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), [Roman agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_agriculture "Roman agriculture") was built on techniques originally pioneered by the Sumerians, transmitted to them by subsequent cultures, with a specific emphasis on the cultivation of crops for trade and export. [The Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") laid the groundwork for the [manorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorial "Manorial") economic system, involving [serfdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfdom "Serfdom"), which flourished in the Middle Ages. The farm sizes in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Rome "History of Rome") can be divided into three categories. Small farms were from 18 to 88 iugera (one iugerum is equal to about 0.65 acre). Medium-sized farms were from 80 to 500 iugera (singular [iugerum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iugerum "Iugerum")). Large estates (called [latifundia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latifundia "Latifundia")) were over 500 iugera. [The Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") had four systems of farm management: direct work by the owner and his family; slaves doing work under the supervision of slave managers; [tenant farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenant_farmer "Tenant farmer") or [sharecropping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharecropping "Sharecropping") in which the owner and a tenant divide up a farm's produce; and situations in which a farm was leased to a tenant.

### The Americas

Agricultural history took a different path from the [Old World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World "Old World") as the Americas lacked large-seeded, easily domesticated grains (such as wheat and barley) and large domestic animals that could be used for agricultural labor. Rather than the practice which developed in the Old World of sowing a field with a single crop, pre-historic American agriculture usually consisted of cultivating many crops close to each other utilizing only hand labor. Moreover, agricultural areas in the Americas lacked the uniformity of the east–west area of [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate "Mediterranean climate") and [semi-arid climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate "Semi-arid climate") in southern Europe and southwestern Asia, but instead had a north–south pattern with a variety of different climatic zones in close proximity to each other. This fostered the domestication of many different plants.

At the time of first contact between the Europeans and the Americans, the Europeans practiced "extensive agriculture, based on the plough and draught animals," with tenants under landlords, but also forced labor or slavery, while the [Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas "Indigenous peoples of the Americas") practiced "intensive agriculture, based on human labour." Europeans wanted control of land for the grazing of their livestock and property rights for the control of production. Though they were impressed with the productivity of traditional farming techniques, they saw no connection to their system and were dismissive of Native American practices as "gardening" rather than a commercializable enterprise. Due to several thousand years of selective breeding, [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize"), the hemisphere's most important crop, was more productive than Old World grain crops. Maize produced two and one-half times more calories per acre than wheat and barley.

#### South America

The earliest known areas of possible agriculture in the Americas dating to about 9000 BC are in [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombia "Colombia"), near present-day [Pereira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pereira,_Colombia "Pereira, Colombia"), and by the [Las Vegas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Vegas_culture_(archaeology) "Las Vegas culture (archaeology)") culture in [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecuador "Ecuador") on the [Santa Elena peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Elena_Province "Santa Elena Province"). The plants cultivated (or manipulated by humans) were [lerén](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goeppertia_allouia "Goeppertia allouia") (*Calathea allouia*), [arrowroot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maranta_arundinacea "Maranta arundinacea") (*Maranta arundinacea*), [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_(plant) "Squash (plant)") (*Cucurbita* species), and [bottle gourd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabash "Calabash") (*Lagenaria siceraria*). All are plants of humid climates and their existence at this time on the semi-arid Santa Elena peninsula may be evidence that they were transplanted there from more humid environments. In another study, this area of South America was identified as one of the four oldest places of origin for agriculture, along with the Fertile Crescent, China, and Mesoamerica, dated between 6200 BC and 10000 BC. (To facilitate comprehension by readers, [Radiocarbon calibrated BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating "Radiocarbon dating") dates in the above sources have been converted to BC.)

In the Andes region, with civilizations including the [Inca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incan_agriculture "Incan agriculture"), the major crop was the [potato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato "Potato"), domesticated between 8000 and 5000 BC. [Coca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca "Coca"), still a major crop to this day, was domesticated in the Andes, as were the [peanut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peanut "Peanut"), [tomato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato "Tomato"), [tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco "Tobacco"), and [pineapple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pineapple "Pineapple"). [Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton "Cotton") was domesticated in [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru") by 4200 BC. [Animals were also domesticated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incan_animal_husbandry "Incan animal husbandry"), including [llamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llama "Llama"), [alpacas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpaca "Alpaca"), and [guinea pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_pig "Guinea pig"). The people of the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire "Inca Empire") of South America grew large surpluses of food which they stored in buildings called [Qullqas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qullqa "Qullqa").

The most important crop domesticated in the [Amazon Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Basin "Amazon Basin") and tropical lowlands was probably [cassava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassava "Cassava") ([Manihot esculenta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manihot_esculenta "Manihot esculenta")), which was domesticated before 7000 BCE, likely in the [Rondônia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rond%C3%B4nia "Rondônia") and [Mato Grosso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mato_Grosso "Mato Grosso") states of [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil "Brazil"). The [Guaitecas Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guaitecas_Archipelago "Guaitecas Archipelago") in modern Chile was the southern limit of [Pre-Hispanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Hispanic "Pre-Hispanic") agriculture near 44° South latitude, as noted by the mention of the cultivation of [Chiloé potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potatoes_of_Chilo%C3%A9 "Potatoes of Chiloé") by a Spanish expedition in 1557.

#### Mesoamerica

In Mesoamerica, wild [teosinte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teosinte "Teosinte") was transformed through human selection into the ancestor of modern maize, about 7,000 BC. It gradually spread across North America and to South America and was the most important crop of Native Americans at the time of European exploration. Other Mesoamerican crops include hundreds of varieties of locally domesticated [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_(plant) "Squash (plant)") and [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean "Bean"), while [cocoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theobroma_cacao "Theobroma cacao"), also domesticated in the region, was a major crop. The [turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey_(bird) "Turkey (bird)"), one of the most important poultry birds, was probably domesticated in Mexico or the U.S. Southwest.

In [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"), the [Aztecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec "Aztec") were active farmers and had an agriculturally focused economy. The land around [Lake Texcoco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Texcoco "Lake Texcoco") was fertile, but not large enough to produce the amount of food needed for the population of their expanding empire. The Aztecs developed irrigation systems, formed [terraced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrace_(agriculture) "Terrace (agriculture)") hillsides, fertilized their soil, and developed [chinampas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinampa "Chinampa") or artificial islands, also known as "floating gardens". The [Mayas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") between 400 BC to 900 AD used extensive canal and raised field systems to farm swampland on the [Yucatán Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucat%C3%A1n_Peninsula "Yucatán Peninsula").

#### North America

The indigenous people of the [Eastern U.S. domesticated numerous crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Agricultural_Complex "Eastern Agricultural Complex"). [Sunflowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflowers "Sunflowers"), [tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco "Tobacco"), varieties of squash and *[Chenopodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenopodium "Chenopodium")*, as well as crops no longer grown, including [marsh elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iva_annua "Iva annua") and [little barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hordeum_pusillum "Hordeum pusillum"). Wild foods including [wild rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_rice "Wild rice") and [maple sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_sugar "Maple sugar") were harvested. The domesticated [strawberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberry "Strawberry") is a hybrid of a Chilean and a North American species, developed by breeding in Europe and North America. Two major crops, [pecans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pecan "Pecan") and [Concord grapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concord_grape "Concord grape"), were used extensively in prehistoric times but do not appear to have been domesticated until the 19th century.

The [indigenous people in what is now California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_the_prehistoric_Southwest "Agriculture in the prehistoric Southwest") and the Pacific Northwest practiced various forms of [forest gardening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_gardening "Forest gardening") and [fire-stick farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-stick_farming "Fire-stick farming") in the forests, grasslands, mixed woodlands, and wetlands, ensuring that desired food and medicine plants continued to be available. The [natives controlled fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire "Native American use of fire") on a regional scale to create a low-intensity [fire ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_ecology "Fire ecology") which prevented larger, catastrophic fires and [sustained a low-density agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_agriculture "Sustainable agriculture") in loose rotation; a sort of "wild" [permaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permaculture "Permaculture").

A system of [companion planting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_planting "Companion planting") called [the Three Sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Sisters_(agriculture) "Three Sisters (agriculture)") was [developed in North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_on_the_prehistoric_Great_Plains "Agriculture on the prehistoric Great Plains"). Three crops that complemented each other were planted together: [winter squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_squash "Winter squash"), [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") (corn), and climbing [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean "Bean") (typically [tepary beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaseolus_acutifolius "Phaseolus acutifolius") or [common beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaseolus_vulgaris "Phaseolus vulgaris")). The maize provides a structure for the beans to climb, eliminating the need for poles. The beans provide the [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen") to the soil that the other plants use, and the squash spreads along the ground, blocking the [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight "Sunlight"), helping prevent the establishment of [weeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed "Weed"). The squash leaves also act as a "living [mulch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulch "Mulch")".

### Sub-Saharan Africa

In the Sahel region, civilizations such as the [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire "Mali Empire") and [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_Empire "Songhai Empire") empires cultivated [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum") and [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet "Pearl millet"), which were domesticated between 3000 and 2500 BC. The [donkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey "Donkey") was domesticated in [Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia") at approximately 5000 BC. Archaeological evidence suggests that [Sanga cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanga_cattle "Sanga cattle") may have been independently domesticated in [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa") at around 1600 BC.

In the tropical region of [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"), crops such as [black-eyed peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-eyed_pea "Black-eyed pea"), [Sea Island red peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Island_red_pea "Sea Island red pea"), [yams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_(vegetable) "Yam (vegetable)"), [kola nuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_nut "Kola nut"), [Jollof rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jollof_rice "Jollof rice") and [kokoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kokoro_(vegetable) "Kokoro (vegetable)") were domesticated between 3000 and 1000 BC. The coastal region of West Africa is often referred to as the "Yam Belt", due to its high production of yams. The [guineafowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_guineafowl "Domestic guineafowl") is a poultry bird that was domesticated in West Africa, and while the time of the guineafowl's domestication remains unclear, there is evidence that it was present in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") during the 5th century BC.

Several species of [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee "Coffee") were also domesticated throughout Sub-Saharan Africa, with *[Coffea arabica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffea_arabica "Coffea arabica")* originating in [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia") and serving as the main production of modern-day coffee since the late 15th century.

### Oceania

#### Australia

[Indigenous Australians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_Australians "Indigenous Australians") were predominately nomadic [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherers "Hunter-gatherers"). Due to the policy of *[terra nullius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_nullius "Terra nullius")*, Aboriginals were regarded as not having been capable of sustained agriculture. However, the current consensus is that various agricultural methods were employed by the indigenous people.

In two regions of Central Australia, the central west coast and eastern central Australia, forms of agriculture were practiced. People living in permanent settlements of over 200 residents sowed or planted on a large scale and stored the harvested food. The Nhanda and Amangu of the central west coast grew yams (*[Dioscorea hastifolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioscorea "Dioscorea")*), while various groups in eastern central Australia (the Corners Region) planted and harvested bush onions (*yaua* – *[Cyperus bulbosus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyperus_bulbosus "Cyperus bulbosus")*), native millet (*cooly, tindil* – *[Panicum decompositum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panicum_decompositum "Panicum decompositum")*) and a [sporocarp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporocarp_(ferns) "Sporocarp (ferns)"), *ngardu* (*[Marsilea drummondii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsilea_drummondii "Marsilea drummondii")*).

Indigenous Australians used systematic burning, [fire-stick farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-stick_farming "Fire-stick farming"), to enhance natural productivity. In the 1970s and 1980s archaeological research in south west Victoria established that the [Gunditjmara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunditjmara "Gunditjmara") and other groups had developed sophisticated eel farming and fish trapping systems over a period of nearly 5,000 years. The archaeologist [Harry Lourandos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Lourandos "Harry Lourandos") suggested in the 1980s that there was evidence of 'intensification' in progress across Australia, a process that appeared to have continued through the preceding 5,000 years. These concepts led the historian [Bill Gammage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gammage "Bill Gammage") to argue that in effect the whole continent was a managed landscape.

Torres Strait Islanders are now known to have planted [bananas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana "Banana").

#### Pacific Islands

In [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea"), archaeological evidence suggests that agriculture independently emerged around 7,000 years ago with the domestication of crops such as bananas and [taro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taro "Taro"). Pigs and chickens were imported to New Guinea, which were later innovated by other Pacific Island nations, such as those in [Polynesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesia "Polynesia").

## Middle Ages and Early Modern period

### Europe

The [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") saw further improvements in agriculture. [Monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastery "Monastery") spread throughout [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") and became important centers for the collection of knowledge related to agriculture and forestry. The [manorial system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorial_system "Manorial system") allowed large landowners to control their land and its laborers, in the form of [peasants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant "Peasant") or [serfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serf "Serf"). During the medieval period, the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world "Arab world") was critical in the exchange of crops and technology between the European, Asia and African continents. Besides transporting numerous crops, they introduced the concept of summer irrigation to Europe and developed the beginnings of the [plantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantation "Plantation") system of [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane "Sugarcane") growing through the use of slaves for intensive cultivation.

By AD 900, developments in [iron smelting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smelting "Smelting") allowed for increased production in Europe, leading to developments in the production of agricultural implements such as [ploughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough "Plough"), [hand tools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_tool "Hand tool") and [horse shoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_shoe "Horse shoe"). The [carruca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carruca "Carruca") heavy plough improved on the earlier [scratch plough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_plough "Scratch plough"), with the adoption of the Chinese [mouldboard plough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouldboard_plough "Mouldboard plough") to turn over the heavy, wet soils of northern Europe. This led to the clearing of northern European forests and an increase in agricultural production, which in turn led to an increase in population. At the same time, some farmers in Europe moved from a two field [crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation "Crop rotation") to a three-field crop rotation in which one field of three was left fallow every year. This resulted in increased productivity and nutrition, as the change in rotations permitted [nitrogen-fixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen-fixing "Nitrogen-fixing") [legumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legumes "Legumes") such as peas, lentils and beans. Improved [horse harnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_harness "Horse harness") and the [whippletree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whippletree_(mechanism) "Whippletree (mechanism)") further improved cultivation.

[Watermills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill "Watermill") were introduced by the Romans, but were improved throughout the Middle Ages, along with [windmills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill "Windmill"), and used to grind grains into flour, to cut wood and to process flax and wool.

Crops included wheat, [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye "Rye"), barley and [oats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oats "Oats"). Peas, beans, and [vetches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vetches "Vetches") became common from the 13th century onward as a [fodder crop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fodder_crop "Fodder crop") for animals and also for their [nitrogen-fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen-fixation "Nitrogen-fixation") fertilizing properties. Crop yields peaked in the 13th century, and stayed more or less steady until the 18th century. Though the limitations of medieval farming were once thought to have provided a ceiling for the population growth in the Middle Ages, recent studies have shown that the technology of medieval agriculture was always sufficient for the needs of the people under normal circumstances, and that it was only during exceptionally harsh times, such as the [terrible weather of 1315–17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_of_1315%E2%80%931317 "Great Famine of 1315–1317"), that the needs of the population could not be met.

### Arab world

From the 8th century to the 14th century, the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age") underwent a transformation in agricultural practice, described by the historian Andrew Watson as the [Arab agricultural revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_agricultural_revolution "Arab agricultural revolution"). This transformation was driven by a number of factors including the diffusion of many crops and plants along Muslim trade routes, the spread of more advanced farming techniques, and an agricultural-economic system which promoted increased yields and efficiency. The shift in agricultural practice changed the economy, [population distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_distribution "Population distribution"), vegetation cover, agricultural production, population levels, [urban growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization "Urbanization"), the distribution of the labour force, cooking, diet, and clothing across the Islamic world. Muslim traders covered much of the [Old World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World "Old World"), and trade enabled the diffusion of many crops, plants and farming techniques across the region, as well as the adaptation of crops, plants and techniques from beyond the Islamic world. This diffusion introduced major crops to Europe by way of [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus "Al-Andalus"), along with the techniques for their cultivation and cuisine. Sugar cane, rice, and cotton were among the major crops transferred, along with [citrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrus "Citrus") and other fruit trees, nut trees, vegetables such as [aubergine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aubergine "Aubergine"), [spinach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinach "Spinach") and [chard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chard "Chard"), and the use of imported spices such as [cumin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumin "Cumin"), [coriander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander "Coriander"), [nutmeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutmeg "Nutmeg") and [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon "Cinnamon"). Intensive irrigation, [crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation "Crop rotation"), and agricultural manuals were widely adopted. Irrigation, partly based on Roman technology, made use of [noria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noria "Noria") water wheels, [water mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_mill "Water mill"), dams and reservoirs.

### Columbian exchange

After 1492, a [global exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange "Columbian exchange") of previously local crops and livestock breeds occurred. Maize, potatoes, [sweet potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potatoes "Sweet potatoes") and [manioc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manioc "Manioc") were the key crops that spread from the New World to the Old, while varieties of wheat, barley, rice and [turnips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnip "Turnip") traveled from the Old World to the New. There had been few livestock species in the New World, with horses, cattle, sheep and goats being completely unknown before their arrival with Old World settlers. Crops moving in both directions across the [Atlantic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean "Atlantic Ocean") caused population growth around the world and a lasting effect on many cultures in the [Early Modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period "Early modern period").

Maize and cassava were introduced from Brazil into Africa by Portuguese traders in the 16th century, becoming staple foods, replacing native African crops. After its introduction from South America to Spain in the late 1500s, the potato became a staple crop throughout Europe by the late 1700s. The potato allowed farmers to produce more food, and initially added variety to the European diet. The increased supply of food reduced disease, increased births and reduced mortality, causing a population boom throughout the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire"), the US and Europe. The introduction of the potato also brought about the first intensive use of fertilizer, in the form of [guano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guano "Guano") imported to Europe from Peru, and the first artificial pesticide, in the form of an [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic "Arsenic") compound used to fight [Colorado potato beetles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_potato_beetle "Colorado potato beetle"). Before the adoption of the potato as a major crop, the dependence on grain had caused repetitive regional and national famines when the crops failed, including 17 major famines in England between 1523 and 1623. The resulting dependence on the potato however caused the [European Potato Failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Potato_Failure "European Potato Failure"), a disastrous crop failure from [disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato_blight "Potato blight") that resulted in widespread [famine and the death of over one million people in Ireland alone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland) "Great Famine (Ireland)").

## Modern agriculture

### British agricultural revolution

Between the 17th century and the mid-19th century, Britain saw a large increase in agricultural productivity and net output. New agricultural practices like [enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enclosure "Enclosure"), mechanization, [four-field crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-field_crop_rotation "Four-field crop rotation") to maintain soil nutrients, and [selective breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding "Selective breeding") enabled an [unprecedented population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malthusian_trap "Malthusian trap") to 5.7 million in 1750, freeing up a significant percentage of the workforce, and thereby helped drive the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"). The productivity of wheat went up from 19 US bushels (670 L; 150 US dry gal; 150 imp gal) per [acre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acre "Acre") in 1720 to around 30 US bushels (1,100 L; 240 US dry gal; 230 imp gal) by 1840, marking a major turning point in history.

Advice on more productive techniques for farming began to appear in England in the mid-17th century, from writers such as [Samuel Hartlib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Hartlib "Samuel Hartlib"), [Walter Blith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Blith "Walter Blith") and others. The main problem in sustaining agriculture in one place for a long time was the depletion of nutrients, most importantly nitrogen levels, in the soil. To allow the soil to regenerate, productive land was often let fallow and, in some places, [crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation "Crop rotation") was used. The Dutch four-field rotation system was popularised by the British agriculturist [Charles Townshend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Townshend,_2nd_Viscount_Townshend "Charles Townshend, 2nd Viscount Townshend") in the 18th century. The system (wheat, turnips, barley and clover) opened up a fodder crop and grazing crop allowing livestock to be bred year-round. The use of clover was especially important as the legume roots replenished soil nitrates.
The mechanisation and rationalisation of agriculture was another important factor. [Robert Bakewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bakewell_(farmer) "Robert Bakewell (farmer)") and [Thomas Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Coke,_1st_Earl_of_Leicester_(seventh_creation) "Thomas Coke, 1st Earl of Leicester (seventh creation)") introduced [selective breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding "Selective breeding") and initiated a process of inbreeding to maximise desirable traits from the mid 18th century, such as the [New Leicester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Leicester "New Leicester") sheep. Machines were invented to improve the efficiency of various agricultural operation, such as [Jethro Tull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jethro_Tull_(agriculturist) "Jethro Tull (agriculturist)")'s [seed drill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed_drill "Seed drill") of 1701 that mechanised seeding at the correct depth and spacing and [Andrew Meikle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Meikle "Andrew Meikle")'s [threshing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshing_machine "Threshing machine") of 1784. Ploughs were steadily improved, from Joseph Foljambe's [Rotherham iron plough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough#Heavy_ploughs "Plough") in 1730 to [James Small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Small_(inventor) "James Small (inventor)")'s improved "Scots Plough" metal in 1763. In 1789 [Ransomes, Sims & Jefferies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomes,_Sims_%26_Jefferies "Ransomes, Sims & Jefferies") was producing 86 plough models for different soils. Powered farm machinery began with [Richard Trevithick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Trevithick "Richard Trevithick")'s [stationary steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_steam_engine "Stationary steam engine"), used to drive a threshing machine, in 1812. Mechanisation spread to additional farm uses throughout the 19th century. The first petrol-driven [tractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractor "Tractor") was built in America by [John Froelich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Froelich "John Froelich") in 1892.

[John Bennet Lawes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bennet_Lawes "John Bennet Lawes") began the scientific investigation of fertilization at the [Rothamsted Experimental Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothamsted_Experimental_Station "Rothamsted Experimental Station") in 1843. He investigated the impact of inorganic and organic fertilizers on crop yield and founded one of the first artificial fertilizer manufacturing factories in 1842. Fertilizer, in the shape of [sodium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_nitrate "Sodium nitrate") deposits in [Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile "Chile"), was imported to Britain by [John Thomas North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Thomas_North "John Thomas North") as well as [guano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guano "Guano") (birds droppings). The first commercial process for fertilizer production was the obtaining of [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_phosphate "Super phosphate") from the dissolution of [coprolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprolite "Coprolite") in [sulphuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulphuric_acid "Sulphuric acid").

### 20th century

[Dan Albone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Albone "Dan Albone") constructed the first commercially successful gasoline-powered general-purpose tractor in 1901, and the 1923 [International Harvester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Harvester "International Harvester") [Farmall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmall "Farmall") tractor marked a major point in the replacement of draft animals (particularly horses) with machines. Since that time, self-propelled mechanical harvesters ([combines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combine_harvester "Combine harvester")), [planters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planter_(farm_implement) "Planter (farm implement)"), [transplanters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transplanter "Transplanter") and other equipment have been developed, further revolutionizing agriculture. These inventions allowed farming tasks to be done with a speed and on a scale previously impossible, leading modern farms to output much greater volumes of high-quality produce per land unit.

The [Haber-Bosch method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process "Haber process") for synthesizing [ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate "Ammonium nitrate") represented a major breakthrough and allowed [crop yields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yields "Crop yields") to overcome previous constraints. It was first patented by German chemist [Fritz Haber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber "Fritz Haber"). In 1910 [Carl Bosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Bosch "Carl Bosch"), while working for German chemical company [BASF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASF "BASF"), successfully commercialized the process and secured further patents. In the years after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), the use of synthetic fertilizer increased rapidly, in sync with the increasing world population.

[Collective farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_farming "Collective farming") was widely practiced in the Soviet Union, the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc "Eastern Bloc") countries, China, and Vietnam, starting in the 1930s in the Soviet Union; one result was the [Soviet famine of 1932–33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_famine_of_1932%E2%80%9333 "Soviet famine of 1932–33"). Another consequence occurred during the [Great Leap Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward "Great Leap Forward") in China initiated by [Mao Tse-tung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Tse-tung "Mao Tse-tung") that resulted in the [Great Chinese Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Chinese_Famine "Great Chinese Famine") from 1959 to 1961 and ultimately reshaped the thinking of [Deng Xiaoping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping "Deng Xiaoping").

In the past century agriculture has been characterized by increased productivity, the substitution of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides for labour, [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution "Water pollution"), and [farm subsidies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_subsidies "Farm subsidies"). Other applications of scientific research since 1950 in agriculture include [gene manipulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_manipulation "Gene manipulation"), [hydroponics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroponics "Hydroponics"), and the development of economically viable [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel "Biofuel") such as [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol_fuel "Ethanol fuel").

The number of people involved in farming in industrial countries fell radically from 24 percent of the American population to 1.5 percent in 2002. The number of farms also decreased, and their ownership became more concentrated; for example, between 1967 and 2002, one million pig farms in America consolidated into 114,000, with 80 percent of the production on factory farms. According to the [Worldwatch Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldwatch_Institute "Worldwatch Institute"), 74 percent of the world's poultry, 43 percent of beef, and 68 percent of eggs are produced this way.

Famines however continued to sweep the globe through the 20th century. Through the effects of climatic events, government policy, war and crop failure, millions of people died in each of at least ten famines between the 1920s and the 1990s.

### Green Revolution

The Green Revolution was a series of research, development, and [technology transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology_transfer "Technology transfer") initiatives between the 1940s and the late 1970s. It increased agriculture production around the world, especially from the late 1960s. The initiatives, led by [Norman Borlaug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Borlaug "Norman Borlaug") and credited with saving over a billion people from starvation, involved the development of high-yielding varieties of cereal grains, expansion of irrigation infrastructure, modernization of management techniques, distribution of hybridized seeds, synthetic fertilizers, and [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide "Pesticide") to farmers.

Synthetic nitrogen, mined [rock phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_phosphate "Rock phosphate"), pesticides, and mechanization have greatly increased crop yields in the early 20th century. Increased supply of [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain "Grain") has also led to cheaper livestock. Further, global yield increases were experienced later in the 20th century when high-yield varieties of common staple grains such as rice, wheat, and corn were introduced as a part of the Green Revolution. The Green Revolution exported the technologies (including pesticides and synthetic nitrogen) of the developed world to the developing world. [Thomas Malthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malthus "Thomas Malthus") famously predicted that the Earth would not be able to support its growing population. Still, technologies such as the Green Revolution have allowed the world to produce a food surplus.

Although the Green Revolution significantly increased rice yields in Asia, yield leveled off. The genetic "yield potential" has increased for wheat, but the yield potential for rice has not increased since 1966, and the yield potential for maize has "barely increased in 35 years". It takes only a decade or two for [herbicide-resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide_resistance "Herbicide resistance") weeds to emerge, and insects become [resistant to insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide_resistance "Insecticide resistance") within about a decade, delayed somewhat by crop rotation.

### Organic agriculture

For most of its history, agriculture has been [organic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_agriculture "Organic agriculture"), without synthetic [fertilisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilisers "Fertilisers") or [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticides "Pesticides"), and without [GMOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GMOs "GMOs"). With the advent of chemical agriculture, [Rudolf Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Steiner "Rudolf Steiner") called for farming without synthetic pesticides, and his Agriculture Course of 1924 laid the foundation for [biodynamic agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodynamic_agriculture "Biodynamic agriculture"). [Lord Northbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Northbourne "Lord Northbourne") developed these ideas and presented his manifesto of [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming "Organic farming") in 1940. This became a worldwide movement, and organic farming is now practiced in many countries.
