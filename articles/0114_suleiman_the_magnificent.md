# Suleiman the Magnificent

**Suleiman I** (6 November 1494 – 6 September 1566), commonly known as **Suleiman the Magnificent**, was the [Ottoman sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sultans_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "List of sultans of the Ottoman Empire") from 1520 to 1566.
The longest reign among the Ottoman sultans, his rule brought about a notable peak in the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire")'s economic, military and political power, and raised the number of the empire's subjects to at least 25 million people.

After succeeding his father [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I "Selim I") on 30 September 1520, Suleiman began his reign by launching military campaigns against the [Christian powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") of Central and Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean; [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Belgrade_(1521) "Siege of Belgrade (1521)") fell to him in 1521 and [Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Rhodes_(1522) "Siege of Rhodes (1522)") in 1522, and at [Mohács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Moh%C3%A1cs "Battle of Mohács") in 1526, Suleiman broke the strength of the [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary "Kingdom of Hungary"). Hungary was subsequently divided, with much of it [incorporated directly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Hungary "Ottoman Hungary") into the empire.
However, his defeat at the [siege of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Vienna_(1529) "Siege of Vienna (1529)") in 1529 prevented him from advancing further into Europe. Suleiman also [fought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Safavid_War_(1532%E2%80%931555) "Ottoman–Safavid War (1532–1555)") for years against the [Shia Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam "Shia Islam") [Safavid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran") of Iran and annexed [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Iraq "Ottoman Iraq"). In North Africa, [Ottoman Tripolitania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Tripolitania "Ottoman Tripolitania") was established and the [Ottoman fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Navy "Ottoman Navy") dominated the seas from the Mediterranean to the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea") and into the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf").

Suleiman personally instituted major judicial changes pertaining to society, education, taxation, and criminal law. His reforms, carried out in conjunction with the Ottoman chief judicial official [Ebussuud Efendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebussuud_Efendi "Ebussuud Efendi"), brought together the two forms of [Ottoman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Law of the Ottoman Empire"): sultanic ([Kanun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanun_(law) "Qanun (law)")) and Islamic ([Sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia")).
He was also a distinguished poet and goldsmith; and a great patron of fine culture, overseeing the "Golden Age" of the Ottoman Empire which was at the height of its [artistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Culture of the Ottoman Empire"), [literary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_literature "Ottoman literature"), and [architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_architecture "Ottoman architecture") development.

In 1534, Suleiman broke with Ottoman tradition by marrying [Hürrem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCrrem "Hürrem"), an influential woman from his [Imperial Harem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Imperial_Harem "Ottoman Imperial Harem") who helped usher in the "[Sultanate of Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Women "Sultanate of Women")" period in Ottoman history. Upon Suleiman's death in 1566, he was succeeded by his and Hürrem's son [Selim II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II"). The death of Suleiman marks a watershed moment in [Ottoman history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_history "Ottoman history"), although scholars typically regard the period after his death to be one of crisis and adaptation rather than of [simple decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_decline_thesis "Ottoman decline thesis"). In the following decades, the Ottoman Empire began to experience significant political, institutional, and economic changes, a period often referred to as the [Era of Transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Transformation of the Ottoman Empire").

## Alternative names and titles

He was known as Suleiman the Magnificent in the [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world").

It is unclear when exactly the term *Kanûnî* (the Lawgiver) first came to be used as an epithet for Suleiman. It is entirely absent from sixteenth and seventeenth-century Ottoman sources and may date from the early 18th century.

There is a tradition of Western origin, according to which Suleiman the Magnificent was "Suleiman II", but that tradition has been based on an erroneous assumption that [Süleyman Çelebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleyman_%C3%87elebi "Süleyman Çelebi") was to be recognised as a legitimate sultan.

## Early life

Suleiman was born in [Trabzon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trabzon "Trabzon") on the southern coast of the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea") to Şehzade Selim (later [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I "Selim I")), probably on 6 November 1494, although this date is not known with absolute certainty or evidence. His mother was [Hafsa Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafsa_Sultan "Hafsa Sultan"), a concubine convert to Islam of unknown origins, who died in 1534. At the age of seven, Suleiman began studies of science, history, literature, theology and military tactics in the schools of the imperial [Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace "Topkapı Palace") in [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople"). As a young man, he befriended [Pargalı Ibrahim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pargal%C4%B1_Ibrahim_Pasha "Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha"), a Greek slave who later became one of his most trusted advisers (but who was later executed on Suleiman's orders). At age seventeen, he was appointed as the governor of first [Kaffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feodosiya#Caffa "Feodosiya") (Theodosia), then [Manisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manisa "Manisa"), with a brief tenure at [Edirne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edirne "Edirne").

### Accession

Upon the death of his father, [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I "Selim I") (r. 1512–1520), Suleiman entered Constantinople and ascended to the throne as the tenth Ottoman sultan. An early description of Suleiman, a few weeks following his accession, was provided by the [Venetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice "Republic of Venice") envoy [Bartolomeo Contarini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolomeo_Contarini_(nobleman) "Bartolomeo Contarini (nobleman)"):

> The sultan is only twenty-five years [actually 26] old, tall and slender but tough, with a thin and bony face. Facial hair is evident, but only barely. The sultan appears friendly and in good humor. Rumor has it that Suleiman is aptly named, enjoys reading, is knowledgeable and shows good judgment."

## Military campaigns

### Conquests in Europe

Upon succeeding his father, Suleiman began a series of military conquests, eventually leading to a revolt led by the Ottoman-appointed governor of [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus "Damascus") in 1521. Suleiman soon made preparations for the [conquest of Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Belgrade_1521 "Siege of Belgrade 1521") from the [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary "Kingdom of Hungary")—something his great-grandfather [Mehmed II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_II "Mehmed II") had failed to achieve because of [John Hunyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hunyadi "John Hunyadi")'s strong defense in the region. Its capture was vital in removing the Hungarians and Croats who, following the defeats of the [Albanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanians "Albanians"), [Bosniaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosniaks "Bosniaks"), [Bulgarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarians "Bulgarians"), [Byzantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") and the [Serbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbs "Serbs"), remained the only formidable force who could block further Ottoman gains in Europe. Suleiman encircled [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrade "Belgrade") on 28 August 1521, with 250,000 Turkish soldiers and over 100 ships. and began a series of heavy bombardments from an island in the [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube "Danube"). Belgrade was made the seat of the [Pashalik of Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashalik_of_Belgrade "Pashalik of Belgrade") (also known as the Sanjak of Smederevo), and quickly became the second largest Ottoman town in Europe at over 100,000 people, surpassed only by [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople").

The road to Hungary and Austria lay open, but Suleiman turned his attention instead to the Eastern [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean "Mediterranean") island of [Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes "Rhodes"), the home base of the [Knights Hospitaller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Hospitaller "Knights Hospitaller"). Suleiman built a large fortification, [Marmaris Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmaris_Castle "Marmaris Castle"), that served as a base for the [Ottoman Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Navy "Ottoman Navy"). Following a five-month [siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Rhodes_(1522) "Siege of Rhodes (1522)"), Rhodes capitulated and Suleiman allowed the [Knights of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_of_Rhodes "Knights of Rhodes") to depart. The conquest of the island cost the Ottomans 50,000 to 60,000 dead from battle and sickness (Christian claims went as high as 64,000 Ottoman battle deaths and 50,000 disease deaths).

As relations between Hungary and the Ottoman Empire deteriorated, Suleiman resumed his campaign in Central Europe, and on 29 August 1526 he defeated [Louis II of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_II_of_Hungary "Louis II of Hungary") (1506–1526) at the [Battle of Mohács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Moh%C3%A1cs "Battle of Mohács"). The Hungarian army, encouraged by the nobility to engage prematurely, launched a frontal assault that collapsed under coordinated Ottoman counterattacks. King Louis and much of the Hungarian aristocracy were killed, resulting in the destruction of the royal army and the end of the [Jagiellonian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_dynasty "Jagiellonian dynasty") in Hungary and Bohemia. The aftermath saw the partition of Hungary between the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire"), the [Habsburg monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy "Habsburg monarchy"), and the [Principality of Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Transylvania_(1570%E2%80%931711) "Principality of Transylvania (1570–1711)"). The battle marked the beginning of sustained [Ottoman–Habsburg wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Habsburg_wars "Ottoman–Habsburg wars") and the decline of Hungary as an independent power. Upon encountering the lifeless body of King Louis, Suleiman is said to have lamented: "I came indeed in arms against him; but it was not my wish that he should be thus cut off before he scarcely tasted the sweets of life and royalty." While Suleiman was campaigning in Hungary, [Turkmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorouks "Yorouks") tribes in central Anatolia (in [Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia "Cilicia")) revolted under the leadership of [Kalender Çelebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jelali_revolts "Jelali revolts").

Some Hungarian nobles proposed that [Ferdinand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor "Ferdinand I, Holy Roman Emperor"), who was the ruler of neighboring Austria and tied to Louis II's family by marriage, be King of Hungary, citing previous agreements that the [Habsburgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburgs "Habsburgs") would take the Hungarian throne if Louis died without heirs. However, other nobles turned to the nobleman [John Zápolya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Z%C3%A1polya "John Zápolya"), whom Suleiman supported. Under [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor "Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor") and his brother Ferdinand I, the Habsburgs reoccupied Buda and took possession of Hungary. Reacting in 1529, Suleiman marched through the valley of the Danube and regained control of Buda; in the following autumn, his forces laid [siege to Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Vienna_(1529) "Siege of Vienna (1529)"). This was to be the Ottoman Empire's most ambitious expedition and the apogee of its drive to the West. With a reinforced garrison of 16,000 men, the Austrians inflicted the first defeat on Suleiman, sowing the seeds of a bitter Ottoman–Habsburg rivalry that lasted until the 20th century. His second attempt to conquer Vienna failed in 1532, as Ottoman forces were delayed by the [siege of Güns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_G%C3%BCns "Siege of Güns") and failed to reach Vienna. In both cases, the Ottoman army was plagued by bad weather, forcing them to leave behind essential siege equipment, and was hobbled by overstretched supply lines. In 1533 the [Treaty of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truce_of_Constantinople_(1533) "Truce of Constantinople (1533)") was signed by Ferdinand I, in which he acknowledged Ottoman suzerainty and recognised Suleiman as his "father and suzerain", he also agreed to pay an annual tribute and accepted the Ottoman grand vizier as his brother and equal in rank.

By the 1540s, a renewal of the conflict in Hungary presented Suleiman with the opportunity to avenge the defeat suffered at Vienna. In 1541, the Habsburgs attempted to lay siege to Buda but were repulsed, and more Habsburg fortresses were captured by the Ottomans in two consecutive campaigns in 1541 and 1544 as a result, In 1542, after Ferdinand's repeated sieges of [Buda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buda "Buda") and [Pest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest,_Hungary "Pest, Hungary"),  Suleiman went to Edirne on 17 November 1542 to prepare for a new campaign and stayed there for a while. On 23 April 1543, he set out on another campaign against Hungary. On 8 August, after a two-week siege, [Esztergom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esztergom "Esztergom") was captured by the Ottoman Empire. Within a few weeks, the cities of Siklós, Székesfehérvár and Szeged were also taken. Ferdinand and Charles were forced to conclude a humiliating five-year treaty with Suleiman. Ferdinand renounced his claim to the Kingdom of Hungary and was forced to pay a fixed yearly sum to the Sultan for the Hungarian lands he continued to control. Of more symbolic importance, the treaty referred to Charles V not as "Emperor" but as the "King of Spain", leading Suleiman to identify as the true "Caesar". In 1552, Suleiman's forces laid [siege to Eger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Eger_(1552) "Siege of Eger (1552)"), located in the northern part of the Kingdom of Hungary, but the defenders led by [István Dobó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istv%C3%A1n_Dob%C3%B3 "István Dobó") repelled the attacks and defended the [Eger Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eger_Castle "Eger Castle").

Suleiman, set out on his 13th expedition, [Siege of Szigetvár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Szigetv%C3%A1r "Siege of Szigetvár") on 1 May 1566, at the age of 72, after an absence of approximately 13 years. The Ottoman army, which arrived in Belgrade on 27 June and was joined by Sigismund Zapolya's forces, arrived in [Szigetvár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szigetv%C3%A1r "Szigetvár") on 2 August. Suleiman arrived at the siege on 5 August and settled in his tent on a hill from which the siege could be seen. On 6 September, Suleiman died in his tent, one day before the fall of Szigetvár. His death was kept secret with great effort, with only the Sultan's innermost circle knowing of his demise. This was because the Ottomans feared that their soldiers would give up the battle if they knew that their leader had died, so his death was kept secret for 48 days. A courier was dispatched from the camp with a message for Suleiman's successor, [Selim II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II").

### Ottoman–Safavid War

Suleiman's father had made war with Persia a high priority. At first, Suleiman shifted attention to Europe and was content to contain [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), which was preoccupied by its own enemies to its east. After Suleiman stabilized his European frontiers, he now turned his attention to Persia, the base for the rival [Shia Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam "Shia Islam") faction. The [Safavid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_dynasty "Safavid dynasty") became the main enemy after two episodes. First, Shah [Tahmasp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahmasp_I "Tahmasp I") killed the [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad") governor loyal to Suleiman, and installed his own man in his place. Second, the governor of [Bitlis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitlis "Bitlis") had defected and sworn allegiance to the Safavids. As a result, in 1533, Suleiman ordered Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha to lead an army into eastern Asia Minor where he retook Bitlis and occupied [Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz "Tabriz") without resistance. Suleiman joined Ibrahim in 1534. They made a push towards Persia, only to find the Shah sacrificing territory instead of facing a pitched battle, resorting to harassment of the Ottoman army as it proceeded along the harsh interior. In 1535 Suleiman made a grand entrance into Baghdad. He enhanced his local support by restoring the tomb of [Abu Hanifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifa "Abu Hanifa"), the founder of the [Hanafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanafi "Hanafi") school of Islamic law to which the Ottomans adhered.

Attempting to defeat the Shah once and for all, Suleiman embarked upon a second campaign in 1548–1549. As in the previous attempt, Tahmasp avoided confrontation with the Ottoman army and instead chose to retreat, using [scorched earth tactics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorched_earth "Scorched earth") in the process and exposing the Ottoman army to the harsh winter of the [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus "Caucasus"). Suleiman abandoned the campaign with temporary Ottoman gains in [Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz "Tabriz") and the [Urmia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urmia "Urmia") region, a lasting presence in the province of [Van](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Province "Van Province"), control of the western half of [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan "Azerbaijan") and some forts in [Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_(country) "Georgia (country)").

In 1553, Suleiman began his third and final campaign against the Shah. Having initially lost territories in [Erzurum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erzurum "Erzurum") to the Shah's son, Suleiman retaliated by recapturing Erzurum, crossing the Upper Euphrates and laying waste to parts of Persia. The Shah's army continued its strategy of avoiding the Ottomans, leading to a stalemate from which neither army made any significant gain. In 1555, a settlement known as the [Peace of Amasya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Amasya "Peace of Amasya") was signed, which defined the borders of the two empires. By this treaty, Armenia and Georgia were divided equally between the two, with [Western Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Armenia "Western Armenia"), western [Kurdistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdistan "Kurdistan"), and western Georgia (incl. western [Samtskhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samtskhe_atabegate "Samtskhe atabegate")) falling in Ottoman hands while [Eastern Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Armenia "Eastern Armenia"), eastern Kurdistan, and eastern Georgia (incl. eastern Samtskhe) stayed in Safavid hands. The Ottoman Empire obtained most of [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq"), including Baghdad, which gave them access to the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf"), while the Persians retained their former capital [Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz "Tabriz") and all their other northwestern territories in the Caucasus and as they were prior to the wars, such as [Dagestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagestan "Dagestan") and all of what is now [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan "Azerbaijan").

### Campaigns in the Indian Ocean

Ottoman ships had been sailing in the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean") since the year 1518. Ottoman [admirals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiral "Admiral") such as [Hadim Suleiman Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadim_Suleiman_Pasha "Hadim Suleiman Pasha"), [Seydi Ali Reis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seydi_Ali_Reis "Seydi Ali Reis") and [Kurtoğlu Hızır Reis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurto%C4%9Flu_H%C4%B1z%C4%B1r_Reis "Kurtoğlu Hızır Reis") are known to have voyaged to the [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") imperial ports of [Thatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thatta "Thatta"), [Surat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surat "Surat") and [Janjira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janjira_State "Janjira State"). The Mughal Emperor [Akbar the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar_the_Great "Akbar the Great") himself is known to have exchanged six documents with Suleiman the Magnificent.

Suleiman led several naval campaigns against the [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Portugal "Kingdom of Portugal") in an attempt to remove them and reestablish trade with the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire"). [Aden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aden "Aden") in [Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen "Yemen") was captured by the Ottomans in 1538, in order to provide an Ottoman base for raids against Portuguese possessions on the western coast of the Mughal Empire. Sailing on, the Ottomans failed against the Portuguese at the [siege of Diu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Diu_(1538) "Siege of Diu (1538)") in September 1538, but then returned to Aden, where they fortified the city with 100 pieces of artillery. From this base, Sulayman Pasha managed to take control of the whole country of Yemen, also taking [Sanaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa "Sanaa").

With its strong control of the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea"), Suleiman successfully managed to dispute control of the trade routes to the Portuguese and maintained a significant level of trade with the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") throughout the 16th century.

From 1526 until 1543, Suleiman stationed over 900 Turkish soldiers to fight alongside the [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_people "Somali people") [Adal Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adal_Sultanate "Adal Sultanate") led by [Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_ibn_Ibrahim_al-Ghazi "Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi") during the [Conquest of Abyssinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abyssinian%E2%80%93Adal_war "Abyssinian–Adal war"). After the [first Ajuran-Portuguese war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Portuguese_conflicts_(1538%E2%80%9357) "Ottoman–Portuguese conflicts (1538–57)"), the Ottoman Empire would in 1559 absorb the weakened Adal Sultanate into its domain. This expansion furthered Ottoman rule in [Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia "Somalia") and the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa"). This also increased its influence in the Indian Ocean to compete with the Portuguese Empire with its close ally, the [Ajuran Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajuran_Sultanate "Ajuran Sultanate").

In 1564, Suleiman received an embassy from [Aceh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_Sultanate "Aceh Sultanate") (a sultanate on [Sumatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumatra "Sumatra"), in modern [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia")), requesting Ottoman support against the Portuguese. As a result, an [Ottoman expedition to Aceh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_embassy_to_Aceh "Ottoman embassy to Aceh") was launched, which was able to provide extensive military support to the Acehnese.

The discovery of new maritime trade routes by Western European states allowed them to avoid the Ottoman trade monopoly. The [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Portugal "Kingdom of Portugal") discovery of the [Cape of Good Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_of_Good_Hope "Cape of Good Hope") in 1488 initiated [a series of Ottoman-Portuguese naval wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_naval_expeditions_in_the_Indian_Ocean "Ottoman naval expeditions in the Indian Ocean") in the Ocean throughout the 16th century. The Ajuran Sultanate allied with the Ottomans defied the Portuguese economic monopoly in the Indian Ocean by employing a new coinage which followed the Ottoman pattern, thus proclaiming an attitude of economic independence in regard to the Portuguese.

### Mediterranean and North Africa

Having consolidated his conquests on land, Suleiman was greeted with the news that the fortress of [Koroni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koroni "Koroni") in [Morea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morea "Morea") (the modern [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese "Peloponnese"), peninsular Greece) had been lost to [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor "Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor")'s admiral, [Andrea Doria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Doria "Andrea Doria"). The presence of the Spanish in the Eastern Mediterranean concerned Suleiman, who saw it as an early indication of Charles V's intention to rival Ottoman dominance in the region. Recognizing the need to reassert naval preeminence in the Mediterranean, Suleiman appointed an exceptional naval commander in the form of [Khair ad Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayreddin_Barbarossa "Hayreddin Barbarossa"), known to Europeans as Barbarossa. Once appointed admiral-in-chief, Barbarossa was charged with rebuilding the Ottoman fleet.

In 1535, Charles V led a Holy League of 26,700 soldiers (10,000 Spaniards, 8,000 Italians, 8,000 Germans, and 700 Knights of St. John) to victory against the Ottomans at [Tunis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunis "Tunis"), which together with the war against [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice "Republic of Venice") the following year, led Suleiman to accept proposals from [Francis I of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_I_of_France "Francis I of France") to form [an alliance against Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Ottoman_alliance "Franco-Ottoman alliance"). Huge Muslim territories in North Africa were annexed. The piracy carried on thereafter by the [Barbary pirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_pirates "Barbary pirates") of North Africa can be seen in the context of the wars against Spain.

In 1541, the Spaniards led an unsuccessful [expedition to Algiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algiers_expedition_(1541) "Algiers expedition (1541)"). In 1542, facing a common Habsburg enemy during the [Italian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Wars "Italian Wars"), Francis I sought to renew the [Franco-Ottoman alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Ottoman_alliance "Franco-Ottoman alliance"). In early 1542, Polin successfully negotiated the details of the alliance, with the Ottoman Empire promising to send 60,000 troops against the territories of the German king Ferdinand, as well as 150 galleys against Charles, while France promised to attack [Flanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Flanders "County of Flanders"), harass the coasts of Spain with a naval force, and send 40 galleys to assist the Turks for operations in the Levant.

In August 1551, Ottoman naval commander [Turgut Reis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turgut_Reis "Turgut Reis") attacked and [captured Tripoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Tripoli_(1551) "Siege of Tripoli (1551)"), which had been a possession of the Knights of Malta since 1530. In 1553, Turgut Reis was nominated commander of [Tripoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripoli,_Libya "Tripoli, Libya") by Suleiman, making the city an important center for [piratical raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_slave_trade "Barbary slave trade") in the Mediterranean and the capital of the Ottoman province of [Tripolitania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Tripolitania "Ottoman Tripolitania"). In 1560, a powerful naval force was sent to recapture Tripoli, but that force was defeated in the [Battle of Djerba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Djerba "Battle of Djerba").

Elsewhere in the Mediterranean, when the Knights Hospitallers were re-established as the [Knights of Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Hospitaller "Knights Hospitaller") in 1530, their actions against Muslim navies quickly drew the ire of the Ottomans, who assembled another massive army in order to dislodge the Knights from Malta. The Ottomans invaded Malta in 1565, undertaking the [Great Siege of Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Siege_of_Malta "Great Siege of Malta"), which began on 18 May and lasted until 8 September, and is portrayed vividly in the frescoes of [Matteo Perez d'Aleccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteo_Perez_d%27Aleccio "Matteo Perez d'Aleccio") in the Hall of St. Michael and St. George. At first, it seemed that this would be a repeat of the battle on Rhodes, with most of Malta's cities destroyed and half the Knights killed in battle; but a relief force from Spain entered the battle, resulting in the loss of 10,000 Ottoman troops and the victory of the local Maltese citizenry.

## Legal and political reforms

While Sultan Suleiman was known as "the Magnificent" in the West, he was always *Kanuni* Suleiman or "The Lawgiver" (قانونی) to his Ottoman subjects. The overriding law of the empire was the [Shari'ah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shari%27ah "Shari'ah"), or Sacred Law, which as the divine law of [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") was outside of the Sultan's powers to change. Yet an area of distinct law known as the *Kanuns* (قانون, canonical legislation) was dependent on Suleiman's will alone, covering areas such as criminal law, land tenure and taxation. He collected all the judgments that had been issued by the nine Ottoman Sultans who preceded him. After eliminating duplications and choosing between contradictory statements, he issued a single legal code, all the while being careful not to violate the basic laws of Islam.

It was within this framework that Suleiman, supported by his [Grand Mufti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mufti "Grand Mufti") [Ebussuud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmet_Ebussuud_el-%C4%B0madi "Mehmet Ebussuud el-İmadi"), sought to reform the legislation to adapt to a rapidly changing empire. When the Kanun laws attained their final form, the code of laws became known as the *kanun‐i Osmani* (قانون عثمانی), or the "Ottoman laws". Suleiman's legal code was to last more than three hundred years.

The Sultan also played a role in protecting the Jewish subjects of his empire for centuries to come. In late 1553 or 1554, on the suggestion of his favorite doctor and dentist, the Spanish Jew [Moses Hamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_Hamon "Moses Hamon"), the Sultan issued a *[firman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firman_(decree) "Firman (decree)")* (فرمان) formally denouncing [blood libels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_libels "Blood libels") against the Jews. Furthermore, Suleiman enacted new criminal and police legislation, prescribing a set of fines for specific offenses, as well as reducing the instances requiring death or mutilation. In the area of taxation, taxes were levied on various goods and produce, including animals, mines, profits of trade, and import-export duties.

Higher *medreses* provided education of university status, whose graduates became *imams* (امام) or teachers. Educational centers were often one of many buildings surrounding the courtyards of mosques, others included libraries, baths, soup kitchens, residences and hospitals for the benefit of the public.

## The arts under Suleiman

Under Suleiman's patronage, the Ottoman Empire entered the golden age of its [cultural development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Culture of the Ottoman Empire"). Hundreds of imperial artistic societies (called the اهل حرف *Ehl-i Hiref*, "Community of the Craftsmen") were administered at the Imperial seat, the [Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace "Topkapı Palace"). After an apprenticeship, artists and craftsmen could advance in rank within their field and were paid commensurate wages in quarterly annual installments. Payroll registers that survive testify to the breadth of Suleiman's patronage of the arts, the earliest of the documents dating from 1526 list 40 societies with over 600 members. The *Ehl-i Hiref* attracted the empire's most talented artisans to the Sultan's court, both from the Islamic world and from the recently conquered territories in Europe, resulting in a blend of Arabic, Turkish and European cultures. Artisans in service of the court included painters, book binders, furriers, jewellers and goldsmiths. Whereas previous rulers had been influenced by [Persian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Iran "Culture of Iran") (Suleiman's father, Selim I, wrote poetry in Persian), Suleiman's patronage of the arts saw the Ottoman Empire assert its own artistic legacy.

The long reign of Suleiman the Magnificent is also recognized as the apogee of Ottoman political and [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Culture of the Ottoman Empire") development, with extensive patronage in art and architecture by the sultan, his family, and his high-ranking officials. The master architect of the classical period, Mimar Sinan, served as the chief court architect (*mimarbaşi*) from 1538 until his death in 1588. Sinan credited himself with the design of over 300 buildings, though another estimate of his works puts it at nearly 500. He is credited with designing buildings as far as [Buda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buda "Buda") (present-day [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest")) and [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca "Mecca"). Sinan was probably not present to directly supervise projects far from the capital, so in these cases his designs were most likely executed by his assistants or by local architects. This also demonstrates the ability of the central Ottoman state to commission and plan building projects across its vast territory at the time, a practice that also helped to establish Ottoman sovereignty in these provinces through the construction of monuments in a visibly Ottoman style. Architects in the capital were able to draw plans and delegate them to other architects who carried them out locally, while the imperial administration developed a set of standards for planning and construction and was able to coordinate the procurement and transportation of the necessary materials.

Suleiman himself was an accomplished poet, writing in Persian and Turkish under the [takhallus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takhallus "Takhallus") (nom de plume) *Muhibbi* (محبی, "Lover"). Some of Suleiman's verses have become Turkish proverbs, such as the well-known *Everyone aims at the same meaning, but many are the versions of the story*[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]. When his young son [Mehmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Mehmed "Şehzade Mehmed") died in 1543, he composed a moving [chronogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronogram "Chronogram") to commemorate the year: *Peerless among princes, my Sultan Mehmed*. In Turkish the chronogram reads شهزاده‌لر گزیده‌سی سلطان محمدم (*Şehzadeler güzidesi Sultan Muhammed'üm*), in which the Arabic [Abjad numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abjad_numerals "Abjad numerals") total 955, the equivalent in the [Islamic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar "Islamic calendar") of 1543 AD. In addition to Suleiman's own work, many great talents enlivened the literary world during Suleiman's rule, including [Fuzûlî](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuz%C3%BBl%C3%AE "Fuzûlî") and [Bâkî](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A2k%C3%AE "Bâkî"). The literary historian [Elias John Wilkinson Gibb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_John_Wilkinson_Gibb "Elias John Wilkinson Gibb") observed that "at no time, even in Turkey, was greater encouragement given to poetry than during the reign of this Sultan". Suleiman's most famous verse is:

The people think of wealth and power as the greatest fate,
But in this world a spell of health is the best state.
What men call sovereignty is a worldly strife and constant war;
Worship of God is the highest throne, the happiest of all estates.

Suleiman is credited with large-scale cultivation of the [tulip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip "Tulip") and it is thought that the tulips spread throughout Europe because of Suleiman. It is thought that diplomats who visited him were gifted the flowers while visiting his court. Some of the nobles in the court had seen the tulip and they also began growing their own. Soon images of the tulip were woven into rugs and fired into [ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic_art "Ceramic art").

### Artistic developments

Among the leading poets who emerged during the reign of Suleiman I are Fuzûlî, Bâki, Pir Sultan Abdal and Bağdatlı Ruhi .  Matrakçı Nasuh was one of the important painters, historians and miniature artists of the period.  Also living in this period and writing the *Suleimanname*, Shahnameh Arifî, Nakkaş Nigarî and calligrapher Ahmed Karahisari are among the leading artists of the period.

During the reign of Sultan Suleiman, the Grand Vizier [Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pargal%C4%B1_Ibrahim_Pasha "Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha") also brought mythological statues known as *the Three Beauties* from Buda to Istanbul after the [Battle of Mohács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Moh%C3%A1cs "Battle of Mohács") and erected them in his palace in the At Meydanı. Although these statues attracted attention, they were not permanent because they were seen as idols by some circles and were not welcomed. In addition to these statues, works by some Eastern and Western thinkers were brought from Buda to Istanbul and a library was established. These works were obtained as war booty from the large library established by the Hungarian king Matthias Corvinus. In this respect, Suleiman takes his place as an influential and important sultan in the Ottoman library culture. At the same time, in 1532, during the Ottoman-Habsburg rivalry, Suleiman the Magnificent wanted to show his superiority in every field, so he had a four-tiered crown made by Venetian merchants for 115,000 ducats through the Italian chief treasurer Alvise Gritti and with the encouragement of Makbul İbrahim Pasha . The four layers of the crown represent the four continents known at the time, and it is also known that it was made as a reference to the famous three-tiered crown of the Pope. Although it is thought that the crown was melted down after Süleyman, its legend in Europe did not end, and it was frequently depicted by European painters on the heads of Ottoman Sultans such as [Mehmed IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_IV "Mehmed IV") and [Ahmed I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_I "Ahmed I").

Suleiman also became renowned for sponsoring a series of monumental [architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_architecture#Classical_period_(1437–1703) "Ottoman architecture") developments within his empire. The Sultan sought to turn Constantinople into the center of Islamic civilization by a series of projects, including bridges, mosques, palaces and various charitable and social establishments. The greatest of these were built by the Sultan's chief architect, [Mimar Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan "Mimar Sinan"), under whom Ottoman architecture reached its zenith. Sinan became responsible for over three hundred monuments throughout the empire, including his two masterpieces, the [Süleymaniye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque "Süleymaniye Mosque") and [Selimiye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selimiye_Mosque_(Edirne) "Selimiye Mosque (Edirne)") mosques—the latter built in Adrianople (now [Edirne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edirne "Edirne")) in the reign of Suleiman's son [Selim II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II"). Suleiman also restored the [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock "Dome of the Rock") in Jerusalem and the [Walls of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walls_of_Jerusalem "Walls of Jerusalem") (which are the current walls of the [Old City of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_City_(Jerusalem) "Old City (Jerusalem)")), renovated the [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba "Kaaba") in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca "Mecca"), and constructed a complex in [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus "Damascus").

[Şehzade Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Mosque "Şehzade Mosque") complex, commissioned by Suleiman was dedicated to [Şehzade Mehmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Mehmed "Şehzade Mehmed"), his son who died in 1543. The mosque complex was built between 1545 and 1548. Like all imperial *külliye*s, it included multiple buildings, of which the mosque was the most prominent element. The mosque has a rectangular floor plan divided into two equal squares, with one square occupied by the courtyard and the other occupied by the prayer hall. Two minarets stand on either side at the junction of these two squares.

In 1550, through Mimar Sinan, Suleiman began construction for the [Süleymaniye complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque "Süleymaniye Mosque"), a monumental religious and charitable complex dedicated to him. Construction finished in 1557. Following the example of the earlier Fatih complex, it consists of many buildings arranged around the main mosque in the center, on a planned site occupying the summit of a hill in Istanbul. The buildings included the mosque itself, four general madrasas, a madrasa specialized for medicine, a madrasa specialized for [hadiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith "Hadith") (*darülhadis*), a *mektep*, a *darüşşifa*, a caravanserai, a *tabhane*, an imaret, a hammam, rows of shops, and a cemetery with two mausoleums. The site was formerly occupied by the grounds of the [Old Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eski_Saray "Eski Saray") (*Eski Saray*) built by Mehmet II, which had been damaged by fire. By this point, Suleiman had also moved his own residence and the royal family to Topkapı Palace. In order to adapt the hilltop site, Sinan had to begin by laying solid foundations and [retaining walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retaining_wall "Retaining wall") to form a wide terrace. The overall layout of buildings is less rigidly symmetrical than the Fatih complex, as Sinan opted to integrate it more flexibly into the existing urban fabric. Thanks to its refined architecture, its scale, its dominant position on the city skyline, and its role as a symbol of Suleiman's powerful reign, the Süleymaniye Mosque complex is one of the most important symbols of Ottoman architecture and is often considered by scholars to be the most magnificent mosque in Istanbul.

The prayer hall consists of a central dome surrounded by semi-domes on four sides, with smaller domes occupying the corners. Smaller semi-domes also fill the space between the corner domes and the main semi-domes. This design represents the culmination of the previous domed and semi-domed buildings in Ottoman architecture, bringing complete symmetry to the dome layout. An early version of this design, on a smaller scale, had been used before Sinan as early as 1520 or 1523 in the Fatih Pasha Mosque in Diyarbakir. While a [cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross "Cross")-like layout had symbolic meaning in Christian architecture, in Ottoman architecture this was purely focused on heightening and emphasizing the central dome.

Under his reign, The [Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace "Topkapı Palace") was greatly expanded with the permanent addition of the Imperial Harem to the Palace. From the reign of Suleiman, Topkapi not only served as the administrative center of the Empire, but also as the Imperial Residence of all succeeding Ottoman Sultans and the entire Ottoman Royal family until the 19th century.

### Consorts

Suleiman had three known consorts:

* [Mahidevran Hatun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahidevran "Mahidevran") (c.1499 – 3 February 1581) a [Circassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassians "Circassians"), [Albanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanians "Albanians"), [Montenegrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrins "Montenegrins"), or Crimean concubine, mother of his eldest surviving son [Şehzade Mustafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Mustafa_(son_of_Suleiman_the_Magnificent) "Şehzade Mustafa (son of Suleiman the Magnificent)"), who was executed in 1553. * [Hürrem Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCrrem_Sultan "Hürrem Sultan") (c. 1505/1507 – 15 April 1558), known in West as Roxelana (m. 1534), Suleiman's favorite [concubine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concubinage_in_Islam "Concubinage in Islam") during his reign, and later his legal wife and first [Haseki sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haseki_sultan "Haseki sultan") of the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire"), influencing state affairs and the administration of the empire, mother of most of his children, including his successor [Selim II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II"), and the only woman with whom he had monogamous relationship during his entire reign. Her influence over the Ottoman empire started an era known as [Sultanate of Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Women "Sultanate of Women"), where several indvidual mothers of Sultans could exercise consderable levels of influence throughout the empire. She was possibly a daughter of a [Ruthenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenians "Ruthenians") [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") priest.

### Relationship with Hürrem Sultan

Suleiman fell in love with [Hürrem Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCrrem_Sultan "Hürrem Sultan"), a harem girl from [Ruthenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenia "Ruthenia"), then part of [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_the_Kingdom_of_Poland "Crown of the Kingdom of Poland"). Western diplomats, taking notice of the palace gossip about her, called her "Russelazie" or "Roxelana", referring to her Ruthenian origins. The daughter of an [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") priest, she was [captured by Tatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean%E2%80%93Nogai_raids_into_East_Slavic_lands "Crimean–Nogai raids into East Slavic lands") from [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Khanate "Crimean Khanate"), sold as a [slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Slavery in the Ottoman Empire") in Constantinople, and eventually rose through the ranks of the Harem to become Suleiman's [favorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Favourite "Favourite"). Hürrem, a former concubine, became the legal wife of the Sultan, much to the astonishment of the observers in the palace and the city. He also allowed Hürrem Sultan to remain with him at court for the rest of her life, breaking another tradition—that when imperial heirs came of age, they would be sent along with the imperial concubine who bore them to govern remote provinces of the Empire, never to return unless their progeny succeeded to the throne.

Hürrem was the first Ottoman woman to directly take part in state affairs of the Ottoman Empire and she acted as an advisor to Suleiman in taking decisions. She used to sign documents in his absence, attended Imperial council meetings, held meetings with Grand Viziers and ministers to discuss regarding state affairs, controlled the appointments and even removal of ministers, grand vizier and even the Sheikh-ul-Islam from office and corresponded with ambassadors and Foreign rulers, particularly with [Sigismund II Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigismund_II_Augustus "Sigismund II Augustus"). She played a major role in the creation of the [Polish-Ottoman alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish-Ottoman_alliance "Polish-Ottoman alliance"). Suleiman not only declared her as his legal wife, but also created an Institutionalized title and position for her as the [Haseki sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haseki_sultan "Haseki sultan") of the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire"), making her as the second most powerful person in the empire after Suleiman. Suleiman was completely loyal towards her for rest of his life, and his love for her and his decisions to grant her more powers, made rumors throughout the Ottoman court that the sultan had been bewitched.

Under his pen name, Muhibbi, Sultan Suleiman composed this poem for Hürrem Sultan:

Throne of my lonely niche, my wealth, my love, my moonlight.
My most sincere friend, my confidant, my very existence, my Sultan, my one and only love.
The most beautiful among the beautiful ...
My springtime, my merry faced love, my daytime, my sweetheart, laughing leaf ...
My plants, my sweet, my rose, the one only who does not distress me in this room ...
My Istanbul, my Karaman, the earth of my Anatolia
My Badakhshan, my Baghdad and Khorasan
My woman of the beautiful hair, my love of the slanted brow, my love of eyes full of misery ...
I'll sing your praises always
I, lover of the tormented heart, Muhibbi of the eyes full of tears, I am happy.

### Grand Vizier Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha

Before his downfall, [Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pargal%C4%B1_Ibrahim_Pasha "Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha") was an inseparable friend and possible lover of Suleiman. In fact, he is referred to by his chroniclers as "the favourite" (Maḳbūl) along with "the executed" (Maḳtūl). Historians state that Suleiman I is remembered for "his passion for two of his slaves: for his beloved Ibrahim when the sultan was a hot-blooded youth, and for his beloved Hurrem when he was mature."

Ibrahim was originally a Christian from [Parga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parga "Parga") (in [Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epirus "Epirus")), who was captured in a raid during the [1499–1503 Ottoman–Venetian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Venetian_War_(1499%E2%80%931503) "Ottoman–Venetian War (1499–1503)"), and was given as a slave to Suleiman most likely in 1514. Ibrahim converted to Islam and Suleiman made him the royal [falconer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falconry "Falconry"), then promoted him to first officer of the Royal Bedchamber. It was reported that they slept together in the same bed. The sultan also built Ibrahim a lavish palace on the ancient [Hippodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodrome "Hippodrome"), Istanbul's main forum outside the [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia "Hagia Sophia") and [Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace "Topkapı Palace"). Despite his following marriage and his new sumptuous residence, Ibrahim sometimes spent the night with Suleiman I at Topkapı Palace. In turn, the sultan occasionally slept at Ibrahim's lodgings. Ibrahim Pasha rose to [Grand Vizier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Vizier "Grand Vizier") in 1523 and commander-in-chief of all the armies. Suleiman also conferred upon Ibrahim Pasha the honor of *[beylerbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beylerbey "Beylerbey")* of [Rumelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumelia "Rumelia") (first-ranking military governor-general), granting Ibrahim authority over all Ottoman territories in Europe, as well as command of troops residing within them in times of war. At the time, Ibrahim was only about thirty years old and lacked any actual military expertise; it is said that 'tongues wagged' at this unprecedented promotion straight from palace service to the two highest offices of the empire.

During his thirteen years as Grand Vizier, his rapid rise to power and vast accumulation of wealth had made Ibrahim many enemies at the Sultan's court. Suleiman's suspicion of Ibrahim was worsened by a quarrel between the latter and the finance secretary (*[defterdar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defterdar "Defterdar")*) [İskender Çelebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0skender_%C3%87elebi "İskender Çelebi"). The dispute ended in the disgrace of Çelebi on charges of intrigue, with Ibrahim convincing Suleiman to sentence the *defterdar* to death. Ibrahim also supported Şehzade Mustafa as the successor of Suleiman. This caused disputes between him and Hürrem Sultan, who wanted her sons to succeed to the throne. Ibrahim eventually fell from grace with the Sultan and his wife. Suleiman consulted his [Qadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qadi "Qadi"), who suggested that Ibrahim be put to death. The Sultan recruited assassins and ordered them to strangle Ibrahim in his sleep.

## Succession

Sultan Suleiman's two known consorts (Hurrem and Mahidevran) had borne him six sons, four of whom survived past the 1550s. They were [Mustafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Mustafa_(son_of_Suleiman_the_Magnificent) "Şehzade Mustafa (son of Suleiman the Magnificent)"), [Selim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II"), [Bayezid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Bayezid "Şehzade Bayezid"), and [Cihangir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Cihangir "Şehzade Cihangir"). The eldest was [Mahidevran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahidevran "Mahidevran")'s son, while Selim, Bayezid, and Cihangir were born to Hurrem. Hurrem is usually held at least partly responsible for the intrigues in nominating a successor, though there is no evidence to support this. Although she was Suleiman's wife, she exercised no official public role. This did not, however, prevent Hurrem from wielding powerful political influence. Until the reign of [Ahmed I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_I "Ahmed I") (1603–1617), the Empire had no formal means of nominating a successor, so successions usually involved the death of competing princes in order to avert civil unrest and rebellions.

By 1552, when the campaign against Persia had begun with Rüstem appointed commander-in-chief of the expedition, intrigues against Mustafa began. Rüstem sent one of Suleiman's most trusted men to report that since Suleiman was not at the head of the army, the soldiers thought the time had come to put a younger prince on the throne; at the same time, he spread rumours that Mustafa had proved receptive to the idea. Angered by what he came to believe were Mustafa's plans to claim the throne, the following summer upon return from his campaign in Persia, Suleiman summoned him to his tent in the [Ereğli valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ere%C4%9Fli,_Konya "Ereğli, Konya"). When Mustafa entered his father's tent to meet with him, Suleiman's [eunuchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eunuch "Eunuch") attacked Mustafa, and after a long struggle the mutes killed him using a bow-string.

Cihangir is said to have died of grief a few months after the news of his half-brother's murder. The two surviving brothers, [Selim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II") and [Bayezid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Bayezid "Şehzade Bayezid"), were given command in different parts of the empire. Within a few years, however, civil war broke out between the brothers, each supported by his loyal forces. With the aid of his father's army, Selim defeated Bayezid in [Konya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konya "Konya") in 1559, leading the latter to seek refuge with the [Safavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavids "Safavids") along with his four sons. Following diplomatic exchanges, the Sultan demanded from the [Safavid Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahmasp_I "Tahmasp I") that Bayezid be either extradited or executed. In return for large amounts of gold, the Shah allowed a Turkish executioner to strangle Bayezid and his four sons in 1561, clearing the path for Selim's succession to the throne five years later.

## Death and legacy

On 6 September 1566, Suleiman, who had set out from Constantinople to command an expedition to Hungary, died before an Ottoman victory at the [siege of Szigetvár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Szigetv%C3%A1r "Siege of Szigetvár") in Hungary at the age of 71 and his Grand Vizier [Sokollu Mehmed Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokollu_Mehmed_Pasha "Sokollu Mehmed Pasha") kept his death secret during the retreat for the enthronement of [Selim II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_II "Selim II"). The sultan's body was taken back to Istanbul to be buried, while his heart, liver, and some other organs were buried in [Turbék](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turb%C3%A9k "Turbék"), outside [Szigetvár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szigetv%C3%A1r "Szigetvár"). A mausoleum constructed above the burial site came to be regarded as a holy place and pilgrimage site. Within a decade a mosque and [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufi "Sufi") hospice were built near it, and the site was protected by a salaried garrison of several dozen men.

### Legacy

The formation of Suleiman's legacy began even before his death. Throughout his reign literary works were commissioned praising Suleiman and constructing an image of him as an ideal ruler, most significantly by Celalzade Mustafa, [chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C5%9Fanc%C4%B1 "Nişancı") of the empire from 1534 to 1557. Later Ottoman writers applied this idealised image of Suleiman to the Near Eastern literary genre of [advice literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_advice_literature "Islamic advice literature") named *[naṣīḥatnāme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasihatname "Nasihatname")*, urging sultans to conform to his model of rulership and to maintain the empire's institutions in their sixteenth-century form. Such writers were pushing back against the political and institutional [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Transformation of the Ottoman Empire") of the empire after the middle of the sixteenth century, and portrayed deviation from the norm as it had existed under Suleiman as evidence of the decline of the empire. Western historians, failing to recognise that these 'decline writers' were working within an established literary genre and often had deeply personal reasons for criticizing the empire, long took their claims at face value and consequently adopted the idea that the empire entered a period of decline after the death of Suleiman. Since the 1980s this view has been thoroughly reexamined, and modern scholars have come to overwhelmingly reject the idea of decline, labelling it an "untrue myth".

Suleiman's conquests had brought under the control of the Empire major [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim "Muslim") cities (such as [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad")), many [Balkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_peninsula "Balkan peninsula") provinces (reaching present day [Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia "Croatia") and Hungary), and most of North Africa. His expansion into Europe had given the Ottoman Turks a powerful presence in the European balance of power. Indeed, such was the perceived threat of the Ottoman Empire under the reign of Suleiman that Austria's ambassador [Busbecq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogier_Ghiselin_de_Busbecq "Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq") warned of Europe's imminent conquest: "On [the Turks'] side are the resources of a mighty empire, strength unimpaired, habituation to victory, endurance of toil, unity, discipline, frugality and watchfulness ... Can we doubt what the result will be? ... When the Turks have settled with Persia, they will fly at our throats supported by the might of the whole East; how unprepared we are I dare not say." Suleiman's legacy was not, however, merely in the military field. The French traveler [Jean de Thévenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_de_Th%C3%A9venot "Jean de Thévenot") bears witness a century later to the "strong agricultural base of the country, the well being of the peasantry, the abundance of staple foods and the pre-eminence of organization in Suleiman's government".

Even thirty years after his death, "Sultan Solyman" was quoted by the English playwright [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare "William Shakespeare") as a military prodigy in *[The Merchant of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merchant_of_Venice "The Merchant of Venice")*, where the Prince of [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco "Morocco") boasts about his prowess by saying that he defeated Suleiman in three battles (Act 2, Scene 1).

Through the distribution of court patronage, Suleiman also presided over a golden age in Ottoman arts, witnessing immense achievement in the realms of architecture, literature, art, theology and philosophy. Today the skyline of the [Bosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosphorus "Bosphorus") and of many cities in modern Turkey and the former Ottoman provinces, are still adorned with the architectural works of [Mimar Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan "Mimar Sinan"). One of these, the [Süleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque "Süleymaniye Mosque"), is the final resting place of Suleiman: he is buried in a domed mausoleum attached to the mosque.

Nevertheless, assessments of Suleiman's reign have frequently fallen into the trap of the [Great Man theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Man_theory "Great Man theory") of history. The administrative, cultural, and military achievements of the age were a product not of Suleiman alone, but also of the many talented figures who served him, such as grand viziers [Ibrahim Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pargal%C4%B1_Ibrahim_Pasha "Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha") and [Rüstem Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%BCstem_Pasha "Rüstem Pasha"), the [Grand Mufti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaykh_al-Islam "Shaykh al-Islam") [Ebussuud Efendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebussuud_Efendi "Ebussuud Efendi"), who played a major role in legal reform, and [chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C5%9Fanc%C4%B1 "Nişancı") and chronicler Celalzade Mustafa, who played a major role in bureaucratic expansion and in constructing Suleiman's legacy.

In an inscription dating from 1537 on the citadel of [Bender, Moldova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bender,_Moldova "Bender, Moldova"), Suleiman the Magnificent gave expression to his power:

> I am God's slave and sultan of this world. By the grace of God I am head of Muhammad's community. God's might and Muhammad's miracles are my companions. I am Süleymân, in whose name the hutbe is read in Mecca and Medina. In Baghdad I am the shah, in Byzantine realms the caesar, and in Egypt the sultan; who sends his fleets to the seas of Europe, the Maghrib and India. I am the sultan who took the crown and throne of Hungary and granted them to a humble slave. The voivoda Petru raised his head in revolt, but my horse's hoofs ground him into the dust, and I conquered the land of Moldovia.

Suleiman, as sculpted by [Joseph Kiselewski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kiselewski "Joseph Kiselewski"), is present on one of the 23 relief portraits over the gallery doors of the House Chamber of the [United States Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Capitol "United States Capitol") that depicts historical figures noted for their work in establishing the principles that underlie [American law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_law "American law").
