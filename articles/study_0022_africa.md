# Africa

**Africa** is the world's second-largest and second-most populous [continent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent") after [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia"). At about 30.3 million km2 (11.7 million square miles), including adjacent islands, it covers around 20% of [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth")'s land area and 6% of its total surface area. With nearly 1.4 billion of the [world's population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population "World population"), [Africa's population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Africa "Demographics of Africa") is the youngest among the continents; the median age in 2012 was 19.7, when the worldwide median age was 30.4. Based on 2024 projections, Africa's population will exceed 3.8 billion people by 2100. Africa is the least wealthy inhabited continent per capita and second-least wealthy by total wealth, ahead of [Oceania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceania "Oceania"). Scholars have attributed this to various factors including [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Africa "Geography of Africa"), [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Africa "Climate of Africa"), corruption, [colonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa "Scramble for Africa"), the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), and [neocolonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neocolonialism "Neocolonialism"). Despite this low concentration of wealth, the [African economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Africa "Economy of Africa") is home to many of the [world's fastest-growing economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate "List of countries by real GDP growth rate").

Africa straddles the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator "Equator") and the [prime meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_meridian "Prime meridian"). The continent is surrounded by the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea") to the north, the [Arabian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_plate "Arabian plate") and the [Gulf of Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Suez "Gulf of Suez") to the northeast, the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean") to the southeast and the [Atlantic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean "Atlantic Ocean") to the west. [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France"), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy"), [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal "Portugal"), [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain "Spain"), and [Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen "Yemen") have parts of their [territories located on African geographical soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_and_dependent_territories_in_Africa "List of sovereign states and dependent territories in Africa"), mostly in the form of islands.

The continent includes [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar "Madagascar") and various archipelagos. It contains [54 fully recognised sovereign states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_and_dependent_territories_in_Africa "List of sovereign states and dependent territories in Africa"), eight cities and islands that are part of non-African states, and two *de facto* independent [states with limited or no recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_states_with_limited_recognition "List of states with limited recognition"). This count does not include [Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malta "Malta") and [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily "Sicily"), which are geologically part of the African continent. [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algeria "Algeria") is the largest country by area, and [Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigeria "Nigeria") is the largest by population. Nations cooperate through the establishment of the [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union "African Union"), which is headquartered in [Addis Ababa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_Ababa "Addis Ababa").

Africa is highly biodiverse; it is the continent with the largest number of [megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megafauna "Megafauna") species, as it was least affected by the [extinction of the Pleistocene megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Pleistocene_extinctions#Introduction "Late Pleistocene extinctions"). However, it is [heavily affected by a wide range of environmental issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_Africa "Environmental issues in Africa"), including desertification, deforestation, water scarcity, and pollution. These entrenched environmental concerns are expected to worsen as [climate change impacts Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Africa "Climate change in Africa"). The UN [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Panel_on_Climate_Change "Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change") has identified Africa as the continent most [vulnerable to climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_vulnerability "Climate change vulnerability").

The [history of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Africa "History of Africa") is long, complex, and varied, and has often been under-appreciated by the global [historical community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_historiography "African historiography"). In [African societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Indigenous_peoples#Africa "List of Indigenous peoples"), the oral word is revered, and they have generally recorded their history via oral tradition, which has led anthropologists to term them "oral civilisations", contrasted with "literate civilisations" which pride the written word. [African culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Africa "Culture of Africa") is rich and diverse both within and across [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_regions_of_Africa "List of regions of Africa"), encompassing [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_art "African art"), [cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_cuisine "African cuisine"), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Africa "Music of Africa") and [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_dance "African dance"), [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Africa "Religion in Africa"), and [dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothing_in_Africa "Clothing in Africa").

Africa, particularly [Eastern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Africa "Eastern Africa"), is widely accepted to be the place of origin of humans and the [Hominidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hominidae "Hominidae") clade, also known as the great apes. The earliest hominids and their ancestors have been dated to around 7 million years ago, and *[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens")* (modern humans) are believed to have originated in Africa 350,000 to 260,000 years ago. Throughout history, the continent saw the rise and fall of [many kingdoms and empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_kingdoms_and_empires_in_African_history "List of kingdoms and empires in African history"), such as [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"), [Makuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuria "Makuria"), the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate "Fatimid Caliphate"), [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire "Mali Empire"), the [Asante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asante_Empire "Asante Empire"), [Kongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kongo "Kingdom of Kongo"), the [Luba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luba_Empire "Luba Empire"), [Bunyoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunyoro "Bunyoro"), [Great Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Zimbabwe "Great Zimbabwe"), [Imerina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imerina "Imerina"), and the [Zulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulu_Kingdom "Zulu Kingdom"). Despite the predominance of states, many societies were [heterarchical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterarchy "Heterarchy") and [stateless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stateless_society "Stateless society"). [Slave trades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_slavery "History of slavery") created [various diasporas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora "African diaspora"), [especially in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Americans "African Americans"). From the late 19th century to the early 20th century, driven by the [Second Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution "Second Industrial Revolution"), most of Africa was [rapidly conquered and colonised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa "Scramble for Africa") by [European nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_empire "Colonial empire"), save for [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia") and [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia "Liberia"). European rule had [significant impacts on Africa's societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_European_colonialism_and_colonization "Analysis of European colonialism and colonization"), and colonies were maintained for the purpose of economic exploitation and extraction of natural resources. Most present states emerged from [a process of decolonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonisation_of_Africa "Decolonisation of Africa") following [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") and established the [Organisation of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity "Organisation of African Unity") in 1963, the predecessor to the African Union. The nascent countries decided to keep their colonial borders, with [traditional power structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_current_non-sovereign_African_monarchs "List of current non-sovereign African monarchs") used in governance to varying degrees.

## Etymology

*[Afri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afri "Afri")* was a Latin name used to refer to the inhabitants of what was then known as northern Africa, located west of the [River Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile"), and in its widest sense referring to all lands south of the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean "Mediterranean"), also known as [Ancient Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Libya "Ancient Libya"). This name seems to have originally referred to a native [Libyan (Berber)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers "Berbers") tribe; see [Terence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence#Biography "Terence") for discussion. In [numitheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_traditional_religion "Berber traditional religion") and ancient [Roman religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome "Religion in ancient Rome"), Africa was a goddess of [fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility "Fertility") and abundance worshipped by the [Berbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers "Berbers") and the tribe of Ifri. It stems from the [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages "Berber languages") word *ifri* (plural *ifran*), meaning "cave", in reference to cave worship. The same word may be found in the name of the [Banu Ifran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Ifran "Banu Ifran") from [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algeria "Algeria") and [Tripolitania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripolitania "Tripolitania"), a Berber tribe originally from [Yafran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yafran "Yafran") (also known as *Ifrane*) in northwestern [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libya "Libya"), as well as [Ifrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifrane "Ifrane"), [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco "Morocco"). The name has also been connected with the [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_language "Phoenician language") word **ʿafar** meaning "dust".

Under [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") rule, [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage "Carthage") became the capital of the province of *[Africa Proconsularis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_(Roman_province) "Africa (Roman province)")*, following the Roman victory over the [Carthaginians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage "Ancient Carthage") in the [Third Punic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Punic_War "Third Punic War") in 146 BC, which also included the coastal part of modern Libya. The Latin suffix *[-ica](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/-ica#Latin "wikt:-ica")* can sometimes be used to denote a land (e.g., in *[Celtica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallia_Celtica "Gallia Celtica")* from *[Celtae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtae "Celtae")*, as used by [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar")). The medieval Muslim region of [Ifriqiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifriqiya "Ifriqiya"), following its conquest of the [Byzantine (Eastern Roman)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") Empire's *[Exarchatus Africae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exarchate_of_Africa "Exarchate of Africa")*, preserved a form of the name. According to the Romans, Africa lies to the west of [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt"), while "Asia" was used to refer to [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") and lands to the east. In his *[Geographia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_(Ptolemy) "Geography (Ptolemy)"),* [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy") draws a definite line between the two continents by indicating [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria") along the [prime meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_meridian "Prime meridian") and making the [Isthmus of Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmus_of_Suez "Isthmus of Suez") and the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea") the boundary between Asia and Africa. As Europeans came to understand the real extent of the continent, the idea of "Africa" expanded with their knowledge.

Other etymological hypotheses have been postulated:

* 1st-century Jewish historian [Flavius Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavius_Josephus "Flavius Josephus") (*Ant. 1.15*) asserted that it was named for [Epher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epher "Epher"), grandson of [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham "Abraham") according to [Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis "Book of Genesis") 25:4, whose descendants, he claimed, had invaded Libya.
* [Isidore of Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Seville "Isidore of Seville"), in his 7th-century *[Etymologiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymologiae "Etymologiae")* XIV.5.2., suggests it comes from the Latin *aprica*, meaning "sunny".
* Massey, in 1881, stated it is derived from the Egyptian *af-rui-ka*, meaning "to turn toward the opening of the Ka". The [ka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_soul#Ka "Egyptian soul") is the energetic double of every person, and the "opening of the Ka" refers to a womb or birthplace. Africa would be, for the Egyptians, "the birthplace".
* Michèle Fruyt in 1976 proposed linking the Latin word with *africus* "south wind", which would be of [Umbrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbrian_language "Umbrian language") origin and mean originally "rainy wind".
* Robert R. Stieglitz of [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University "Rutgers University") in 1984 proposed: "The name Africa, derived from the Latin *\*Aphir-ic-a*, is cognate to Hebrew [Ophir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophir "Ophir") ['rich']."
* [Ibn Khallikan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khallikan "Ibn Khallikan") and some other historians claim the name came from a [Himyarite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite "Himyarite") king called Afrikin ibn Kais ibn Saifi ("Afrikus son of Abraham") who subdued Ifriqiya.
* Arabic *afrīqā* (feminine noun) and *ifrīqiyā*, now usually pronounced *afrīqiyā* (feminine) 'Africa', from *'afara* [' = *'ain*, not *'alif*] 'to be dusty' from *'afar* 'dust, powder' and *'afir* 'dried, dried up by the sun, withered' and *'affara* 'to dry in the sun on hot sand' or 'to sprinkle with dust'.
* Possibly [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_language "Phoenician language") *faraqa* in the sense of 'colony, separation'.

The terms "[North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa")" and "[Sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa")" have been subject to recent criticism from some scholars due to their historical root in [colonialist discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racialization "Racialization") which arbitrarily separated Africa into a white "northern" Africa and black "southern" Africa, overlooking [common genetic links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_history_of_Africa "Genetic history of Africa") and regional diversity across the continent.

## History

In accordance with [African cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_traditional_religions "African traditional religions"), African historical consciousness viewed historical change and continuity, order and purpose within the framework of man and his environment, the gods, and his ancestors; he believed himself part of a holistic spiritual entity. In African societies, the historical process is largely a [communal one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_communalism "African communalism"), with eyewitness accounts, hearsay, reminiscences, and occasionally visions, dreams, and hallucinations crafted into narrative oral traditions which are performed and transmitted through generations.

In oral traditions, time is sometimes mythical and social, and ancestors were considered historical actors. Mind and memory shape traditions, as events are condensed over time and crystallise into clichés.Oral tradition can be [exoteric](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/exoteric "wiktionary:exoteric") or [esoteric](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/esoteric "wiktionary:esoteric"). It speaks to people according to their understanding, unveiling itself in accordance with their aptitudes. In [African epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_philosophy "African philosophy"), the epistemic subject "experiences the epistemic object in a sensuous, emotive, intuitive, abstractive understanding, rather than through abstraction alone, as is the case in Western epistemology" to arrive at a "complete knowledge", and as such oral traditions, [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Africa "Music of Africa"), proverbs, and the like were used in the preservation and transmission of knowledge.

### Prehistory

Africa is considered by most [paleoanthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoanthropology "Paleoanthropology") to be the [oldest inhabited territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_Humankind "Cradle of Humankind") on Earth, with the Human species originating from the continent. During the mid-20th century, anthropologists discovered many fossils and evidence of human occupation perhaps as early as seven million years ago ("[Before Present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present "Before Present")"; BP). Fossil remains of several species of early apelike humans thought to have evolved into modern humans, such as *[Australopithecus afarensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australopithecus_afarensis "Australopithecus afarensis")* [radiometrically dated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometrically_dated "Radiometrically dated") to approximately 3.9–3.0 million BP, *[Paranthropus boisei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranthropus_boisei "Paranthropus boisei")* (c. 2.3–1.4 million BP) and *[Homo ergaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_ergaster "Homo ergaster")* (c. 1.9 million–600,000 BP) have been discovered.

After the evolution of *[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens")* approximately 350,000 to 260,000 BP, the continent was mainly populated by groups of [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer"). These first modern humans left Africa and populated the rest of the globe during the [Out of Africa II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recent_African_origin_of_modern_humans "Recent African origin of modern humans") migration dated to approximately 50,000 years BP, exiting either across [Bab-el-Mandeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab-el-Mandeb "Bab-el-Mandeb") over the Red Sea, the [Strait of Gibraltar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Gibraltar "Strait of Gibraltar"), or the Isthmus of Suez.

Other migrations of modern humans have been dated to that time, with evidence of early human settlement found in [southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa "Southern Africa"), [Southeast Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Africa "Southeast Africa"), [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa"), and the [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara"). During the [African humid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_humid_period "African humid period"), estimated to have been around 10,500 BP, the Sahara had again become a green fertile valley, and its populations returned from the interior and coastal highlands, with [rock art paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saharan_rock_art "Saharan rock art") depicting a fertile Sahara and large populations discovered in [Tassili n'Ajjer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tassili_n%27Ajjer "Tassili n'Ajjer") dating back perhaps 10 millennia. However, the warming and drying climate meant that by 5,000 BP, the Sahara region was becoming increasingly dry and hostile. Around 3500 BC, due to a tilt in [Earth's orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_orbit "Earth's orbit"), the Sahara experienced a period of rapid desertification. The domestication of cattle preceded agriculture and seems to have existed alongside hunter-gatherer cultures. It is speculated that by 6,000 BP, cattle were domesticated in North Africa.

In [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"), a wet phase ushered in an expanding [rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest "Rainforest") and wooded [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna "Savanna") from Senegal to Cameroon. Between 9,000 and 5,000 BP, [Niger–Congo speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages "Niger–Congo languages") domesticated the [oil palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaeis_guineensis "Elaeis guineensis") and [raffia palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raffia_palm "Raffia palm"). [Black-eyed peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-eyed_pea "Black-eyed pea") and [voandzeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voandzeia "Voandzeia") (African groundnuts) were domesticated, followed by [okra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra "Okra") and [kola nuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_nut "Kola nut"). Since most of the plants grew in the forest, the Niger–Congo speakers invented polished stone axes for clearing forest. [Pygmies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Pygmies "African Pygmies") are thought to have inhabited [Central Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Africa "Central Africa") for many millennia, splitting into eastern and western groups around 5,000 BP. Over 150,000 BP, there was an early dispersal of [anatomically modern humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_human "Early modern human") to Southern Africa, equated with the modern-day [Khoisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan "Khoisan") who have preserved their traditional hunter-gatherer way of life.

### 4th millennium BC – 6th century AD

#### Northeast Africa

From 3500 BC, [nomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nome_(Egypt) "Nome (Egypt)") (ruled by [nomarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomarch "Nomarch")) coalesced to form the kingdoms of [Lower Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Egypt "Lower Egypt") and [Upper Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Egypt "Upper Egypt") in [northeast Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_Africa "Northeast Africa"). Around 3100 BC, Upper Egypt conquered Lower Egypt to unify [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") under the [1st dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt "First Dynasty of Egypt"), with the process of consolidation and assimilation completed by the time of the [3rd dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_dynasty "3rd dynasty"), which formed the [Old Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Kingdom_of_Egypt "Old Kingdom of Egypt") in 2686 BC. The [Kingdom of Kerma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kerma "Kingdom of Kerma") emerged around this time to become the dominant force in [Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia"), controlling territory as large as Egypt between the first and fourth [cataracts of the Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataracts_of_the_Nile "Cataracts of the Nile").

The height of the Old Kingdom saw the construction of many [great pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_pyramids "Egyptian pyramids"), though under the [6th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Sixth Dynasty of Egypt") power gradually decentralised to the nomarchs, culminating in the [disintegration of the kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt "First Intermediate Period of Egypt"), exacerbated by drought and famine. Around 2055 BC, the [11th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleventh_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Eleventh Dynasty of Egypt"), based in [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt "Thebes, Egypt"), conquered the others to form the [Middle Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt "Middle Kingdom of Egypt"), and the [12th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelfth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Twelfth Dynasty of Egypt") expanded into [Lower Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Nubia "Lower Nubia") at the expense of Kerma. Around 1700 BC, the Middle Kingdom [fractured in two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt "Second Intermediate Period of Egypt"), and the [Hyksos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyksos "Hyksos") (a militaristic people from [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_(region) "Palestine (region)")) invaded and conquered Lower Egypt, while Kerma coordinated invasions deep into Egypt to reach its greatest extent. In 1550 BC, the [18th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Eighteenth Dynasty of Egypt") expelled the Hyksos and established the [New Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt "New Kingdom of Egypt"). The New Kingdom conquered the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") from the [Canaanites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaanites "Canaanites"), [Mittani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittani "Mittani"), [Amorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorites "Amorites"), and [Hittites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittites "Hittites"), and extinguished Kerma, incorporating Nubia into the empire, and sending the Egyptian empire into its golden age. Internal struggles, drought, famine, and invasions by a [confederation of seafaring peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_peoples "Sea peoples") contributed to the New Kingdom's [collapse in 1069 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt "Third Intermediate Period of Egypt").

The New Kingdom's collapse liberated the [Kingdom of Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush") in Nubia, which manoeuvred into power in Upper Egypt and conquered Lower Egypt in 754 BC to form the [Kushite Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Twenty-fifth Dynasty of Egypt"). The Kushites ruled for a century and oversaw a [revival in pyramid building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubian_pyramids "Nubian pyramids"), until they were [driven out of Egypt by the Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_conquest_of_Egypt "Assyrian conquest of Egypt") in 663 BC in reprisal for their expansion towards the [Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Empire "Assyrian Empire"). The Assyrians [installed a puppet dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-sixth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Twenty-sixth Dynasty of Egypt") that later gained independence and once more [unified Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Period_of_Egypt "Late Period of Egypt"), until it was conquered by the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") in 525 BC.

Egypt briefly regained independence from the Achaemenids under the [28th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-eighth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Twenty-eighth Dynasty of Egypt") from 404 to 343 BC. The conquest of [Achaemenid Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Persian_Egypt "History of Persian Egypt") by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") in 332 BC marked the beginning of [Hellenistic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period") and the installation of the [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians "Ancient Macedonians") [Ptolemaic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty "Ptolemaic dynasty"). The Ptolemaics lost their holdings outside of Africa to the [Seleucids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_dynasty "Seleucid dynasty") in the [Syrian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Wars "Syrian Wars"), expanded into [Cyrenaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica "Cyrenaica"), and briefly occupied [part of Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triakontaschoinos "Triakontaschoinos") in the 3rd century BC. In the 1st century BC, Ptolemaic Egypt became entangled in a [Roman civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar%27s_civil_war "Caesar's civil war"), leading to its conquest by the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") in 30 BC. Kush persisted as a major regional power until, having been weakened by internal rebellion amid worsening climatic conditions, invasions by [Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum "Kingdom of Aksum") and the [Noba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noba "Noba") caused its disintegration into [Makuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuria "Makuria"), [Alodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alodia "Alodia"), and [Nobatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobatia "Nobatia") around the 5th century AD.

#### Horn of Africa

In the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa"), there was the [Land of Punt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_Punt "Land of Punt"), a kingdom on the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea") which was a close trading partner of Ancient Egypt in the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC. Archaeologist [Rodolfo Fattovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodolfo_Fattovich "Rodolfo Fattovich") equates it to the [Gash Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gash_Group "Gash Group") in the Sudanese-Eritrean lowlands, and some scholars have hypothesised modern-day [Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia "Somalia"), while [Kenneth Kitchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Kitchen "Kenneth Kitchen") and [Felix Chami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Chami "Felix Chami") locate it on [Zanzibar Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar_Island "Zanzibar Island"). In the [Eritrean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean_Highlands "Eritrean Highlands")-[Ethiopian Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Highlands "Ethiopian Highlands"), the kingdom of [dʿmt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%CA%BFmt "Dʿmt") rose c. 980 BC as the region was incorporated into global trading networks, and it exhibited [Sabaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheba "Sheba") influences which most scholars attribute to a small migration of Sabaeans and their assimilation. Several scholars consider there to have been other contemporaneous states, and dʿmt's collapse in the mid-1st century BC saw the region inhabited by small polities.

Modern-day Somalia was inhabited by [nomadic pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_pastoralism "Nomadic pastoralism"), and along the Horn's coast many [ancient Somali city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Somali_city-states "Ancient Somali city-states") thrived off of the [wider Red Sea trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_maritime_history "Somali maritime history") and enjoyed a lucrative monopoly on cinnamon from India due to their freedom from Roman interference. In the 1st century AD, the [Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum "Kingdom of Aksum") rose from a city-state to rule much of the northern Ethiopian-Eritrean Highlands and the Red Sea port of [Adulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adulis "Adulis"). Aksum was described as one of the four great powers by Persian prophet [Mani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mani_(prophet) "Mani (prophet)") in the 3rd century. King [Ezana of Axum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezana_of_Axum "Ezana of Axum") converted from [traditional religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum#Religion "Kingdom of Aksum") to [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") in the 4th century, gradually followed by the population. In the 6th century, Aksum [conquered South Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite_invasion_of_Himyar "Aksumite invasion of Himyar"), though it struggled to maintain control over the region, and began to gradually lose its dominance over Red Sea trade to Persians and Arabs.

#### Northwest Africa

Northwest Africa (the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb "Maghreb")) was inhabited by [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers "Berbers") semi-nomadic pastoralists. In the 1st millennium BC, [Phoenician migration and settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_settlement_of_North_Africa "Phoenician settlement of North Africa") came in search of precious metals in the [Gulf of Tunis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tunis "Gulf of Tunis"). This grew into [Ancient Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage "Ancient Carthage") after gaining independence from [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") in the 6th century BC, and they built an [extensive trading empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage#Independence,_expansion_and_hegemony "Ancient Carthage") with a strict mercantile network. Carthage's collapse and conquest by Rome in the [Punic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punic_Wars "Punic Wars") (3rd and 2nd centuries BC) saw [Numidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numidia "Numidia") and [Mauretania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauretania "Mauretania") become major powers in the Maghreb.

Towards the end of the 2nd century BC, Mauretania fought alongside King [Jugurtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jugurtha "Jugurtha") of Numidia against the Romans in the [Jugurthine War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jugurthine_War "Jugurthine War") after he had usurped the Numidian throne from a Roman ally. Together they inflicted heavy casualties, with the war ending inconclusively when King [Bocchus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bocchus_I "Bocchus I") of Mauretania sold out Jugurtha to the Romans. Around the turn of the millennium, both came under direct Roman rule. While [Berber traditional religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_traditional_religion "Berber traditional religion") predominated, some converted to Christianity. In the 5th century AD, the [Vandals conquered Roman Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandal_conquest_of_Roman_Africa "Vandal conquest of Roman Africa") before the [fall of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Rome "Fall of Rome"), though the province was reconquered by the [Byzantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") in the [Vandalic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandalic_War "Vandalic War"). Large swaths of indigenous peoples regained self-governance in [Masuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauro-Roman_Kingdom "Mauro-Roman Kingdom") and its [numerous successor polities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romano-Berber_kingdoms "Romano-Berber kingdoms") in the Maghreb, including the kingdoms of [Ouarsenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ouarsenis "Kingdom of Ouarsenis"), [Aurès](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_Aur%C3%A8s "Kingdom of the Aurès"), and [Altava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Altava "Kingdom of Altava").

#### West Africa

In the western [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel"), the rise of settled communities occurred largely as a result of the domestication of [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet "Millet") and of [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum"), and cattle [pastoralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism "Pastoralism") began c. 2500 BC. Extensive east-west belts of deserts, [grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sudanian_savanna "West Sudanian savanna"), and [forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinean_forest%E2%80%93savanna_mosaic "Guinean forest–savanna mosaic") from north to south were crucial for the moulding of their respective societies and meant that symbiotic trade relations developed in response to the differing environments. Beginning around 4000 BC, the [Tichitt culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tichitt_tradition "Tichitt tradition") in modern-day Mauritania and Mali is the oldest known [complexly organised society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_society "Complex society") in West Africa, while others included the [Kintampo culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kintampo_culture "Kintampo culture") in modern-day Ghana, the [Nok culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nok_culture "Nok culture") in modern-day Nigeria, and the [Daima culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daima "Daima") around [Lake Chad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Chad "Lake Chad").

Towards the end of the 3rd century AD, a [wet period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsoon#Africa_(West_African_and_Southeast_African) "Monsoon") in the Sahel created areas for human habitation and exploitation that had not been habitable for the best part of a millennium. The [Ghana Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire "Ghana Empire") (also called *Wagadu*) rose out of the Tichitt culture, growing wealthy following the introduction of the camel to the western Sahel, which revolutionised the [trans-Saharan trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade "Trans-Saharan trade") that linked their capital and [Aoudaghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aoudaghost "Aoudaghost") with [Tahert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahert "Tahert") and [Sijilmasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sijilmasa "Sijilmasa") in North Africa. [Soninke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soninke_people "Soninke people") tradition holds that the final founding of Wagadu occurred after [Dinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinga_(ruler) "Dinga (ruler)") did a deal with [Bida](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bida_(mythology)&action=edit&redlink=1 "Bida (mythology) (page does not exist)") [[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bida_(mythologie) "fr:Bida (mythologie)")], a serpent deity who was guarding a well, to sacrifice one maiden a year in exchange for assurance regarding plenty of rainfall and gold supply. Based on [large tumuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumulus#Africa "Tumulus") scattered across West Africa dating to this period, several scholars have speculated that there were further simultaneous and preceding states relative to Wagadu.

#### Central, Eastern, and Southern Africa

In the [grasslands of Cameroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_High_Plateau "Western High Plateau"), [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages "Bantu languages")-speaking agriculturalists [migrated southward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion "Bantu expansion") sometime between 5000 BC and 3000 BC. Despite intensive research, the cause of the migrations, and that of the directions taken, is still unclear; however, there is consensus that there were multiple dispersal events. Around 1500 BC, Bantu speakers reached central Cameroon. The 'Western Stream' likely followed the coast and the major rivers of the [Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_River "Congo River") system southwards to reach the southern fringe of the [Congolian Rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congolian_rainforests "Congolian rainforests") around 500 BC (some may have used the sea to circumvent the rainforest). Their arrival coincided with the spread of [iron metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_metallurgy_in_Africa "Iron metallurgy in Africa") through Central Africa.

Meanwhile, the 'Eastern Stream' travelled either the northern fringe of the rainforest or the [Ubangi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubangi_River "Ubangi River") eastwards, and reached just west of [Lake Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Victoria "Lake Victoria") around 500 BC. While there, Bantu speakers adopted iron metallurgy from [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages "Cushitic languages") speakers already present, and coexisted with them. Dispersal from the [Great Lakes region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Great_Lakes "African Great Lakes") occurred in two more streams. One went west to meet the Western Stream in the DR Congo and Angola, while the other went south and spread across Eastern and Southern Africa.

Around the turn of the millennium, Bantu speakers reached central modern-day Tanzania and near [Dar es Salaam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dar_es_Salaam "Dar es Salaam"), before rapidly moving southwards along the coast to reach modern-day [KwaZulu Natal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwazulu_Natal "Kwazulu Natal") in South Africa around the 3rd century AD. Throughout this, Bantu speakers displaced, replaced, or intermarried with and absorbed hunter-gatherer and agricultural groups.

### c. 600 – c. 1800

#### North Africa, the Central Sahel and Sudan, and the Horn

In the 7th and 8th centuries, as part of the [Arab conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests") which sought to [spread Islamic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam "Spread of Islam"), the [Rashidun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate "Rashidun Caliphate") and [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate "Umayyad Caliphate") caliphates conquered North Africa at the expense of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") and Berber leaders such as [Kusaila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kusaila "Kusaila") and [Kahina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahina "Kahina"). Large numbers of Berber and [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_people "Coptic people") people willingly converted to Islam, and followers of [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") ('[People of the Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_the_Book "People of the Book")') were protected, though followers of Berber traditional religion were violently oppressed. In the 8th century the [Berber Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_Revolt "Berber Revolt") rocked the caliphate, and Berber dynasties took control over the Maghreb, while the [Tulunids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulunids "Tulunids") briefly controlled Egypt.

In the 10th century, the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate "Fatimid Caliphate") rose to power in modern-day Tunisia, established a rival caliphate, and [conquered Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_conquest_of_Egypt "Fatimid conquest of Egypt"), before expanding into the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"). In the 11th century, the [Almoravids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almoravid_dynasty "Almoravid dynasty") conquered the Maghreb and intervened in the Christian [reconquest of Iberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista"). [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin "Saladin"), a Fatimid [vizier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vizier "Vizier"), usurped power in the 12th century and established the [Ayyubid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty "Ayyubid dynasty"), which restored Egyptian prestige, while the [Almohad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almohad_Caliphate "Almohad Caliphate") revolution deposed the Almoravids to the east. After Christian gains in Iberia in the 13th century, the Almohads disintegrated into the [Marinids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marinids "Marinids"), [Zayyanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zayyanids "Zayyanids"), and [Hafsids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafsids "Hafsids") in modern-day Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia, respectively.

In the face of [Mongol expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasion_of_Syria "Mongol invasion of Syria"), [Mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk "Mamluk") generals [seized power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate "Mamluk Sultanate") in Egypt and expanded into the Middle East. In the early 16th century, the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") rapidly [conquered North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_wars_in_Africa "Ottoman wars in Africa") (save for Morocco under [Saadi rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadi_Sultanate "Saadi Sultanate")) to counter [Spanish expansionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars#Clash_of_interests_in_the_Mediterranean_and_Europe "Spanish–Ottoman wars"). In the following centuries, over a million Europeans are estimated to have been [captured and enslaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_slave_trade "Barbary slave trade") by [Barbary corsairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_corsairs "Barbary corsairs"). The [Ottoman regencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Eyalets_of_the_Ottoman_Empire_in_Africa "Category:Eyalets of the Ottoman Empire in Africa") became more independent during the 18th century.

In the central Sahel around Lake Chad, the [Kanem–Bornu Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanem%E2%80%93Bornu_Empire "Kanem–Bornu Empire") ruled from the 6th century, and it projected power over the [Hausa Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_Kingdoms "Hausa Kingdoms"). To the east during the 7th century, [Makuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuria "Makuria") conquered [Nobatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobatia "Nobatia") to become the dominant power in the region and [resisted Muslim expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_battle_of_Dongola "First battle of Dongola"). They later entered a severe decline following civil war and [Arab migrations to the Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization_of_the_Sudan_region "Islamization of the Sudan region") and had disintegrated by the 15th century, giving rise to the [Funj Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funj_Sultanate "Funj Sultanate"). The [Tunjur kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunjur_kingdom "Tunjur kingdom")'s collapse in the central Sudan saw the rise of the [Bagirmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Bagirmi "Sultanate of Bagirmi"), [Wadai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadai_Sultanate "Wadai Sultanate"), and [Darfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Darfur "Sultanate of Darfur") sultanates, which frequently raided the populations to their south for slaves.

In the Horn of Africa, Aksum lost control of the Red Sea, and its isolation from Christendom caused Ethiopian society to introspect and take inspiration from the [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament"). Islamised [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_kingdoms "Beja kingdoms") invaded the Ethiopian Highlands, forcing Aksum to migrate south, where, according to tradition, Aksum's expansion was met by a [Jewish or traditionalist queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudit "Gudit") who destroyed the state c. 960. During the 13th century, hegemony in the Horn was contested by the Christian [Zagwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagwe_dynasty "Zagwe dynasty"), the Muslims of [Shewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Shewa "Sultanate of Shewa"), and the traditionalist [Damot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Damot "Kingdom of Damot"); the [Solomonic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonic_dynasty "Solomonic dynasty") overthrew the Zagwe to found the [Ethiopian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Empire "Ethiopian Empire"), while Shewa was replaced by [Ifat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Ifat "Sultanate of Ifat"). Soon after, Ethiopia [conquered Damot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_Ethiopia#Early_Solomonic_period_to_Zemene_Mesafint "Territorial evolution of Ethiopia") and the Muslim states, entering into a 'golden age'.

In the 16th century, Muslims united behind [Adal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adal_Sultanate "Adal Sultanate") in *[jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihad "Jihad")* to reconquer the Muslim lands and occupy Ethiopia, until, with [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire "Portuguese Empire") assistance, [Ethiopia counter-attacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wayna_Daga "Battle of Wayna Daga"), and its sovereignty was restored in a reduced state. The 16th and 17th centuries also saw [Oromo invasions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_expansion "Oromo expansion") into the Horn, weakening Ethiopia further and causing the collapse of Adal, while [Ajuran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajuran_Sultanate "Ajuran Sultanate") on the east coast also disintegrated. Ethiopia briefly consolidated its authority in the late-17th century, though the turn of the century saw [fragmentation and gradually increasing anarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zemene_Mesafint "Zemene Mesafint"), which continued throughout the 18th century.

#### West Africa

The [Ghana Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire "Ghana Empire") formed in the Sahel between the 2nd and 8th centuries, while from the 7th century the [Gao Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao_Empire "Gao Empire") ruled to its east. Almoravid capture of royal [Aoudaghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aoudaghost "Aoudaghost") led to Ghana's conversion to Islam in the 11th century, and climatic changes led to Ghana's conquest by its vassal [Sosso Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sosso_Empire "Sosso Empire") in the 13th century. Sosso was quickly overthrown by the [Mali Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire "Mali Empire"), which conquered Gao and dominated the trans-Saharan trade. The [Mossi Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_Kingdoms "Mossi Kingdoms") were established to its south.

The 15th century saw the crumbling of the Mali Empire, with the dominant power in the region becoming the [Songhai Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_Empire "Songhai Empire") centered on [Gao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao "Gao"). Weakened by internal conflict, in 1591 Gao was [conquered by Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_invasion_of_the_Songhai_Empire "Moroccan invasion of the Songhai Empire"), and the political vacuum came to be filled by the [Segou Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9gou_Empire "Ségou Empire") and several other states. The once lucrative trans-Saharan trade gradually lost its prominence to the coastal Atlantic trade with Europeans, and the following centuries saw many droughts, famines, and epidemics.

In the [forest regions of West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinean_forest%E2%80%93savanna_mosaic "Guinean forest–savanna mosaic"), various kingdoms and empires flourished, such as the [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people "Yoruba people") empires of [Ife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ife_Empire "Ife Empire") and [Oyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyo_Empire "Oyo Empire"), the [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people "Igbo people") [Kingdom of Nri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Nri "Kingdom of Nri"), the [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_people "Edo people") [Kingdom of Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Benin "Kingdom of Benin") (famous for [its art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_the_Kingdom_of_Benin "Art of the Kingdom of Benin")), the [Dagomba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagomba_people "Dagomba people") [Kingdom of Dagbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Dagbon "Kingdom of Dagbon"), and the [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people "Akan people") kingdoms of [Bonoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonoman "Bonoman") and [Adanse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adanse "Adanse"). They came into contact with the Portuguese in the 15th century, which saw the start of the [Atlantic slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade "Atlantic slave trade"). The [Asante Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asante_Empire "Asante Empire") was established in present-day Ghana. Between 1515 and 1800, 8 million Africans were exported in the Atlantic slave trade.

#### Central Africa

In the western [Congo Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Basin "Congo Basin"), by the 13th century, there were three main confederations of states: the [Seven Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Kingdoms_of_Kongo_dia_Nlaza "Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza"), [Mpemba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mpemba "Mpemba"), and one led by [Vungu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vungu "Vungu"). In the 14th century, the [Kingdom of Kongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kongo "Kingdom of Kongo") emerged and dominated the region, with the [Tio Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tio_Kingdom "Tio Kingdom") to its north and [Mwene Muji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mwene_Muji "Mwene Muji") to its northeast. Portuguese arrival in the 15th century saw Kongo's ruler willingly [convert to Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Kongo "Catholic Church in Kongo") and the start of the Atlantic trade, with slaves being the most lucrative export; in the 16th century, [Afonso I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afonso_I_of_Kongo "Afonso I of Kongo")'s conquests produced many captives for Kongo's [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly "Monopoly"), and the slave trade rapidly grew to meet demand in the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"). Over the course of the late-16th and 17th centuries, the Portuguese sought to conquer [Ndongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ndongo "Kingdom of Ndongo") (allied to [Matamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Matamba "Kingdom of Matamba")) in the [Angolan Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angolan_Wars "Angolan Wars"); despite fierce resistance from Queen [Nzinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba "Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba"), and brief [occupation of Luanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Loango-Angola "Dutch Loango-Angola") by the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_colonial_empire "Dutch colonial empire") (allied to Kongo), Portugal controlled most of Ndongo by the 1670s. The late-17th century saw Kongo [fragment between royal houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongo_Civil_War "Kongo Civil War") as several vassals broke away; by the time of Kongo's restoration in 1709, it had ceased being the dominant regional power. Also during the 17th century, in the [textile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuba_textiles "Kuba textiles")-producing northeast, the [Boma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boma_Kingdom "Boma Kingdom"), [Yaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yaka_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Yaka Kingdom (page does not exist)"), and possibly the [Kuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuba_Kingdom "Kuba Kingdom") and [Pende](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pende_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Pende Kingdom (page does not exist)") kingdoms broke away from Mwene Muji.

In the eastern Congo Basin, farmers, fishermen, and hunters (often Pygmies) built symbiotic relations. [Jan Vansina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Vansina "Jan Vansina") wrote that in the [southern savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Congolian_forest%E2%80%93savanna_mosaic "Southern Congolian forest–savanna mosaic") 'lords of the land' held priestly roles due to their special relationship with the spirits of the land, and held sway over multiple villages, effectively ruling embryonic kingdoms. Accordingly, as lineages grew, authority was opportunistically absorbed or incorporated by force. The [centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabongo,_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo "Kabongo, Democratic Republic of the Congo") of the [Luba Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luba_Empire "Luba Empire") is yet to undergo archaeological research, meaning knowledge about its rise is limited; its foundation is typically dated to the 14th/15th centuries, though scholarly estimates range from the 8th to 17th centuries. A [Lunda state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunda_Empire "Lunda Empire") formed in the [Nkalany Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nkalany_River "Nkalany River") (estimates range from c. 1450 to c. 1700), in the 18th century, Lunda rapidly expanded into an extensive commonwealth across the southern savanna, using the institutions of perpetual kinship and positional succession to incorporate peoples. Meanwhile, Luba expanded its number of [tributaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary_state "Tributary state"), in some cases using its widely acknowledged prestige to intervene in succession disputes and distribute ritually powerful royal regalia related to *bulopwe* (sacred kingship), in other cases using conquest.

In the northern Great Lakes, the [Empire of Kitara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Kitara "Empire of Kitara") rose around the 11th century, famed for [its oral traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Kitara#Legends "Empire of Kitara"). Historian Peter Robertshaw considers its rulers (the [Chwezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chwezi "Chwezi")) to have been religious leaders who utilised networks of hill-top shrines to project authority. Around 1500, severe droughts and famine destabilised the region, occurring again during the early-17th and 18th centuries. [Luo-speaking peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luo_peoples "Luo peoples") migrated from the Upper Nile basin into modern-day Uganda, and a [Biito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biito_clan "Biito clan") dynasty replaced the Chwezi in [Bunyoro-Kitara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunyoro "Bunyoro"). Over the following centuries, the kingdoms of Bunyoro-Kitara, [Buganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buganda "Buganda"), and [Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Rwanda "Kingdom of Rwanda") struggled for hegemony among others. Throughout the 16th and 17th centuries, Bunyoro raided states to the south such as [Nkore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nkore "Nkore") and Rwanda. [Mpororo](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mpororo&action=edit&redlink=1 "Mpororo (page does not exist)") rose and fragmented during the 18th century, while Buganda rapidly expanded at the expense of Bunyoro to become the major power in the region.

#### Eastern and Southern Africa

The East African interior was inhabited by pastoralist [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages "Cushitic languages") and [Nilotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilotic_languages "Nilotic languages") speakers, along with Bantu speakers who continued to expand and adapt to their lands while absorbing the former two; the 17th century saw the establishment of [tributary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribute "Tribute") systems that stabilised the region. On the [Swahili coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_coast "Swahili coast") the [Swahili city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_city-states "Swahili city-states") thrived off of the [Indian Ocean trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade "Indian Ocean trade") and gradually Islamised, giving rise to the [Kilwa Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilwa_Sultanate "Kilwa Sultanate") from the 10th century. In the 15th century, trade routes for Zimbabwean gold shifted north, weakening Kilwa. The Portuguese arrived at the coast soon after and exploited divisions between coastal city-states to gain control over them while utilising a powerful navy. In the 17th and 18th centuries, [Lamu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamu_Archipelago "Lamu Archipelago")-Swahili cities rebelled against the Portuguese and allied with the [Omani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omani_Empire "Omani Empire"), which supplanted Portuguese rule.

Madagascar was settled by [Austronesian peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_peoples "Austronesian peoples") between the 5th and 7th centuries, as societies organised at the behest of *[hasina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasina_(Madagascar) "Hasina (Madagascar)")* and competed with one another over the [island's estuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rivers_of_Madagascar "List of rivers of Madagascar") and [bridgeheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgehead "Bridgehead"). From around the 15th century, southern Madagascar became integrated into global trade; the [Sakalava Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakalava_Empire "Sakalava Empire") rose to dominate west-coastal trade, accompanied by numerous other states, and the island saw several failed European colonial ventures.

In Southern Africa, an early kingdom was [Mapungubwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Mapungubwe "Kingdom of Mapungubwe"), which collapsed c. 1300 as [Great Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Zimbabwe "Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe"), with its monumental dry-stone walls, rose to become the region's main political and economic centre. Following shifting trade routes north to the [Zambezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambezi "Zambezi") and unclear circumstances, Great Zimbabwe entered into decline and was succeeded by the [Mutapa Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutapa_Empire "Mutapa Empire") and [Kingdom of Butua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Butua "Kingdom of Butua") in the 15th century. Mutapa was briefly vassalised by the Portuguese in the 17th century, the latter half of which saw widespread famine and disease, until Changamire Dombo revolted in the 1680s, conquered Butua to found the [Rozvi Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rozvi_Empire "Rozvi Empire"), and expelled all Portuguese from the [Zimbabwean plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwean_plateau "Zimbabwean plateau"). [Maravi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maravi "Maravi") around [Lake Malawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Malawi "Lake Malawi") expanded in the 17th century, but the collapse of its succession system in the 18th century precipitated its disintegration.

By the late-1st millennium CE, Bantu-speaking farmers populated most of Southern Africa's cultivable savannas, reaching southwards to the [Kei River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kei_River "Kei River"); they traded goods such as livestock, grain, and metal tools between communities and with hunter-gatherers. Around the 11th century, more agriculturists—who likely brought with them ancestral [Nguni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguni_languages "Nguni languages") and [Sotho-Tswana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sotho%E2%80%93Tswana_languages "Sotho–Tswana languages") languages—migrated across the [Limpopo River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limpopo_River "Limpopo River") and came to dominate the earlier farmers both politically and culturally, organising into loosely-defined chiefdoms that held dynamic alliance networks. Further south, the Dutch began colonizing the [western Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Cape "Western Cape") in the 17th century, and Britain seized control of the [Dutch Cape Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Cape_Colony "Dutch Cape Colony") around the turn of the 19th century. In the late-18th century, the interplay of pre-existing trends of political centralisation with the effects of international trade, environmental instability, and European colonisation set the *[Mfecane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mfecane "Mfecane")* ("Crushing") in motion, as various African states were founded.

### c. 1800 – c. 1935

By the turn of the 19th century, many of Africa's economic and political structures were unstable and brittle as a result of long-term effects of the slave trade. In Egypt, [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt "Muhammad Ali of Egypt") led a national movement to seize power from Ottoman suzerainty, instituting sweeping reforms and greatly strengthening Egyptian power by militaristic expansion. Meanwhile, in West Africa, the [Fula *jihad*s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fula_jihads "Fula jihads"), which had been gaining momentum throughout the 18th century, culminated in the establishment of the expansive [Sokoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate "Sokoto Caliphate") and [Hamdullahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate_of_Hamdullahi "Caliphate of Hamdullahi") caliphates. In the Horn, Ethiopia underwent reforms, seeking to revive the imperial state, something that was later achieved by [Menelik II's conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II%27s_conquests "Menelik II's conquests"). In Southern Africa, the *[Mfecane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mfecane "Mfecane")* propelled large long-distance migrations that caused widespread disruption across the vast region, while several [Boer republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boer_republics "Boer republics") were founded.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] European campaigns to abolish the Atlantic slave trade led to supply refocusing on internal markets and saw the rapid growth of the [Indian Ocean slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_slave_trade "Indian Ocean slave trade"). Across the continent, many rulers attempted reforms and indigenous forms of 'modernisation'; however, these efforts were consistently undermined by European traders and missionaries as the tide of European encroachment rose.

For 400 years, European nations had mainly limited their involvement to trading stations on the African coast, with few daring to venture inland. The [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution "Second Industrial Revolution") in Europe produced several technological innovations which assisted them in overcoming this 400-year pattern. One was the development of [repeating rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeating_rifle "Repeating rifle"), which were easier and quicker to load than [muskets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musket "Musket"). [Artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery "Artillery") was being used increasingly. In 1885, [Hiram Maxim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiram_Maxim "Hiram Maxim") developed the [Maxim gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun "Maxim gun"), the model of the modern-day [machine gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_gun "Machine gun"). European states kept these weapons largely among themselves by refusing to sell these weapons to African leaders. African germs took numerous European lives and deterred [permanent settlements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler_colonialism "Settler colonialism"). Diseases such as [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever "Yellow fever"), [sleeping sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis "African trypanosomiasis"), [yaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaws "Yaws"), and [leprosy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprosy "Leprosy") made Africa a very inhospitable place for Europeans. The deadliest disease was [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria"), endemic throughout [Tropical Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrotropical_realm "Afrotropical realm"). In 1854, the discovery of [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine") and other medical innovations helped to make conquest and colonisation in Africa possible.

There were strong motives for the conquest of Africa. [Raw materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources_of_Africa "Natural resources of Africa") were needed for European factories, and free trade was championed to prevent Africans from regulating their economies. Prestige and imperial rivalries were at play. Acquiring African colonies would show rivals that a nation was powerful and significant. These contextual factors forged the [Scramble for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa "Scramble for Africa"); during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the [European powers partitioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference "Berlin Conference") and conquered almost all of Africa despite fierce resistance (only Ethiopia and [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia "Liberia") remained independent). Traditional leaders were incorporated into the colonial regimes as a form of indirect rule to extract human and natural resources and curb organised resistance. Colonial borders were drawn unilaterally by the Europeans, often cutting across bonds of kinship, language, culture, and established routes, and sometimes incorporating groups who previously had little in common. The threat to trade routes was mitigated by poor policing and African entrepreneurs (viewed as smugglers) who exploited the differing tax and legal schemes. The first few decades of European colonialism saw intense violence, disorder, and atrocities, such as that of the [Congo Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State "Atrocities in the Congo Free State"), [German South West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide "Herero and Nama genocide"), and [French Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_genocide "Algerian genocide"), with the late colonial period relatively peaceful in comparison. Colonial rule had [wide-reaching effects on African societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_European_colonialism_and_colonization "Analysis of European colonialism and colonization"), such as the [spread of Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_colonialism "Christianity and colonialism") as part of the '[civilising mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilising_mission "Civilising mission")'. African culture was strongly denigrated and persecuted, as pervasive racial discrimination deepened the scars of humiliation.

### Independence struggles

Imperial rule by Europeans continued until after the conclusion of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), when almost all remaining colonial territories gradually obtained formal independence. [Independence movements in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonisation_of_Africa "Decolonisation of Africa") gained momentum following World War II, which left the major European powers weakened. In 1951, Libya, a former Italian colony, gained independence. In 1956, Tunisia and Morocco won their independence from France. Ghana followed suit in 1957, becoming the first of the sub-Saharan colonies to be granted independence. Over the next decade, waves of decolonisation took place across the continent, culminating in the 1960 [Year of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_Africa "Year of Africa") and the establishment of the [Organisation of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity "Organisation of African Unity") in 1963.

Portugal's overseas presence in sub-Saharan Africa (most notably in [Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Angola "Portuguese Angola"), Cape Verde, [Mozambique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Mozambique "Portuguese Mozambique"), [Guinea-Bissau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Guinea "Portuguese Guinea"), and São Tomé and Príncipe) lasted from the 16th century to 1975, after the [Estado Novo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estado_Novo_(Portugal) "Estado Novo (Portugal)") regime was overthrown in [a military coup in Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnation_Revolution "Carnation Revolution"). [Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesia "Rhodesia") [unilaterally declared independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesia%27s_Unilateral_Declaration_of_Independence "Rhodesia's Unilateral Declaration of Independence") from the United Kingdom in 1965, under the white minority government of [Ian Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Smith "Ian Smith"), but it was not internationally recognised as an independent state (as [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe "Zimbabwe")) until 1980, when black nationalists gained power after a [bitter guerrilla war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesian_Bush_War "Rhodesian Bush War"). Although South Africa was one of the first African countries to gain independence, the state remained under the control of the country's white minority, initially through qualified voting rights and from 1956 by a system of [racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation "Racial segregation") known as [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid "Apartheid"), until 1994.

### Post-colonial Africa

As of 2025, Africa contains 54 sovereign countries. Since independence, African states have frequently been hampered by instability, corruption, violence, and authoritarianism. The vast majority of African states are republics that operate under some form of the [presidential system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_system "Presidential system") of rule. However, few of them have been able to sustain democratic governments on a permanent basis—per the criteria laid out by Lührmann et al. (2018), only [Botswana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botswana "Botswana") and [Mauritius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritius "Mauritius") have been consistently democratic for the entirety of their post-colonial history. Most African countries have experienced several coups or periods of [military dictatorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_dictatorship "Military dictatorship"). Between 1990 and 2018, though, the continent as a whole has trended towards more democratic governance.

Upon independence, an overwhelming majority of Africans lived in extreme poverty. The continent suffered from a lack of infrastructural or industrial development under colonial rule, along with political instability. With limited financial resources or access to global markets, relatively stable countries such as [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya") still experienced only very slow economic development. Only a handful of countries succeeded in obtaining rapid economic growth prior to 1990. Exceptions include Libya and Equatorial Guinea, both of which possess large oil reserves.

Instability after decolonisation resulted primarily from [marginalisation of ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_racism "Institutional racism"), as well as corruption. In pursuit of personal political gain, many leaders deliberately promoted ethnic conflicts, some of which had originated during the colonial period, such as from the grouping of multiple unrelated ethnic groups into a single colony, the splitting of a distinct ethnic group between multiple colonies, or existing conflicts being exacerbated by colonial rule (for instance, the preferential treatment given to ethnic [Hutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutu "Hutu") over [Tutsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutsi "Tutsi") in Rwanda during German and Belgian rule).

Faced with increasingly frequent and severe violence, military rule was widely accepted by the population of many countries as a means to maintain order, and during the 1970s and 1980s a majority of African countries were controlled by military dictatorships. Territorial disputes between nations and rebellions by groups seeking independence were also common in independent African states. The most devastating of these was the [Nigerian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigerian_Civil_War "Nigerian Civil War"), fought between government forces and an [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people "Igbo people") [separatist republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biafra "Biafra"), which resulted in a famine that killed 1–2 million people. Two civil wars in Sudan, [the first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sudanese_Civil_War "First Sudanese Civil War") lasting from 1955 to 1972 and [the second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sudanese_Civil_War "Second Sudanese Civil War") from 1983 to 2005, collectively killed around 3 million. Both were fought primarily on ethnic and religious lines.

[Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") conflicts between the United States and the Soviet Union also contributed to instability. Both the Soviet Union and the United States offered considerable incentives to political and military leaders who aligned themselves with the superpowers' foreign policy. As an example, during the [Angolan Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angolan_Civil_War "Angolan Civil War"), the Soviet- and Cuban-aligned [MPLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPLA "MPLA") and the American-aligned [UNITA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNITA "UNITA") received the vast majority of their military and political support from these countries. Many African countries became highly dependent on foreign aid. The sudden loss of both Soviet and American aid at the end of the Cold War and the [fall of the USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_USSR "Fall of the USSR") resulted in severe economic and political turmoil in the countries most dependent on foreign support.

There was a [major famine in Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983%E2%80%931985_famine_in_Ethiopia "1983–1985 famine in Ethiopia") between 1983 and 1985, killing up to 1.2 million people, which most historians attribute primarily to the forced relocation of farmworkers and seizure of grain by the communist [Derg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derg "Derg") government, further exacerbated by the [Ethiopian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Civil_War "Ethiopian Civil War"). In 1994, a [genocide in Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwandan_genocide "Rwandan genocide") resulted in up to 800,000 deaths, added to [a severe refugee crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_refugee_crisis "Great Lakes refugee crisis") and fuelled the rise of militia groups in neighbouring countries. This contributed to the outbreak of the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War "First Congo War") and [Second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Congo_War "Second Congo War") Congo Wars, which were the most devastating military conflicts in modern Africa, with up to 5.5 million deaths, making it by far the deadliest conflict in modern African history and one of the [costliest wars in human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_wars_by_death_toll "List of wars by death toll"). In 2025, it is estimated that 20% of Africans face hunger in 2025, compared to 6% in Asia, 5% in Latin America and the Caribbean, 8% in Oceania, and less than 2.5% in North America and Europe.

Various conflicts between various insurgent groups and governments continue. From 2003 to 2020, a [conflict in Darfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Darfur "War in Darfur") resulted in around 300,000 deaths. The [Boko Haram insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boko_Haram_insurgency "Boko Haram insurgency") in Sub-Saharan Africa has killed around 350,000 people since 2009. The [Tigray war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray_war "Tigray war") from 2020 to 2022 killed an estimated 300,000–500,000 people, primarily due to [famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_in_the_Tigray_War "Famine in the Tigray War"). Overall, violence across Africa has greatly declined in the 21st century, with the end of civil wars in Angola, [Sierra Leone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Leone_Civil_War "Sierra Leone Civil War"), and [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_Civil_War "Algerian Civil War") in 2002, [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Liberian_Civil_War "Second Liberian Civil War") in 2003, and [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sudanese_Civil_War "Second Sudanese Civil War") and [Burundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burundian_Civil_War "Burundian Civil War") in 2005. The Second Congo War, which involved 9 countries and several insurgent groups, ended in 2003. This decline in violence coincided with many countries abandoning communist-style command economies and opening up for market reforms, which over the course of the 1990s and 2000s promoted the establishment of permanent, peaceful trade between neighbouring countries.

Improved stability and economic reforms have led to a great increase in foreign investment into many nations, mainly from China, which further spurred economic growth. Between 2000 and 2014, annual GDP growth in sub-Saharan Africa averaged 5%, doubling its total GDP from $811 billion to $1.63 trillion (constant 2015 [USD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD "USD")). North Africa experienced comparable growth rates. A significant part of this growth can also be attributed to the facilitated diffusion of information technologies, specifically the mobile telephone. While several individual countries have maintained high growth rates, since 2014 overall growth has considerably slowed, primarily as a result of falling commodity prices, continued lack of industrialisation, and epidemics such as [Ebola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic "Western African Ebola virus epidemic") and [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Africa "COVID-19 pandemic in Africa").

## Geography

Africa is the largest of the three great southward projections from the largest [landmass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landmass "Landmass") of the Earth. Separated from Europe by the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea"), it is joined to Asia at its northeast extremity by the [Isthmus of Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmus_of_Suez "Isthmus of Suez") (transected by the [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal "Suez Canal")), 163 km (101 mi) wide. Geopolitically, Egypt's [Sinai Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula "Sinai Peninsula") east of the Suez Canal is often considered part of Africa as well.

The coastline is 26,000 km (16,000 mi) long, and the absence of deep indentations of the shore is illustrated by the fact that Europe, which covers only 10,400,000 km2 (4,000,000 sq mi) – about a third of the surface of Africa – has a coastline of 32,000 km (20,000 mi). From the most northerly point, [Ras ben Sakka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ras_ben_Sakka "Ras ben Sakka") in Tunisia (37°21' N), to the most southerly point, [Cape Agulhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Agulhas "Cape Agulhas") in South Africa (34°51'15" S), is a distance of approximately 8,000 km (5,000 mi). [Cape Verde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cap-Vert "Cap-Vert"), 17°33'22" W, the westernmost point, is a distance of approximately 7,400 km (4,600 mi) to [Ras Hafun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ras_Hafun "Ras Hafun"), 51°27'52" E, the most easterly projection that neighbours [Cape Guardafui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Guardafui "Cape Guardafui"), the tip of the Horn of Africa.

The largest country is Algeria, and the smallest is [Seychelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seychelles "Seychelles"), an [archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago "Archipelago") off the east coast. The smallest nation on the continental mainland is [The Gambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gambia "The Gambia").

### African plate

The [African plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_plate "African plate"), also known as the Nubian plate, is a [major tectonic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tectonic_plates#Major_plates "List of tectonic plates") that includes most of the [continent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent") of Africa (except for its [easternmost part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa")) and the adjacent oceanic crust to the west and south. It also includes a narrow strip of [Western Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia "Western Asia") along the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea"), including much of [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel"), [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine "Palestine") and [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon "Lebanon"). It is bounded by the [North American plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_plate "North American plate") and [South American plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_American_plate "South American plate") to the west (separated by the [Mid-Atlantic Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_Ridge "Mid-Atlantic Ridge")); the [Arabian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_plate "Arabian plate") and [Somali plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_plate "Somali plate") to the east; the [Eurasian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_plate "Eurasian plate"), [Aegean Sea plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea_plate "Aegean Sea plate") and [Anatolian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolian_plate "Anatolian plate") to the north; and the [Antarctic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_plate "Antarctic plate") to the south.

Between [60](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=60) million years ago and [10](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=10) million years ago, the [Somali plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_plate "Somali plate") began [rifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift_(geology) "Rift (geology)") from the African plate along the [East African Rift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_African_Rift "East African Rift"). Since the continent of Africa consists of crust from both the African and the Somali plates, some literature refers to the African plate as the *Nubian plate* to distinguish it from the continent as a whole.

### Climate

The [climate of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Africa "Climate of Africa") ranges from [tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate "Tropical climate") to [subarctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subarctic_climate "Subarctic climate") on its highest peaks. Its northern half is primarily [desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert "Desert") or [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid "Arid"), while its central and southern areas contain both [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna "Savanna") plains and dense [jungle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle "Jungle") ([rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest "Rainforest")) regions. In between, there is a convergence, where vegetation patterns such as [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") and [steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steppe "Steppe") dominate. Africa is the hottest continent on Earth, and 60% of the land surface consists of drylands and deserts. The record for the highest-ever recorded temperature, in [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libya "Libya") in 1922 (58 °C (136 °F)), was discredited in 2013.

### Climate change

[Climate change in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Africa "Climate change in Africa") is a serious threat because the continent is one of the most [vulnerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_vulnerability "Climate change vulnerability") regions to the [effects of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change "Effects of climate change"), despite being responsible for less than 4% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Climate change is causing increasingly erratic rainfall patterns, more frequent [extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather "Extreme weather") events such as [droughts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drought "Drought") and [floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood "Flood"), and rising [sea-surface temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature "Sea surface temperature") across the continent. These changes threaten [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_security "Food security") and [water security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_security "Water security"), [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity "Biodiversity"), [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health "Public health"), and [economic development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_development "Economic development"). Africa is currently warming faster than the rest of the world on average.

Climate change intensifies existing socioeconomic vulnerabilities. Large segments of the African population depend on climate-sensitive livelihoods such as agriculture (55 - 62% of the workforce in sub-Saharan Africa) and already live in poverty, heightening their exposure to shocks. Health outcomes worsen as [heat stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperthermia "Hyperthermia"), [vector-borne diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector "Disease vector") (such as [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria") and [dengue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dengue_fever "Dengue fever")), and [malnutrition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malnutrition "Malnutrition") become more prevalent. Over half (56%) of the over 2,000 recorded public health incidents in Africa between 2001 and 2021 were connected to climate change. [Resource scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_depletion "Resource depletion") contributes to [displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_migration "Climate migration") and conflict, particularly in fragile regions. [Urban areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area "Urban area"), often characterized by informal settlements, face heightened risks from flooding and extreme heat.

### Ecology and biodiversity

Africa has over 3,000 [protected areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_area "Protected area"), with 198 marine protected areas, 50 biosphere reserves, and 80 wetland reserves. Significant habitat destruction, increases in human population, and poaching are reducing biological diversity and [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land "Arable land"). Human encroachment, civil unrest and the introduction of non-native species threaten biodiversity. This has been exacerbated by administrative problems, inadequate personnel and funding problems.

[Deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation "Deforestation") is affecting Africa at twice the world rate, according to the [United Nations Environment Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Environment_Programme "United Nations Environment Programme"). The deforestation rate of 4.08 million hectares per year in 2000–2015 declined to 3.45 million hectares per year in 2015–2025. According to the University of Pennsylvania African Studies Center, 31% of pasture lands and 19% of forests and woodlands are classified as degraded, and the deforestation rate is twice the average for the rest of the world. Some sources claim that approximately 90% of the original, virgin forests in West Africa have been destroyed. Over 90% of [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar "Madagascar")'s original forests have been destroyed since the arrival of humans 2,000 years ago. About 65% of agricultural land suffers from [soil degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_retrogression_and_degradation "Soil retrogression and degradation").

### Fauna

Africa boasts perhaps the world's largest combination of density and "range of freedom" of wild animal populations and diversity, with wild populations of large carnivores (such as [lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion "Lion"), [hyenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyena "Hyena"), and [cheetahs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheetah "Cheetah")) and herbivores (such as [buffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_buffalo "African buffalo"), [elephants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant "Elephant"), [camels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel "Camel"), and [giraffes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giraffe "Giraffe")) ranging freely on primarily open non-private plains. It is also home to a variety of "jungle" animals, including snakes, primates and aquatic life such as crocodiles and amphibians. Africa has the largest number of [megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megafauna "Megafauna") species, as it was least affected by the [extinction of the Pleistocene megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Pleistocene_extinctions "Late Pleistocene extinctions").

### Environmental issues

[African environmental problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_Africa "Environmental issues in Africa") are problems caused by the direct and indirect [human impacts on the natural environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment "Human impact on the environment") and affect humans and nearly all forms of life in Africa. Issues include [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_Africa "Deforestation in Africa"), [soil degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_retrogression_and_degradation "Soil retrogression and degradation"), [air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution "Air pollution"), water pollution, [coastal erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_erosion "Coastal erosion"), garbage pollution, [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Africa "Climate change in Africa"), [Oil spills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_spill "Oil spill"), [Biodiversity loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss "Biodiversity loss"), and [water scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_scarcity_in_Africa "Water scarcity in Africa") (resulting in problems with access to safe [water supply and sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa "Water supply and sanitation in Sub-Saharan Africa")). These issues result in [environmental conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_conflict "Environmental conflict") and are connected to broader social struggles for [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy "Democracy") and [sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereignty "Sovereignty"). The scarcity of climate adaptation techniques in Africa makes it the least resilient continent to climate change.

## Politics

### African Union

The [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union "African Union") (AU) is a [continental union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_union "Continental union") consisting of [55 member states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_states_of_the_African_Union "Member states of the African Union"). The union was formed with [Addis Ababa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_Ababa "Addis Ababa") as its headquarters in 2001 as a successor to the [Organisation of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity "Organisation of African Unity"). The [Pan-African Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-African_Parliament "Pan-African Parliament") (PAP) is located in [Midrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midrand "Midrand"), South Africa.

The African Union, not to be confused with the [AU Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union_Commission "African Union Commission"), is formed by the [Constitutive Act of the African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutive_Act_of_the_African_Union "Constitutive Act of the African Union"), which aims to transform the [African Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Economic_Community "African Economic Community"), a federated commonwealth, into a state under established international conventions. The [Assembly of the African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_of_the_African_Union "Assembly of the African Union") consists of legislative, judicial and executive organs. It is led by the African Union president and head of state, who is also the president of PAP. The president is elected by PAP. The powers and authority of the president of the Assembly derive from the Constitutive Act and the Protocol of the Pan-African Parliament, as well as the inheritance of presidential authority stipulated by African treaties and by international treaties. The government consists of all-union, regional, state, and municipal authorities, as well as hundreds of institutions, that together manage the day-to-day affairs of the institution.

[Extensive human rights abuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Africa "Human rights in Africa") still occur in several parts of Africa, often under the oversight of the state. Most of such violations occur for political reasons, often as a side effect of civil war. Countries where major human rights violations have been reported in recent times include the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Sudan, Zimbabwe, and Ivory Coast.

### Boundary conflicts

African nations have made great efforts to respect international borders as inviolable for a long time. For example, the [Organization of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organization_of_African_Unity "Organization of African Unity") (OAU), which was established in 1963 and replaced by the [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union "African Union") in 2002, set respect for the territorial integrity of each country as one of its principles in the OAU Charter. Indeed, compared with the formation of European countries, there have been fewer international conflicts in Africa over changing the borders, which has influenced country formation there and has enabled some countries to survive that might have been defeated and absorbed by others. Yet international conflicts have played out through support for proxy armies or rebel movements. Many states have experienced civil wars, including Rwanda, Sudan, Angola, Sierra Leone, Congo, Liberia, Ethiopia and Somalia. Wars of national liberation also often took up a Marxist, Marxist–Leninist, or Maoist character in ideological terms, where the independence of African nations was raised in Marxian terms. Early examples included the [Union of the Peoples of Cameroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_the_Peoples_of_Cameroon "Union of the Peoples of Cameroon") armed movement against French colonialism, a Marxist–Leninist insurgency, followed by similar movements in Congo (the [Simba Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simba_Rebellion "Simba Rebellion")). [Che Guevara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Che_Guevara "Che Guevara") participated in the latter, and opined in 1967 that Africa was not ready for revolution. Despite this, in [Zanzibar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar "Zanzibar") before and after unification with Tanzania, forms of socialism took hold. In Sudan under [Gaafar Nimeiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaafar_Nimeiry "Gaafar Nimeiry"), in Somalia under [Siad Barre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siad_Barre "Siad Barre"), in Ethiopia under the Dergue and [Mengistu Haile Mariam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mengistu_Haile_Mariam "Mengistu Haile Mariam"), in Congo-Brazzaville under various administrations from the 1960s onwards, including that of [Denis Sassou-Nguesso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Sassou-Nguesso "Denis Sassou-Nguesso"), in Benin under [Mathieu Kérékou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathieu_Kerekou "Mathieu Kerekou"), in the Seychelles under [France-Albert René](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France-Albert_Ren%C3%A9 "France-Albert René"), in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea-Bissau before Portugal's 1974 Carnation Revolution, Marxism–Leninism was fashionable. Even after 1990, there are some examples of Military Marxism, such as a militia established by [Ernest Wamba-dia-Wamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Wamba-dia-Wamba "Ernest Wamba-dia-Wamba") in Eastern Congo in the 1990s.

## Economy

Although it has abundant natural resources, Africa remains the world's poorest and [least-developed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index "Human Development Index") continent (other than Antarctica), the result of a variety of causes that may include [corrupt governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_Perceptions_Index "Corruption Perceptions Index") that have often committed serious human rights violations, failed central planning, high levels of illiteracy, low self-esteem, lack of access to foreign capital, legacies of colonialism, the slave trade, the Cold War, and frequent tribal and military conflict (ranging from guerrilla warfare to genocide). The continent's total nominal GDP remains behind that of several individual countries, including the United States, China, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom, India and France. According to the United Nations' Human Development Report in 2003, the bottom 24 ranked nations (151st to 175th) were all African.

[Poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_in_Africa "Poverty in Africa"), illiteracy, malnutrition, inadequate [water supply and sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WASH "WASH"), and poor health affect a large proportion of the people. A 2008 [World Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank "World Bank") study estimates 81% of the sub-Saharan African population were living on less than $2.50 (PPP) per day in 2005, compared with 86% for India. Sub-Saharan Africa is the least successful region of the world in reducing poverty; some 50% of the population were living in poverty in 1981 (200 million people), a figure that rose to 58% in 1996 before dropping to 50% in 2005 (380 million people). The average poor person in sub-Saharan Africa is estimated to live on only 70 cents per day and was poorer in 2003 than in 1973, indicating increasing poverty in some areas. Some of it is attributed to unsuccessful economic liberalisation programmes spearheaded by foreign companies and governments, but other studies cite bad domestic government policies more than external factors.

The last debt crisis in 2005 was resolved with help from the [heavily indebted poor countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavily_indebted_poor_countries "Heavily indebted poor countries") scheme, which resulted in some positive and negative effects on the economy in Africa. About 10 years after the 2005 debt crisis in sub-Saharan Africa was resolved, Zambia fell back into debt. One reason was the fall in copper prices in 2011, but the bigger reason was that a large amount of the money Zambia borrowed was wasted or pocketed by the elite.

From 1995 to 2005, Africa's rate of economic growth increased, averaging 5% in 2005. Some countries experienced even higher growth rates, notably Angola, Sudan and Equatorial Guinea, all of which had increased oil production.

The continent is believed to hold 90% of the world's [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt "Cobalt"), 90% of its [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum "Platinum"), 50% of its gold, 98% of its [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium "Chromium"), 70% of its [tantalite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantalite "Tantalite"), 64% of its [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese "Manganese") and one-third of its [uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium "Uranium"). The DRC has 70% of the world's [coltan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coltan "Coltan"), a mineral used in the production of [tantalum capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantalum_capacitor "Tantalum capacitor") for electronic devices such as cell phones. The DRC also has more than 30% of the world's diamond reserves. Guinea is the world's largest exporter of [bauxite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauxite "Bauxite"). As growth in Africa has been driven mainly by services and not manufacturing or agriculture, it has been growth without jobs and without reduction in poverty levels. In fact, the [food security crisis of 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007%E2%80%932008_world_food_price_crisis "2007–2008 world food price crisis") (which took place following the [global financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis "2008 financial crisis")) pushed 100 million people into food insecurity. In 2024, nearly 307 million people were undernourished (46% of the global total), an increase of 10 million people since 2023 and 34 million from 2022.

China has built increasingly stronger ties with African nations and is Africa's largest trading partner. In 2007, Chinese companies invested a total of US$1 billion in Africa.

A [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") study led by researcher [Calestous Juma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calestous_Juma "Calestous Juma") shows that Africa could feed itself by making the transition from importer to self-sufficiency. "African agriculture is at the crossroads; we have come to the end of a century of policies that favoured Africa's export of raw materials and importation of food. Africa is starting to focus on agricultural innovation as its new engine for regional trade and prosperity."

### Energy

[Energy in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Africa "Energy in Africa") is characterised by low [per-capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita "Per capita") consumption and limited access to modern energy services, reflecting both infrastructural and socio-economic challenges across the continent. As of 2023, Africa accounted for just 5.5% of global total final energy consumption, with per-capita energy use more than three times lower than the global average. The [energy mix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_mix "Energy mix") is dominated by [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel "Biofuel") and waste, which make up 43% of total final consumption, primarily for household cooking and heating, followed by oil products at 33%, electricity at 11%, natural gas at 9%, and coal at 4%.

[Electricity access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification "Electrification") remains a major challenge, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, where nearly 450 million rural residents lack reliable electricity, and grid coverage is often inconsistent. [Clean cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_poverty_and_cooking "Energy poverty and cooking") solutions are similarly underdeveloped, leaving over 900 million people reliant on polluting fuels such as firewood, charcoal, and agricultural residues, which disproportionately affect women and children. Transport energy is dominated by oil products, reflecting low motorisation rates and limited adoption of electric mobility, although countries such as Ethiopia, Morocco, and Egypt have begun adopting policies to reduce fossil fuel dependency.

Africa possesses some of the world's most abundant energy resources, including [oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil "Oil"), [natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas "Natural gas"), [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal "Coal"), [hydropower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydropower "Hydropower"), [solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power "Solar power"), [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power "Wind power"), [geothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_energy "Geothermal energy"), and [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass "Biomass"), much of which remains underutilised. While [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel "Fossil fuel") such as coal, oil, and natural gas continue to dominate energy production and electricity generation in Africa, [renewable sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_Africa "Renewable energy in Africa") are increasingly expanding across the continent. Hydropower, solar, wind, geothermal, and modern biomass are growing in capacity, though their deployment remains uneven and limited compared to the continent's vast potential. Hydropower is the largest non-combustible renewable source, while solar energy offers immense potential due to high irradiation levels, particularly in the [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara") and [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel").

There are regional differences: [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa") is dominated by oil and gas production, [Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa "Southern Africa") by coal and hydropower, [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa") by hydropower and geothermal, and [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa") and [Central Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Africa "Central Africa") face major electrification gaps despite hydroelectric potential.

### Infrastructure

#### Water resources

Water development and management are complex due to the multiplicity of trans-boundary water resources (rivers, lakes and aquifers). Around 75% of sub-Saharan Africa falls within 53 international river basin catchments that traverse multiple borders. This particular constraint can also be converted into an opportunity if the potential for trans-boundary cooperation is harnessed in the development of the area's water resources. A multi-sectoral analysis of the [Zambezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambezi "Zambezi") River, for example, shows that [riparian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riparian_zone "Riparian zone") cooperation could lead to a 23% increase in firm energy production without any additional investments. Several institutional and legal frameworks for transboundary cooperation exist, such as the Zambezi River Authority, the [Southern African Development Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_African_Development_Community "Southern African Development Community") protocol, [Volta River Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volta_River_Authority "Volta River Authority") and the Nile Basin Commission. However, additional efforts are required to further develop political will as well as the financial capacities and institutional frameworks needed for multilateral cooperative actions and optimal solutions for all riparians.

## Demographics

Africa is considered by anthropologists to be the most genetically diverse continent as a result of having been inhabited the longest. The population has rapidly increased over the last 40 years and is consequently relatively young. In some states, more than half the population is under 25 years of age. The population increased from 229 million in 1950 to 630 million in 1990. As of 2021, the population is estimated at 1,394 million. Africa's total population surpassing other continents is fairly recent; the population surpassed Europe in the 1990s, while the Americas were overtaken sometime around 2000. This increase in the number of babies born in Africa compared to the rest of the world is expected to reach approximately 37% in the year 2050, while in 1990 sub-Saharan Africa accounted for only 16% of the world's births.

The [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate "Total fertility rate") (children per woman) for sub-Saharan Africa is 4.7 as of 2018, the highest in the world. All countries in sub-Saharan Africa had rates above replacement level in 2019 and accounted for 27.1% of global live births. In 2021, sub-Saharan Africa accounted for 29% of global births.

Speakers of [Bantu languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages "Bantu languages") (part of the [Niger–Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages "Niger–Congo languages") family) are the majority in southern, central and southeast Africa. The Bantu-speaking peoples from the Sahel progressively expanded over most of sub-Saharan Africa. But there are also several [Nilotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilotic_languages "Nilotic languages") groups in South Sudan and East Africa, the mixed [Swahili people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_people "Swahili people") on the [Swahili coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_coast "Swahili coast"), and a few remaining indigenous [Khoisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan "Khoisan") and Pygmy peoples in Southern and Central Africa, respectively. Bantu speakers predominate in Gabon and Equatorial Guinea and are found in parts of southern Cameroon. In the [Kalahari Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalahari_Desert "Kalahari Desert"), the distinct people known as the Bushmen (also "[San](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_peoples "San peoples")", closely related to but distinct from [Khoekhoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoekhoe "Khoekhoe")) have long been present. The San are physically distinct from other Africans and are the indigenous people of southern Africa.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Pygmies are the pre-Bantu indigenous peoples of Central Africa.

The peoples of West Africa primarily speak Niger–Congo languages, belonging mostly to its non-Bantu branches, though some [Nilo-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan_languages "Nilo-Saharan languages") and [Afroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages "Afroasiatic languages")-speaking groups are also found. The Niger–Congo-speaking [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_language "Yoruba language"), [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_language "Igbo language"), [Fulani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulani "Fulani"), [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twi-Fante_language "Twi-Fante language"), and [Wolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolof_people "Wolof people") ethnic groups are the largest and most influential. In the central Sahara, [Mandinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandinka_people "Mandinka people") or [Mande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mande_languages "Mande languages") groups are most significant. [Chadic-speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chadic_languages "Chadic languages") groups, including the [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_language "Hausa language"), are found in more northerly parts of the region nearest to the Sahara. Nilo-Saharan communities such as the [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people "Songhai people"), [Kanuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanuri_people "Kanuri people"), and [Zarma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarma_people "Zarma people") are found in eastern West Africa.

The peoples of North Africa consist of three main indigenous groups: [Berbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers "Berbers") in the northwest, [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians "Egyptians") in the northeast, and Nilo-Saharan-speaking peoples in the east. The Arabs who arrived in the 7th century introduced the Arabic language and Islam to North Africa. The Semitic [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") (who founded [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage "Carthage")) and [Hyksos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyksos "Hyksos"), the Indo-Iranian [Alans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alans "Alans"), the Indo-European [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"), Romans, and [Vandals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandals "Vandals") settled in North Africa as well. Significant Berber communities remain within Morocco and Algeria in the 21st century, while Berber speakers are also present in some regions of Tunisia and Libya. The Berber-speaking [Tuareg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg "Tuareg") and other often nomadic peoples are the principal inhabitants of the Saharan interior of North Africa. In Mauritania, there is a small, nearly extinct Berber community in the north and Niger–Congo-speaking peoples in the south, though in both regions Arabic and Arab culture predominate. In Sudan, although Arabic and Arab culture predominate, it is mostly inhabited by groups that originally spoke Nilo-Saharan, such as the [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubians "Nubians"), [Fur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_people "Fur people"), [Masalit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masalit_people "Masalit people") and [Zaghawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaghawa_people "Zaghawa people"), who, over the centuries, have variously intermixed with migrants from the Arabian Peninsula. Small communities of Afroasiatic-speaking [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_people "Beja people") nomads are found in Egypt and Sudan.

In the Horn of Africa, some [Ethiopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopians "Ethiopians") and [Eritrean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritreans "Eritreans") groups (like the [Amhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amhara_people "Amhara people") and [Tigrayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigrayans "Tigrayans"), collectively known as [Habesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habesha_peoples "Habesha peoples")) speak languages from the [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages "Semitic languages") branch of the Afroasiatic language family, while the [Oromo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_people "Oromo people") and [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalis "Somalis") speak languages from the [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages "Cushitic languages") branch of Afroasiatic.

Prior to the decolonisation movements of the post-World War II era, Europeans were represented in every part of Africa. Decolonisation during the 1960s and 1970s often resulted in the mass emigration of white settlers—especially from Algeria and Morocco (1.6 million *[pieds-noirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieds-noir "Pieds-noir")* in North Africa), Kenya, Congo, Rhodesia, Mozambique and Angola. Between 1975 and 1977, over a million colonials returned to Portugal alone. Nevertheless, [white Africans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Africans_of_European_ancestry "White Africans of European ancestry") remain an important minority in many African states, particularly Zimbabwe, Namibia, Réunion, and South Africa. The country with the largest white African population is South Africa. [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_diaspora "Dutch diaspora") and [British diasporas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_diaspora_in_Africa "British diaspora in Africa") represent the largest communities of European ancestry on the continent.

European colonisation brought [sizable groups of Asians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_diaspora "Asian diaspora"), particularly [South Asians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_diaspora "South Asian diaspora"), to British colonies. Large [Indian communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_diaspora "Indian diaspora") are found in South Africa, and smaller ones are present in Kenya, Tanzania, and some other southern and southeast African countries. The large [Indian community in Uganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Uganda "Indians in Uganda") was [expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_Asians_from_Uganda "Expulsion of Asians from Uganda") by [Idi Amin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idi_Amin "Idi Amin") in 1972, though many have since returned. The islands in the Indian Ocean are populated primarily by people of Asian origin, often mixed with Africans and Europeans. The [Malagasy peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malagasy_peoples "Malagasy peoples") of Madagascar are [Austronesian peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_peoples "Austronesian peoples"), but those along the coast are generally mixed with Bantu, Arab, Indian and European origins. Malay and Indian ancestries are components in [Cape Coloureds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Coloureds "Cape Coloureds") (people with origins in two or more races and continents). During the 20th century, small but economically important communities of [Lebanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Lebanon#Diaspora "Demographics of Lebanon") have developed in West and East Africa.

### Genetic history

Africa has the greatest human genetic diversity of any continent. Ancient DNA and genomic studies reveal deep population structure, with substantial differentiation among ancestral populations in southern, eastern, central, western, and northern Africa, alongside repeated [migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-modern_human_migration "Pre-modern human migration") and admixture. Some genetic models also infer [admixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interbreeding_between_archaic_and_modern_humans "Interbreeding between archaic and modern humans") with deeply divergent, now-extinct ["ghost" populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_population "Ghost population") of [archaic humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo "Homo") in Africa, whose fossil or genomic identity remains unknown. Later demographic expansions, including the [spread of Bantu-speaking populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion "Bantu expansion"), substantially reshaped this genetic landscape. Ancient genomes also show distinct demographic histories in North Africa, including long-term regional continuity and later gene flow from Eurasia and elsewhere in Africa.

The [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara") served as a trans-regional passageway and place of dwelling for people in Africa during various [humid phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstadial "Interstadial") and periods throughout the [history of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Africa "History of Africa"). Early Holocene genetic evidence indicates contacts between populations of the Lake Chad Basin and North Africa. The mitochondrial DNA haplogroup [L3e5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L3_(mtDNA) "Haplogroup L3 (mtDNA)"), which probably originated in the Lake Chad Basin around 10,000 years ago, occurs both there and in parts of North Africa, suggesting gene flow across the Sahara during a period of increased humidity.

Ancient DNA provides evidence for substantial genetic continuity in North Africa alongside contacts with populations from Western Asia. A whole genome from an individual buried at Nuwayrat in Middle Egypt around 2855–2570 BCE was modelled as predominantly deriving from North African Neolithic ancestry, with about 20% of his ancestry related to populations of the eastern [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent"), including Mesopotamia. Later ancient Egyptians also show increased ancestry related to the Levant, while present-day Egyptians have additional ancestry from sub-Saharan Africa and other regions. Ancient genomes from the [Green Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Sahara "Green Sahara") likewise indicate the existence of a deeply divergent North African lineage, closely related to ancestry found in 15,000-year-old [Taforalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taforalt "Taforalt") foragers in Morocco.

According to a recent study, the [Arab migrations to the Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_migrations_to_the_Maghreb "Arab migrations to the Maghreb") were mainly a demographic process that heavily implied gene flow and remodelled the genetic structure of the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb "Maghreb") region in North Africa, rather than a mere cultural replacement as claimed by older studies. Another study found that the majority of [J-M267](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_J-M267 "Haplogroup J-M267") (Eu10) chromosomes in the Maghreb are due to the recent gene flow caused by the Arab migrations to the Maghreb in the first millennium CE, as both southern [Qahtanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qahtanite "Qahtanite") and northern [Adnanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adnanites "Adnanites") Arabs added to the heterogeneous Maghrebi ethnic melting pot. The Eu10 chromosome pool in the Maghreb is derived not only from early [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") dispersions from Western Asia but, to a much greater extent, from recent expansions of [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs "Arabs") tribes from the [Arabian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula "Arabian Peninsula"). While acknowledging the genetic impact of Arabisation of Northwest Africa during the Islamic period, other authors have suggested that earlier migration processes – such as the arrival of [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution")-era famers from [Western Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia "Western Asia") and [Southern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Europe "Southern Europe") – together with [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") and [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age "Iron Age") input from [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") and the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") were ultimately more genetically impactful.

David Schoenbrun, Christopher Ehret, Steven A. Brandt and Shomarka Keita (2025) have highlighted the problematic categorisation of genetic haplogroups characterised as 'African' and '[Eurasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian "Eurasian")' in North African genome studies. In reference to the van de Loosdrecht et al. 2018 study on the epipalaeolithic Taforalt remains from [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco "Morocco"), which identified the [EM35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_E-M35 "Haplogroup E-M35") (primarily [EM78](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_E-V68 "Haplogroup E-V68")) common in north-eastern Africa but characterised the [mtDNA (female lineage haplogroups)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA "Mitochondrial DNA") of [U6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_U "Haplogroup U") and [M1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_M_(mtDNA) "Haplogroup M (mtDNA)") as 'Eurasian', the authors questioned the classification of these maternal haplogroups despite their localised and long-established presence in ancient African populations. In their view, identifying a range of African populations may remain an issue "since the idea of 'African' still gets stereotyped or restricted. (Accepting the [southwestern Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwestern_Asian "Southwestern Asian")/[Levantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") geographical continuity with Africa eliminates a conceptual barrier related to racio-typological thinking permitting an [Africasian construct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Asia "Afro-Asia") analogous to Eurasian.)".

### Religion

While Africans profess a wide variety of religious beliefs, the majority of the people respect African religions or parts of them. However, in formal surveys or censuses, most people will identify with major religions that came from outside the continent, mainly through colonisation. There are several reasons for this, the main one being the colonial idea that African religious beliefs and practices are not good enough. Religious beliefs and statistics on religious affiliation are difficult to come by since they are often a sensitive topic for governments with mixed religious populations. According to the *[World Book Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Book_Encyclopedia "World Book Encyclopedia")*, Islam and Christianity are the two largest religions. Islam is most prevalent in and is the state religion of many North African countries, such as Algeria, where 99% of the population practices Islam. The majority of people in Southern, Southeast, and Central Africa, as well as in a sizable portion of the Horn of Africa and West Africa, identify as Christians. [Coptic Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Christians "Coptic Christians") constitute a sizable minority in Egypt, and the [Ethiopian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church "Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church") is the largest church in Ethiopia, with 36 million and 51 million adherents. According to [Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica "Encyclopædia Britannica"), 45% of the population are Christians, 40% are Muslims, and 10% follow [traditional religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religions "Traditional African religions").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] A small number of Africans are Hindu, Buddhist, Confucianist, Baháʼí, or [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Africa "History of the Jews in Africa"). There is a minority of [irreligious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion_in_Africa "Irreligion in Africa") people.

### Languages

By most estimates, well over a thousand languages ([UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO") has estimated around two thousand) are spoken in Africa. Most are of African origin, though some are of European or Asian origin. Africa is the most multilingual continent in the world, and it is not rare for individuals to fluently speak multiple African languages as well as one or more European languages. There are four major groups indigenous to Africa:

* The [*Afroasiatic*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages "Afroasiatic languages") languages are a language family of about 240 languages and 285 million people widespread throughout the Horn of Africa, North Africa, the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel"), and Southwest Asia.
* The [*Nilo-Saharan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan_languages "Nilo-Saharan languages") languages consist of a group of several possibly related [families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family "Language family"), spoken by 30 million people across 100 languages. Nilo-Saharan languages are spoken by ethnic groups in Chad, Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, Sudan, South Sudan, Uganda, and northern Tanzania.
* The [*Niger-Congo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages "Niger–Congo languages") language family covers much of sub-Saharan Africa. In terms of number of languages, it is the largest language family in Africa and perhaps one of the largest in the world.
* The [*Khoisan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan_languages "Khoisan languages") languages form a group of three unrelated families and two [isolates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_isolate "Language isolate") and number about fifty in total. They are mainly spoken in Southern Africa by approximately 400,000 people. Many of the Khoisan languages are [endangered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_language "Endangered language"). The Khoi and San peoples are considered the original inhabitants of this part of Africa.

Following the end of colonialism, nearly all countries adopted official languages that originated outside the continent, although several countries also granted legal recognition to indigenous languages (such as [Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_language "Swahili language"), [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_language "Yoruba language"), [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_language "Igbo language") and [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_language "Hausa language")). In numerous countries, English and French (*see [African French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_French "African French")*) are used for communication in the public sphere such as government, commerce, education and the media. Arabic, [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language "Portuguese language"), [Afrikaans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrikaans "Afrikaans") and Spanish are examples of languages that trace their origin to outside of Africa, and that are used by millions of Africans today, both in the public and private spheres. Italian is spoken by some in former Italian colonies. German is spoken in Namibia, as it was a former German protectorate. At least one-fifth of Africans speak the former colonial languages. Moreover, in recent years some countries have been considering removing their official former colonial languages; examples include Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger, which removed French as an official language in the 2020s in favour of native languages, while also renaming colonial street names.

### Health

More than 85% of individuals use [traditional medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_traditional_medicine "African traditional medicine") as an alternative to often expensive allopathic medical health care and costly pharmaceutical products. The OAU declared the 2000s as the African Decade on African traditional medicine in an effort to promote the WHO African Region's adopted resolution for institutionalising traditional medicine in health care systems. Public policymakers in the region are challenged with considering the importance of traditional/indigenous health systems and whether their coexistence with the modern medical and health sub-sector would improve the equity and accessibility of health care distribution, the health status of populations, and the socio-economic development of nations within sub-Saharan Africa.

[AIDS in post-colonial Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Africa "HIV/AIDS in Africa") is a prevalent issue. Although the continent is home to about 15.2 percent of the world's population, more than two-thirds of the total infected worldwide—some 35 million people—were Africans, of whom 15 million have died. Sub-Saharan Africa alone accounted for an estimated 69 percent of all people living with HIV and 70 percent of all AIDS deaths in 2011. In sub-Saharan Africa, AIDS has raised death rates and lowered life expectancy among adults between ages 20 and 49 by about 20 years. Life expectancy has declined largely as a result of the HIV/AIDS epidemic, with life expectancy in some countries falling as low as 34 years.

## Culture

Africa is divided into a great number of ethnic cultures. Cultural regeneration has been an integral aspect of post-independence nation-building, with a recognition of the need to harness the cultural resources to enrich the process of education, requiring the creation of an enabling environment in several ways. In recent times, the call for a much greater emphasis on the cultural dimension in all aspects of development has become increasingly vocal. Some aspects of traditional cultures have become less practised in recent years as a result of neglect and suppression by colonial and post-colonial regimes. For example, African customs were discouraged, and African languages were prohibited in mission schools, while colonialists such as [Leopold II of Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_II_of_Belgium "Leopold II of Belgium") attempted to "[civilize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilizing_mission "Civilizing mission")" Africans by discouraging polygamy and [witchcraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft_in_Africa "Witchcraft in Africa").

Editors F. Abiola Irele and Simon Gikandi comment that the current identity of African literature had its genesis in the "traumatic encounter between Africa and Europe". On the other hand, Mhoze Chikowero believes that Africans deployed music, dance, spirituality, and other performative cultures to (re)assert themselves as active agents and indigenous intellectuals, to unmake their colonial marginalisation and reshape their own destinies.

As of March 2023, 98 African properties are listed by [UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO") as [World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Heritage_Site "World Heritage Site"), with 54 cultural sites, 39 natural sites, and 5 mixed sites. The [List of World Heritage in Danger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_in_Danger "List of World Heritage in Danger") includes 15 African sites.

### Visual art

[African art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_art "African art") refers to works of [visual art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_arts "Visual arts"), including works of sculpture, painting, [metalwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalworking "Metalworking"), and [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery "Pottery"), originating from the [various peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ethnic_groups_of_Africa "List of ethnic groups of Africa") of the African continent and influenced by distinct, indigenous traditions of [aesthetic expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics").

While the various artistic traditions of such a large and diverse continent display considerable regional and cultural variety, there are consistent artistic themes, recurring [motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(visual_arts) "Motif (visual arts)"), and unifying elements across the broad spectrum of African visual expression. As is the case for every [artistic tradition in human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_history "Art history"), African art was created within specific social, political, and religious contexts. Likewise, African art was often created not purely [for art's sake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_for_art%27s_sake "Art for art's sake"), but rather with some practical, [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality"), and/or [didactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didactic_art "Didactic art") purpose in mind. In general, African art prioritizes conceptual and symbolic representation over [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(arts) "Realism (arts)"), aiming to visualize the subject's spiritual essence.

[Ethiopian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_art "Ethiopian art"), heavily influenced by [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia")'s long-standing [Christian tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Ethiopia "Christianity in Ethiopia"), is also different from most African art, where [Traditional African religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religion "Traditional African religion") (with [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") prevalent in the northeast and northwest presently) was dominant until the 20th century. African art includes [prehistoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory "Prehistory") and [ancient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history "Ancient history") art, the Islamic art of [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"), the [Christian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_art "Christian art") of [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa"), and the traditional [artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_(archaeology) "Artifact (archaeology)") of these and other regions. Many [African sculptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_sculpture "African sculpture") were historically made of wood and other natural materials that have not survived from earlier than a few centuries ago, although rare older pottery and metal figures can be found in some areas. Some of the earliest decorative objects, such as [shell beads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_jewelry "Shell jewelry") and evidence of paint, have been discovered in Africa, dating to the [Middle Stone Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Stone_Age "Middle Stone Age").

[Masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_masks "Traditional African masks") are important elements in the art of many people, along with human figures, and are often highly stylized. There exist diverse styles, which can often be observed within a single [context of origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_of_origin "Country of origin") and may be influenced by the intended use of the object. Nevertheless, broad regional trends are discernible. Sculpture is most common among "groups of settled cultivators in the areas drained by the [Niger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger "Niger") and [Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_River "Congo River") rivers" in West Africa. Direct images of deities are relatively infrequent, but masks in particular are or were often made for ritual ceremonies. Since the late 19th century, there has been an increasing amount of [African art in Western collections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_art_in_Western_collections "African art in Western collections"), the finest pieces of which are displayed as part of the history of [colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonization "Colonization").

African art had an important influence on European [Modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") art, which was inspired by their interest in abstract depiction. It was this appreciation of African sculpture that has been attributed to the very concept of "African art", as seen by European and American artists and art historians.

Obidoh Freeborn posits that colonialism is one element that has created the character of modern African art. According to authors Douglas Fraser and Herbert M. Cole, "The precipitous alterations in the power structure wrought by colonialism were quickly followed by drastic iconographic changes in the art." Fraser and Cole assert that in Igboland some art objects "lack the vigor and careful craftsmanship of the earlier art objects that served traditional functions". Author Chika Okeke-Agulu states that "the racist infrastructure of British imperial enterprise forced upon the political and cultural guardians of empire a denial and suppression of an emergent sovereign Africa and modernist art".

### Architecture

Like other aspects of the [culture of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Africa "Culture of Africa"), the [architecture of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Africa "Architecture of Africa") is exceptionally diverse. Throughout the [history of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Africa "History of Africa"), [Africans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_people "African people") have developed their own local [architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture") traditions. In some cases, broader regional styles can be identified, such as the [Sudano-Sahelian architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudano-Sahelian_architecture "Sudano-Sahelian architecture") of [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"). A common theme in traditional African architecture is the use of [fractal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal "Fractal") scaling: small parts of the structure tend to look similar to larger parts, such as a circular village made of circular houses.

African architecture in some areas has been influenced by external cultures for centuries, according to available evidence. Western architecture has influenced coastal areas since the late 15th century and is now an important source of inspiration for many larger buildings, particularly in major cities.

African architecture uses a wide range of materials, including thatch, stick/wood, mud, [mudbrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudbrick "Mudbrick"), [rammed earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rammed_earth "Rammed earth"), and stone. These material preferences vary by region: North Africa for stone and rammed earth, the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa") for stone and mortar, West Africa for mud/adobe, Central Africa for thatch/wood and more perishable materials, Southeast and Southern Africa for stone and thatch/wood.

### Cinema

[Cinema of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Africa "Cinema of Africa") refers to the [film industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_industry "Film industry") and [films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film "Film") produced in the continent of Africa. It covers both the [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_film "History of film") and present of the [making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmmaking "Filmmaking") or screening of films on the African continent, and also refers to the persons involved in this form of [audiovisual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audiovisual "Audiovisual") culture. It dates back to the late 19th century, when film reels were the primary cinematic technology in use. [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo") has been the capital of the [film industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_industry "Film industry") in Africa from the early 20th century to the present day.

As there are more than 50 countries with audiovisual traditions, there is no single 'African cinema'. Both historically and culturally, there are major regional differences between [North African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa") and [sub-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa") cinemas, and between the cinemas of different countries. The [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Egypt "Cinema of Egypt") and the [Tunisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Tunisia "Cinema of Tunisia") film industries are also among the oldest in the world. The cinema of Egypt in particular is the most established and flourishing industry in Africa. Pioneers [Auguste and Louis Lumière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_and_Louis_Lumi%C3%A8re "Auguste and Louis Lumière") screened their films in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"), [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo"), [Tunis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunis "Tunis"), [Susa, Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa,_Libya "Susa, Libya") and [Hammam-Lif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam-Lif "Hammam-Lif"), [Tunisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunisia "Tunisia"), in 1896. [Albert Samama Chikly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Samama_Chikly "Albert Samama Chikly") is often cited as the first producer of indigenous African cinema, screening his own short documentaries in the [casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casino "Casino") of Tunis as early as December 1905.

### Music

The [music of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Africa "Music of Africa") is vast and highly diverse, with different [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_Africa "Regions of Africa") and [nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_African_countries "List of African countries") maintaining distinct [musical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music "Music") traditions. African music includes genres such as makwaya, [highlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highlife "Highlife"), [mbube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbube_(genre) "Mbube (genre)"), [township music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Township_music "Township music"), [jùjú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B9j%C3%BA_music "Jùjú music"), [fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuji_music "Fuji music"), [jaiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaiva "Jaiva"), [afrobeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrobeat "Afrobeat"), [afrofusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro_fusion "Afro fusion"), [mbalax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbalax "Mbalax"), [Congolese rumba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congolese_rumba "Congolese rumba"), [soukous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soukous "Soukous"), [ndombolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ndombolo "Ndombolo"), [makossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makossa "Makossa"), [kizomba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kizomba "Kizomba"), and [taarab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taarab "Taarab"), among others. African music also uses a wide variety of instruments from across the continent.

The music and dance traditions of the [African diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora "African diaspora"), shaped to varying degrees by African musical traditions, include [American genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_the_United_States "Music of the United States") such as [Dixieland jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland_jazz "Dixieland jazz"), [blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues "Blues"), and [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz"), as well as [Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_music "Caribbean music") styles such as [calypso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calypso_music "Calypso music") (see [kaiso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiso "Kaiso")) and [soca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soca_music "Soca music"). [Latin American music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_music "Latin American music") genres including [cumbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumbia "Cumbia"), [salsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salsa_music "Salsa music"), [son cubano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_cubano "Son cubano"), [rumba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_rumba "Cuban rumba"), [conga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conga_(music) "Conga (music)"), [bomba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomba_(Puerto_Rico) "Bomba (Puerto Rico)"), [samba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samba "Samba"), and [zouk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zouk_(musical_movement) "Zouk (musical movement)") developed from the music of [enslaved Africans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Atlantic_slave_trade "Trans-Atlantic slave trade") and have, in turn, influenced contemporary [African popular music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_popular_music "African popular music").

### Dance

[African dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_dance "African dance") (also Afro dance, Afrodance and Afro-dance) refers to the various dance styles of [sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa"). These dances are closely connected with the traditional [rhythms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa "Rhythm in Sub-Saharan Africa") and [music traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_African_music_traditions "Sub-Saharan African music traditions") of the region. Music and dancing are an integral part of many traditional African societies. Songs and dances facilitate teaching and promoting social values, the celebration of special events and major life milestones, the performance of [oral history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_history "Oral history") and other recitations, and spiritual experiences. African dance uses the concepts of [polyrhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyrhythm "Polyrhythm") and total body articulation. African dances are often a collective activity performed in large groups, with significant interaction between dancers and onlookers.

### Sports

Fifty-four African countries have football teams in the [Confederation of African Football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_African_Football "Confederation of African Football"). Egypt has won the [African Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_Cup_of_Nations "Africa Cup of Nations") seven times, and a record-making three times in a row. Cameroon, Nigeria, Morocco, Senegal, Ghana, and Algeria have advanced to the knockout stage of recent [FIFA World Cups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIFA_World_Cup "FIFA World Cup"). Morocco at the [2022 World Cup in Qatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_FIFA_World_Cup "2022 FIFA World Cup") was the first African nation to reach the semi-finals of the FIFA Men's World Cup. South Africa hosted the [2010 World Cup tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_FIFA_World_Cup "2010 FIFA World Cup"), becoming the first African country to do so. The top clubs in each football league play in the [CAF Champions League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAF_Champions_League "CAF Champions League"), while lower-ranked clubs compete in the [CAF Confederation Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAF_Confederation_Cup "CAF Confederation Cup").

In recent years, the continent has progressed in terms of state-of-the-art basketball facilities, which have been built in cities such as [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo"), [Dakar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakar "Dakar"), [Johannesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannesburg "Johannesburg"), [Kigali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali "Kigali"), [Luanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luanda "Luanda"), and [Rades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rades "Rades"). The number of African basketball players who were drafted into the U.S. [NBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBA "NBA") experienced growth in the 2010s.

Cricket is popular in some nations. [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa_national_cricket_team "South Africa national cricket team") and [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe_national_cricket_team "Zimbabwe national cricket team") have [Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_cricket "Test cricket") status, while [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya_national_cricket_team "Kenya national cricket team") is the leading non-Test team and previously had [One-Day International cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Day_International "One Day International") status (from [10 October 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President%27s_Cup_1997-98 "President's Cup 1997-98"), until [30 January 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Cricket_World_Cup_Qualifier#Super_Six "2014 Cricket World Cup Qualifier")). The three countries jointly hosted the [2003 Cricket World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Cricket_World_Cup "2003 Cricket World Cup"). [Namibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibia_national_cricket_team "Namibia national cricket team") has also played in a World Cup. Morocco hosted the [2002 Morocco Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Morocco_Cup "2002 Morocco Cup"), but [the national team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco_national_cricket_team "Morocco national cricket team") has never qualified for a major tournament.

Rugby is popular in several southern African nations. Namibia and Zimbabwe have appeared on multiple occasions at the [Rugby World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_World_Cup "Rugby World Cup"), while South Africa is the most successful national team at the Rugby World Cup, having won the tournament on four occasions, in 1995, 2007, 2019, and 2023.

[Traditional sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_traditional_games_in_Africa "List of traditional games in Africa") were strictly marginalised during the colonial era, and many are dying or have gone extinct under the pressure of modernisation; however, many remain popular despite not having formal governmental recognition or support. Some examples are [Senegalese wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegalese_wrestling "Senegalese wrestling"), [Dambe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dambe "Dambe"), [Nguni stick-fighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguni_stick-fighting "Nguni stick-fighting"), and [Savika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savika "Savika").
