# Jesus

**Jesus** (c. 6 to 4 BC – AD 30 or 33), also known as **Jesus Christ**, **Jesus of Nazareth**, and by various [other names and titles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_and_titles_of_Jesus_in_the_New_Testament "Names and titles of Jesus in the New Testament"), was a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") preacher and [religious leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_founders_of_religious_traditions "List of founders of religious traditions") active in the [Roman province of Judaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaea_(Roman_province) "Judaea (Roman province)") during the 1st century. He is the [central figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Christianity "Jesus in Christianity") of [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), the [world's largest religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups "Major religious groups"). Most [branches of Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denominations "Christian denominations") consider Jesus the [incarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_(Christianity) "Incarnation (Christianity)") of [God the Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Son "God the Son") and the awaited [Messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah#Christianity "Messiah") or [Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_(title) "Christ (title)"). Accounts of [Jesus's life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_of_Jesus "Life of Jesus") are contained in the [Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel "Gospel"), especially the [four canonical Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_canonical_Gospels "Four canonical Gospels") of the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament"). Since [the Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment"), [academic research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quest_for_the_historical_Jesus "Quest for the historical Jesus") has produced various views on the [historical reliability of the Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_reliability_of_the_Gospels "Historical reliability of the Gospels") and the extent to which they reflect the [historical Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Jesus "Historical Jesus"), but virtually all modern scholars of [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") agree that [Jesus existed historically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historicity_of_Jesus "Historicity of Jesus").

According to [Christian tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_tradition "Christian tradition"), as represented in the Gospels and the [Acts of the Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles "Acts of the Apostles"), Jesus [was circumcised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcision_of_Jesus "Circumcision of Jesus") at eight days old, [presented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presentation_of_Jesus "Presentation of Jesus") at the [Temple in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem "Temple in Jerusalem") at 40 days. He was [baptized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism_of_Jesus "Baptism of Jesus") by [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist "John the Baptist") as a young adult, and after [40 days and nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temptation_of_Christ "Temptation of Christ") of fasting in the [Judean wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaean_Desert "Judaean Desert"), he began [his public ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Jesus "Ministry of Jesus"). He was an [itinerant teacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itinerant_teacher "Itinerant teacher") whom his followers believed to possess divine authority in interpreting [Jewish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha "Halakha"). Jesus often debated with other Jews, most commonly the [Pharisees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharisees "Pharisees") and [Sadducees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadducees "Sadducees"), about how best to follow [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Judaism "God in Judaism"); engaged in healings; taught in [parables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parables_of_Jesus "Parables of Jesus"); and gathered followers, 12 of whom he appointed as his [apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostles_in_the_New_Testament "Apostles in the New Testament").

According to the New Testament accounts, Jesus was arrested in [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") and [tried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanhedrin_trial_of_Jesus "Sanhedrin trial of Jesus") by the [Sanhedrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanhedrin "Sanhedrin"); he was then handed over to the Roman authorities, and [crucified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus "Crucifixion of Jesus") on the order of [Pontius Pilate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontius_Pilate "Pontius Pilate"), the Roman prefect of [Judaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaea_(Roman_province) "Judaea (Roman province)"). In [Christian theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theology "Christian theology"), the [Resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus "Resurrection of Jesus") is the event in which Jesus rose from the [dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead "Dead"), on the third day after his death, and this belief became the foundation of the [early Christian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christianity "Early Christianity"), which expanded into a [worldwide movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Christianity "Spread of Christianity").

Christian theology includes the beliefs that Jesus was conceived by the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_in_Christianity "Holy Spirit in Christianity"), was [born of a virgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_birth_of_Jesus "Virgin birth of Jesus") named [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus "Mary, mother of Jesus"), performed [miracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracles_of_Jesus "Miracles of Jesus"), founded the [Christian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church "Christian Church"), died by crucifixion as a sacrifice for [atonement for sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonement_in_Christianity "Atonement in Christianity"), was resurrected by God the Father, and [ascended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_of_Jesus "Ascension of Jesus") into [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven_in_Christianity "Heaven in Christianity"), from where he will return. Christians commonly believe that Jesus enables people to be [reconciled to God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity "God in Christianity"). The [Nicene Creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_Creed "Nicene Creed") asserts that Jesus will [judge the living and the dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Judgment "Last Judgment"), either [before](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate_state_(Christianity) "Intermediate state (Christianity)") or [after](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mortalism "Christian mortalism") their [bodily resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_the_dead#Christianity "Resurrection of the dead"), an event associated with the [Second Coming of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming "Second Coming") in [Christian eschatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_eschatology "Christian eschatology"). Many branches of Christianity consider Jesus as the incarnation of God the Son, the second of the three [persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosopon "Prosopon") of the [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity "Trinity").

The [birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_Jesus "Birth of Jesus") in the [Judean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea "Judea") town of [Bethlehem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem "Bethlehem") is celebrated annually, generally on 25 December, as [Christmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas "Christmas"). His [crucifixion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion "Crucifixion") is commemorated on [Good Friday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday "Good Friday") and his resurrection on [Easter Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Sunday "Easter Sunday"). The world's most widely used [calendar era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_era "Calendar era")—in which the current year is [AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anno_Domini "Anno Domini") 2026 (or 2026 [CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era "Common Era"))—is traditionally based on the approximate [date of the birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_of_the_birth_of_Jesus "Date of the birth of Jesus").

Mainstream Judaism [rejects the belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_views_on_Jesus "Jewish views on Jesus") that Jesus was the awaited messiah, holding that he did not fulfil [messianic prophecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah_in_Judaism "Messiah in Judaism"), was not lawfully [anointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anointed "Anointed"), and was neither divine nor resurrected. In contrast, [Jesus in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Islam "Jesus in Islam") is considered the [messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah#Islam "Messiah") and a [prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_and_messengers_in_Islam "Prophets and messengers in Islam") of [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam"), who was sent to the [Israelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelites "Israelites") and [will return to Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming#Islam "Second Coming") before the [Day of Judgement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgement_Day_in_Islam "Judgement Day in Islam"). [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims") believe that Jesus was born of the virgin [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_in_Islam "Mary in Islam") but was neither God nor the son of God. Most Muslims [do not believe that he was killed or crucified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_views_on_Jesus%27s_death "Islamic views on Jesus's death"), but that God [raised him into Heaven while he was still alive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entering_heaven_alive "Entering heaven alive"). Jesus is also revered in the [Baháʼí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith") and [Druze faiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_faith "Druze faith"), as well as in [Rastafari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rastafari "Rastafari").

## Name

A Jewish person in Jesus's time usually [had only one name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_name "Jewish name"), sometimes followed by a [patronymic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patronymic "Patronymic") phrase of the form "son of [father's name]", or by the person's home town. Thus, in the New Testament, Jesus is commonly referred to as "Jesus of [Nazareth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazareth "Nazareth")". Jesus's neighbours in Nazareth referred to him as "the carpenter, the son of [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus "Mary, mother of Jesus") and brother of [James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James,_brother_of_Jesus "James, brother of Jesus") and [Joses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joses,_brother_of_James_the_younger "Joses, brother of James the younger") and [Judas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jude,_brother_of_Jesus "Jude, brother of Jesus") and [Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon,_brother_of_Jesus "Simon, brother of Jesus")", "the carpenter's son", or "[Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Joseph "Saint Joseph")'s son"; in the [Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John "Gospel of John"), the disciple [Philip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_the_Apostle "Philip the Apostle") refers to him as "Jesus son of Joseph from Nazareth".

The name *Jesus* is the English transliteration, through Latin *Iesus*, of [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): Ἰησοῦς, which is the Greek rendering of the [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew "Hebrew") יְהוֹשֻׁעַ‎ *Yehoshua* (Yehōšuᵃʿ). The Hebrew/[Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic "Aramaic") name was common among [Judean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea "Judea") Jews at the time of Jesus's birth, although by that period it had been shortened to יֵשׁוּעַ *[Yeshua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshua "Yeshua")* (Yešūᵃʿ); the contraction had already occurred in later biblical books such as [Nehemiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Nehemiah "Book of Nehemiah"), where [Joshua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua "Joshua") is referred to as *Yeshua*.

The name means "[God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragrammaton "Tetragrammaton") saves" in Hebrew, literally "[Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh "Yahweh") saves", from the [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_root "Hebrew root") ישׁע (*y-š-ʿ,* 'to save') and the noun יְשׁוּעָה (*yeshuah*, 'salvation'). The [Gospel of Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew "Gospel of Matthew") asserts the etymological significance of Jesus's name explicitly in the prophecy of the angel to Joseph about his birth: "you will call his name Jesus (Ἰησοῦς), for he will save (σώσει) his people from their sins".

The fact that [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses")' successor [Joshua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua "Joshua") bears the same name as Jesus in the original Greek, Hebrew, and Aramaic has been given theological significance by commentators, as a parallel is often drawn between the two leaders and the etymology of their shared name ('to save'): Joshua leads the Jews into the [Promised Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promised_Land "Promised Land"), while in Christianity Jesus is understood to save both Jews and [Gentiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentile "Gentile") from their sins.

### Jesus Christ

Since the 1st century, Christians have commonly referred to Jesus as "Jesus Christ". The word [*Christ*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_(title) "Christ (title)") is not a given name but was originally a [title or office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threefold_office "Threefold office") ("the Christ"), meaning "The [Messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah "Messiah")". The term derives from the Greek [Χριστός](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%A7%CF%81%CE%B9%CF%83%CF%84%CF%8C%CF%82 "wikt:Χριστός") (*Christos*), a [calque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calque "Calque") of the Hebrew word [משיח](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D7%9E%D6%B8%D7%A9%D6%B4%D7%81%D7%99%D7%97%D6%B7 "wikt:מָשִׁיחַ") *[mashiakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah_in_Judaism "Messiah in Judaism")* (Mašiᵃḥ), transliterated into English as *messiah*. The Hebrew term means "[anointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anointed "Anointed")", from the verb מָשַׁח (*mashaḥ*), "to rub with oil, to anoint". In biblical Judaism, [sacred oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_anointing_oil "Holy anointing oil") was used to anoint certain exceptionally holy people and objects as part of their religious investiture. In the [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint "Septuagint"), the Hebrew word was rendered into Greek as χριστός (*christos*), meaning "anointed", from the verb χρίω (*chrio*), "to rub with oil, to anoint".

Early Christians designated Jesus as "the Christ" because they believed him to be the Messiah (the "anointed one") whose arrival was [prophesied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_and_messianic_prophecy "Jesus and messianic prophecy") in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") (Old Testament). In post-biblical usage, *Christ* came to be viewed as a name—one part of "Jesus Christ". The term *[Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian "Christian")*, meaning a follower of Christ, has been in use since the 1st century.

## Life and teachings in the New Testament

### Canonical gospels

The four [canonical gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_gospel "Canonical gospel") ([Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew "Gospel of Matthew"), [Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Mark "Gospel of Mark"), [Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Luke "Gospel of Luke"), and [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John "Gospel of John")) are the foremost sources for the life and message of Jesus. Other parts of the New Testament also include references to key episodes in his life, such as the [Last Supper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Supper "Last Supper") in [1 Corinthians 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Corinthians_11 "1 Corinthians 11"):23–26. [Acts of the Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles "Acts of the Apostles") refers to Jesus's early ministry and its anticipation by [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist "John the Baptist"). Acts 1:1–11 provides more detail about the [Ascension of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_of_Jesus "Ascension of Jesus") than the canonical gospels do. In the [undisputed Pauline letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_epistles#Authenticity "Pauline epistles"), which were written earlier than the gospels, Jesus's words or instructions are cited several times.

Some [early Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christian "Early Christian") groups had separate descriptions of Jesus's life and teachings that are not included in the New Testament. These include the [Gospel of Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Thomas "Gospel of Thomas"), [Gospel of Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Peter "Gospel of Peter"), [Gospel of Judas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Judas "Gospel of Judas"), the [Apocryphon of James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocryphon_of_James "Apocryphon of James"), and [many other apocryphal writings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament_apocrypha "New Testament apocrypha"). Most scholars conclude that these texts were written later and are less historically reliable than the canonical gospels.

#### Authorship, date, and reliability

The canonical gospels are four accounts, each attributed to a different author. The authors of the gospels are generally regarded as pseudonymous and are attributed by tradition to the [four evangelists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_evangelists "Four evangelists"), each associated with Jesus or his close followers: Mark by [John Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mark "John Mark"), an associate of [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter "Saint Peter"); [Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_the_Apostle "Matthew the Apostle") to one of Jesus's [disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciple_(Christianity) "Disciple (Christianity)"); [Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_the_Evangelist "Luke the Evangelist") to a companion of [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle "Paul the Apostle") mentioned in a few epistles; and John to another of Jesus's disciples, the "[beloved disciple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beloved_disciple "Beloved disciple")".

According to [Marcan priority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcan_priority "Marcan priority"), the first to be written was the [Gospel of Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Mark "Gospel of Mark") (AD 60–75), followed by the Gospel of Matthew (AD 65–85), the Gospel of Luke (AD 65–95), and the Gospel of John (AD 75–100). Most scholars agree that the authors of Matthew and Luke used Mark as a source for their gospels. Since Matthew and Luke also share some content not found in Mark, many scholars infer that they used a hypothetical [Q source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q_source "Q source") in addition to Mark, while a growing number support the [Farrer hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farrer_hypothesis "Farrer hypothesis") or [Matthean Posteriority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthean_Posteriority_hypothesis "Matthean Posteriority hypothesis"), in which Matthew and Luke used each other directly. Luke and Matthew treat their sources more conservatively than other ancient historians like [Diodorus Siculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus "Diodorus Siculus"), though the parallels and variations of the Synoptic gospels are typical of ancient historical biographies.

One important aspect of the study of the gospels is the [literary genre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_genre "Literary genre") under which they fall. Whether the gospel authors set out to write novels, myths, histories, or biographies has a significant impact on how their works ought to be interpreted. Although not without critics, the view that the gospels are a type of ancient biography represents the consensus among scholars today.

Concerning the accuracy of the accounts, viewpoints range from considering them [inerrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inerrant "Inerrant") descriptions of Jesus's life, to doubting their historical reliability on various points, to regarding them as providing very little historical information about his life beyond the basics.

#### Comparative structure and content

Matthew, Mark, and Luke are known as the Synoptic Gospels, from the Greek σύν (*syn*, 'together') and ὄψις (*opsis*, 'view'), because they are similar in content, narrative arrangement, language, and paragraph structure, and can readily be set side by side for synoptic comparison. Scholars generally agree that it is impossible to find any direct literary relationship between the Synoptic Gospels and the Gospel of John. Many events—such as Jesus's baptism, crucifixion, and interactions with his [apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostles_in_the_New_Testament "Apostles in the New Testament")—appear in the Synoptic Gospels, but incidents such as the [transfiguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfiguration_of_Jesus "Transfiguration of Jesus") and Jesus's [exorcising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism "Exorcism") demons do not appear in John, which also differs on other matters, such as the [cleansing of the Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleansing_of_the_Temple "Cleansing of the Temple").

The Synoptics emphasize different aspects of Jesus. In Mark, Jesus is the [Son of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_God_(Christianity) "Son of God (Christianity)") whose mighty works demonstrate the presence of [God's Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God%27s_Kingdom "God's Kingdom"). He is portrayed as a tireless wonder worker and the servant of both God and humanity. This short gospel records relatively few of Jesus's words or extended teachings. The Gospel of Matthew emphasizes that Jesus is the fulfilment of God's will as revealed in the Old Testament and the Lord of the Church. He is presented as the "[Son of David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davidic_line "Davidic line")", a "king", and the Messiah. Luke presents Jesus as the divine-human saviour who shows compassion to the needy. He is depicted as the friend of sinners and outcasts, who came to seek and save the lost. This gospel includes well-known parables, such as the [Good Samaritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Samaritan "Good Samaritan") and the [Prodigal Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prodigal_Son "Prodigal Son").

The [prologue to the Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prologue_to_the_Gospel_of_John "Prologue to the Gospel of John") identifies Jesus as an incarnation of the divine Word ([Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_(Christianity) "Logos (Christianity)")). As the Word, Jesus is described as eternally present with God, active in all creation, and the source of humanity's moral and spiritual nature. In this gospel, Jesus is portrayed as not only greater than any past human prophet but greater than any prophet could be: he not only speaks God's Word; he is God's Word. In the Gospel of John, Jesus reveals his divine role publicly and is depicted as the [Bread of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_of_Life "Bread of Life"), the [Light of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_of_the_World "Light of the World"), the [True Vine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Vine "True Vine"), and more.

The authors of the New Testament generally showed little interest in establishing an absolute [chronology of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_Jesus "Chronology of Jesus")'s life or in synchronizing the episodes of his life with the secular history of the age. As stated in [John 21:25](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/John#21:25 "s:Bible (American Standard)/John"), the gospels do not claim to provide an exhaustive list of the events of Jesus's life. The accounts were primarily written as theological documents in the context of [early Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christianity "Early Christianity"), with timelines as a secondary consideration. The gospels devote about one third of their text to the last week of Jesus's life in [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem"), referred to as the [Passion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_(Christianity) "Passion (Christianity)"). They do not provide enough detail to satisfy the demands of modern historians regarding exact dates, but it is possible to draw from them a general picture of Jesus's life story.

### Genealogy and nativity

Jesus was Jewish, born to [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus "Mary, mother of Jesus"), the wife of [Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Joseph "Saint Joseph"). The Gospels of Matthew and Luke offer two different accounts of his [genealogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genealogy "Genealogy"). Matthew traces Jesus's ancestry to [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham "Abraham") through [David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David "David"), while Luke traces Jesus's ancestry through [Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam "Adam") to God. The lists are identical between Abraham and David but differ markedly from that point onward; Matthew has 27 generations from David to Joseph, whereas Luke has 42, with almost no overlap between the names on the two lists. Various theories have been put forward to explain why the two genealogies are so different.

Both Matthew and Luke describe Jesus's birth, particularly that he was born to a virgin named Mary in [Bethlehem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem "Bethlehem") in fulfilment of [prophecy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecy "Prophecy"). Luke's account emphasizes events before the [birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_Jesus "Birth of Jesus") and centres on Mary, while Matthew's mostly covers events after the birth and centres on Joseph. Both accounts state that Mary was engaged to a man named Joseph, who was descended from King David and was not Jesus's biological father, and both support the doctrine of the [virgin birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_birth_of_Jesus "Virgin birth of Jesus"), according to which Jesus was miraculously conceived by the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit "Holy Spirit") in Mary's womb when she was still a virgin. At the same time, there is evidence, at least in the Lukan *Acts of the Apostles*, that Jesus was thought to have had, like many figures in antiquity, a dual paternity, since there it is stated that he descended from the seed or loins of David. By [taking Jesus as his own son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_in_Judaism "Adoption in Judaism"), Joseph is understood to confer on him the necessary Davidic descent. Some scholars suggest that Jesus had [Levite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levite "Levite") heritage from Mary, based on her blood relationship with [Elizabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_(biblical_figure) "Elizabeth (biblical figure)").

In Matthew, Joseph is troubled because Mary, his betrothed, is [pregnant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregnancy "Pregnancy"), but in the first of [Joseph's four dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph%27s_dream "St. Joseph's dream") an angel assures him not to be afraid to take Mary as his wife because her child was conceived by the Holy Spirit. In [Matthew 2:1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_2:1 "Matthew 2:1")–[12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_2:12 "Matthew 2:12"), [wise men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Magi "Biblical Magi") or [Magi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magi "Magi") from the [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East "East") bring gifts to the young Jesus as the [King of the Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus,_King_of_the_Jews "Jesus, King of the Jews"). They find him in a house in Bethlehem. [Herod the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great "Herod the Great") hears of Jesus's birth and, wanting him killed, [orders the killings of male infants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_the_Innocents "Massacre of the Innocents") in Bethlehem and its surroundings. However, an angel warns Joseph in his second dream, and the family [flees to Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_to_Egypt "Flight to Egypt"), later returning and settling in Nazareth.

In Luke 1:31–38, Mary learns from the angel [Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel "Gabriel") that she will conceive and bear a child called Jesus through the action of the Holy Spirit. When Mary is due to give birth, she and Joseph travel from Nazareth to Joseph's ancestral home in Bethlehem to register in the census ordered by [Caesar Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar_Augustus "Caesar Augustus"). While there, Mary gives birth to Jesus, and, as they have found no room in the inn, she places the newborn in a [manger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manger "Manger"). An [angel announces the birth to a group of shepherds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annunciation_to_the_shepherds "Annunciation to the shepherds"), who go to Bethlehem to see Jesus and subsequently spread the news abroad. Luke 2:21 recounts how Joseph and Mary [have their baby circumcised on the eighth day after birth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcision_of_Jesus "Circumcision of Jesus") and name him Jesus, as Gabriel had commanded Mary. After the [presentation of Jesus at the Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presentation_of_Jesus_at_the_Temple "Presentation of Jesus at the Temple"), Joseph, Mary, and Jesus return to Nazareth.

### Early life, family, and profession

Jesus's childhood home is identified in the Gospels of Luke and Matthew as Nazareth, a town in [Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilee "Galilee") in present-day [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel"), where he lived with his family. Although Joseph appears in descriptions of Jesus's childhood, no mention is made of him thereafter. His other family members, including his mother Mary; [his four brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brothers_of_Jesus "Brothers of Jesus"), [James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James,_brother_of_Jesus "James, brother of Jesus"), [Joses (or Joseph)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joses,_brother_of_Jesus "Joses, brother of Jesus"), [Judas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jude,_brother_of_Jesus "Jude, brother of Jesus"), and [Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon,_brother_of_Jesus "Simon, brother of Jesus"); and his unnamed sisters, are mentioned in the Gospels and other sources. Jesus's maternal grandparents are named [Joachim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim "Joachim") and [Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anne "Saint Anne") in the [Gospel of James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_James "Gospel of James"). The Gospel of Luke records that Mary was a relative of Elizabeth, the mother of John the Baptist. Some extra-biblical contemporary sources consider Jesus and John the Baptist to be second cousins, based on the belief that Elizabeth was the daughter of [Sobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobe_(sister_of_Saint_Anne) "Sobe (sister of Saint Anne)"), the sister of Anne.

The Gospel of Mark reports that at the beginning of [his ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Jesus "Ministry of Jesus"), Jesus comes into conflict with his neighbours and family. Jesus's mother and brothers come to get him because people are saying that [he is out of his mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_health_of_Jesus "Mental health of Jesus"). Jesus responds that his followers are his true family. In the Gospel of John, Jesus and his mother attend a [wedding at Cana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding_at_Cana "Wedding at Cana"), where he performs his first miracle at her request. Later, she is present at his crucifixion, and he expresses concern for her well-being.

Jesus is called a τέκτων (*[tektōn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekt%C5%8Dn "Tektōn")*) in [Mark 6:3](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/Mark#6:3 "s:Bible (American Standard)/Mark"), a term traditionally understood as "carpenter" but which can also refer to makers of objects in various materials, including builders. Given the term's broad semantic range and "the socio-historical reality of a common Nazarene τέκτων", Matthew K. Robinson, a minister and academic, prefers to translate τέκτων as 'builder-craftsman'. The Gospels indicate that Jesus could read, paraphrase, and debate scripture, but this does not necessarily mean that he received formal scribal training.

The Gospel of Luke reports two journeys of Jesus and his parents in Jerusalem during his childhood. They come to the [Temple in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem "Temple in Jerusalem") for the [presentation of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presentation_of_Jesus "Presentation of Jesus") as a baby in accordance with Jewish law, where a man named [Simeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simeon_(Gospel_of_Luke) "Simeon (Gospel of Luke)") prophesies about Jesus and Mary. When Jesus, at the age of twelve, goes missing on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem for [Passover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passover "Passover"), his parents [find him in the Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finding_in_the_Temple "Finding in the Temple") sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking questions, and the people are amazed at his understanding and answers. Mary scolds Jesus for going missing, to which Jesus replies that he must "be in his Father's house".

### Baptism and temptation

The [synoptic gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_gospels "Synoptic gospels") describe [Jesus's baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism_of_Jesus "Baptism of Jesus") in the [Jordan River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_River "Jordan River") and [the temptations he faced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temptation_of_Christ "Temptation of Christ") while spending forty days in the [Judaean Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaean_Desert "Judaean Desert") as a preparation for his public ministry. In each of these accounts, the accounts of Jesus's baptism is preceded by information about John the Baptist. They portray John preaching repentance for the forgiveness of sins, encouraging the giving of [alms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alms "Alms") to the poor, baptizing people in the region of the Jordan River around [Perea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perea "Perea"), and foretelling the arrival of someone "more powerful" than he.

In the Gospel of Mark, John the Baptist baptizes Jesus, and as Jesus comes up out of the water he sees the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit "Holy Spirit") descending on him like a dove, and a voice comes from heaven and declares him to be God's Son. This is one of two events described in the Gospels where a voice from Heaven refers to Jesus as "Son", the other being the [Transfiguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfiguration_of_Jesus "Transfiguration of Jesus"). The Spirit then drives him into the wilderness, where he is tempted by [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan "Satan"). After John's arrest, Jesus begins his ministry in Galilee.

In the Gospel of Matthew, when Jesus comes to John to be baptized, John protests, saying, "I need to be baptized by you." Jesus instructs him to proceed with the baptism "to fulfil all righteousness". Matthew then narrates three specific temptations that Satan offers Jesus in the wilderness. In the Gospel of Luke, the Holy Spirit descends in bodily form like a dove after all the people have been baptized and while Jesus is [praying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayers_of_Jesus "Prayers of Jesus"). Later, John implicitly acknowledges Jesus by sending his followers to inquire about him. Luke also describes three temptations experienced by Jesus in the wilderness before he begins his ministry in Galilee.

The Gospel of John does not narrate Jesus's baptism and temptation. Instead, John the Baptist testifies that he saw the Spirit descend and remain on Jesus. John publicly proclaims Jesus as the [Lamb of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb_of_God "Lamb of God"), and some of John's followers become disciples of Jesus. Before John is imprisoned, Jesus leads his followers to baptize, and they baptize more people than John.

### Public ministry

The Synoptics depict two main geographical settings in Jesus's ministry. The first takes place in Galilee, north of [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea "Judea"), where Jesus conducts a largely successful ministry; the second occurs in Jerusalem, where he is rejected and killed. Often referred to as "[rabbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbi "Rabbi")", Jesus delivers his message orally. In these accounts, he forbids those who recognize him as the messiah—including people he heals and demons he is said to exorcise—to speak about it (see [Messianic Secret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messianic_Secret "Messianic Secret")). By contrast, the Gospel of John portrays Jesus's ministry as taking place primarily in and around Jerusalem rather than in Galilee, and his divine nature is more openly proclaimed and recognized.

Scholars commonly divide the ministry of Jesus into several stages. The Galilean ministry begins when Jesus returns to Galilee from the [Judaean Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaean_Desert "Judaean Desert") after resisting the temptations of [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan "Satan"). He then preaches throughout Galilee, and in [Matthew 4:18–20](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/Matthew#4:18 "s:Bible (American Standard)/Matthew"), his [first disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_disciples_of_Jesus "First disciples of Jesus")—who will later form the core of the early Church—encounter him and begin to follow him. This period includes the [Sermon on the Mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon_on_the_Mount "Sermon on the Mount"), one of Jesus's major discourses, as well as the [calming of the storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calming_of_the_storm "Calming of the storm"), the [feeding of the 5,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feeding_of_the_5,000 "Feeding of the 5,000"), [walking on water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_walking_on_water "Jesus walking on water"), and various other miracles and [parables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parables_of_Jesus "Parables of Jesus"). It concludes with the [Confession of Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confession_of_Peter "Confession of Peter") and the Transfiguration.

As Jesus travels towards Jerusalem, during what is often called the Perean ministry, he returns to the region where he was baptized, roughly a third of the way down from the [Sea of Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Galilee "Sea of Galilee") along the Jordan River. The [final phase of his ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Jesus#Final_ministry_in_Jerusalem "Ministry of Jesus"), In Jerusalem, begins with his [triumphal entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_entry_into_Jerusalem "Triumphal entry into Jerusalem") into the city on [Palm Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_Sunday "Palm Sunday"). In the Synoptic Gospels, during that week Jesus [drives the money changers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleansing_of_the_Temple "Cleansing of the Temple") from the [Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple "Second Temple"), and [Judas bargains to betray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bargain_of_Judas "Bargain of Judas") him. This period culminates in the [Last Supper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Supper "Last Supper") and, in the Johannine account, the [Farewell Discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farewell_Discourse "Farewell Discourse").

#### Disciples and followers

Near the beginning of his ministry, Jesus [appoints twelve apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioning_of_the_Twelve_Apostles "Commissioning of the Twelve Apostles"). In Matthew and Mark, Jesus calls his first four apostles, who are fishermen, and they are described as immediately leaving their nets to follow him. In John, Jesus's first two apostles are initially disciples of John the Baptist; the Baptist sees Jesus and calls him the Lamb of God, and the two, hearing this, begin to follow Jesus. In addition to the Twelve Apostles, the introduction of the [Sermon on the Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon_on_the_Plain "Sermon on the Plain") in Luke identifies a much larger group of people as disciples. In [Luke 10:1–16](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/Luke#10:1 "s:Bible (American Standard)/Luke"), Jesus sends [70 or 72 of his followers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventy_disciples "Seventy disciples") out in pairs to prepare towns for his prospective visits; they are instructed to accept hospitality, heal the sick, and proclaim the [Kingdom of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_God "Kingdom of God").

#### Teachings and miracles

In the Synoptics, Jesus teaches extensively—often in [parables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable "Parable")—about the Kingdom of God. Jesus also speaks of the "[Son of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_man_(Christianity) "Son of man (Christianity)")", an [apocalyptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypse "Apocalypse") figure who will come to gather the chosen. Jesus calls people to repent of their sins and to devote themselves wholly to God. He instructs his followers to observe [Jewish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_law "Jewish law"), although he is perceived by some contemporaries as having broken the law himself, for example in relation to [Sabbath observance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabbath "Sabbath"). When asked what the [greatest commandment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Commandment "Great Commandment") is, Jesus replies: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind ... And a second is like it: 'You shall love your neighbour as yourself.'" Other ethical teachings attributed to Jesus include [loving one's enemies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_5:44 "Matthew 5:44"), refraining from [hatred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatred "Hatred") and [lust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lust "Lust"), [turning the other cheek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turning_the_other_cheek "Turning the other cheek"), and [forgiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forgiveness "Forgiveness") those who have sinned against oneself.

The Gospel of John presents the teachings of Jesus not merely as his own preaching but as divine [revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation "Revelation"). John the Baptist, for example, states in [John 3:34](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/John#3:34 "s:Bible (American Standard)/John"): "He whom God has sent speaks the words of God, for he gives the Spirit without measure." In [John 7:16](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/John#7:16 "s:Bible (American Standard)/John"), Jesus says, "My teaching is not mine but his who sent me." He reiterates this in [John 14:10](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/John#14:10 "s:Bible (American Standard)/John"): "Do you not believe that I am in the Father and the Father is in me? The words that I say to you I do not speak on my own; but the Father who dwells in me does his works."

Approximately 30 parables constitute about one-third of Jesus's recorded teachings. The parables appear both within longer sermons and at various other places in the narrative. They often contain symbolism and typically relate aspects of the physical world to [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") realities. Common themes include the kindness and generosity of God, as well as the dangers and consequences of transgression. Some parables, such as that of the [Prodigal Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prodigal_Son "Prodigal Son"), are relatively straightforward, while others, such as the [Growing Seed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable_of_the_Growing_Seed "Parable of the Growing Seed"), are more complex, profound, and difficult to interpret. When his disciples ask why he speaks to the people in parables, Jesus replies that the chosen disciples have been granted "to know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven", unlike the rest, adding: "For the one who has will be given more and he will have in abundance. But the one who does not have will be deprived even more", and he goes on to say that most of their generation have developed "dull hearts" and are therefore unable to understand.

In the gospel accounts, Jesus devotes a substantial portion of his ministry to performing [miracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle "Miracle"), especially healings. These miracles are commonly classified into two main categories: healing miracles and nature miracles. The healing miracles include cures of physical ailments, [exorcisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism "Exorcism"), and [the raising of the dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracles_of_Jesus#Resurrection_of_the_dead "Miracles of Jesus"). The nature miracles demonstrate authority over the natural world and include [turning water into wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turning_water_into_wine "Turning water into wine"), walking on water, and calming a storm, among others. Jesus attributes his miracles to a divine source. When opponents accuse him of casting out demons by the power of [Beelzebub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beelzebub "Beelzebub"), the prince of demons, he replies that he does so by the "Spirit of God" ([Matthew 12:28](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/Matthew#12:28 "s:Bible (American Standard)/Matthew")) or "finger of God", arguing that it would be illogical for Satan to undermine his own domain; he also asks, if he exorcises by Beelzebub, "by whom do your sons cast them out?" In Matthew 12:31–32, he further states that while all kinds of sin, including "insults against God" or "insults against the Son of Man", may be forgiven, blasphemy against "The [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit "Holy Spirit")" will never be forgiven, and those guilty of it bear their sin permanently.

In John, Jesus's miracles are described as "signs", performed to manifest his mission and identity. In the Synoptic Gospels, when some teachers of the law and Pharisees ask him for a miraculous sign to validate his authority, Jesus refuses, saying that no sign will be given to a corrupt and evil generation except the sign of the prophet [Jonah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonah "Jonah"). In the Synoptics, the crowds typically respond to his miracles with awe and press upon him to heal their sick, whereas in John, Jesus is depicted as less constrained by the crowds, who often respond to his signs with belief and trust. A feature common to all the miracle narratives is that Jesus performs them freely and does not request or accept payment. The miracle stories are frequently interwoven with teachings, and the miracles themselves often carry a [didactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didactic_method "Didactic method") dimension. Many emphasize the importance of faith: in the [cleansing of ten lepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleansing_ten_lepers "Cleansing ten lepers") and the [raising of Jairus's daughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_of_Jairus%27s_daughter "Raising of Jairus's daughter"), for instance, the beneficiaries are told that their healing is due to their faith.

In *A Marginal Jew*, scholar [John P. Meier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_P._Meier "John P. Meier") argues that "the miracle traditions about Jesus' public ministry are already so widely attested in various sources" that any "total fabrication by the early church is, practically speaking, impossible". He bases this claim on literary sources such as the Gospels of Matthew, Luke, and John, as well as on the writings of the historian Josephus. Meier contends that the "criterion of multiple attestation of sources and forms" supports the conclusion that Jesus performed "extraordinary deeds" which his contemporaries regarded as miracles.

Scholar [Paul J. Achtemeier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_J._Achtemeier "Paul J. Achtemeier") argues that such miracles were not unique to Jesus in the ancient world and were perceived as ambiguous even by eyewitnesses. He notes that Jesus likely performed acts understood as exorcisms, which were "accepted as reality by his contemporaries", but that these should not be seen as having "probative value with respect to Jesus," since witnesses could claim that he was working with either Satan or God. Scholar [Gregory Sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Sterling "Gregory Sterling") observes that, in the case of Jesus's alleged exorcisms, "For first-century Galileans who believed in the personal presence of evil in the form of demons, Jesus' act was a validation of his ministry."

#### Proclamation, Transfiguration, and Passion Week

At approximately the midpoint of each of the three Synoptic Gospels, two significant events are narrated: the Confession of [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter "Saint Peter") and the Transfiguration of Jesus—not mentioned in the Gospel of John. In the Confession of Peter, Peter declares to Jesus, "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God"; Jesus affirms that this is a divinely revealed truth. Following this confession, Jesus begins to tell his disciples about his forthcoming suffering, death, and resurrection. In the Transfiguration, Jesus takes Peter and two other apostles up an unnamed mountain, where "he was transfigured before them, and his face shone like the sun, and his clothes became dazzling white". A bright cloud envelops them, and a voice from the cloud proclaims, "This is my Son, the Beloved; with him I am well pleased; listen to him."

The description of the final week of Jesus's life—often referred to as [Passion Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_Week "Passion Week")—occupies roughly one-third of the narrative in the canonical gospels. This section begins with Jesus's [triumphal entry into Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_entry_into_Jerusalem "Triumphal entry into Jerusalem") and concludes with his crucifixion.

#### Activities in Jerusalem

In the Synoptic Gospels, the final week in [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") concludes the journey through Perea and Judea that Jesus began in Galilee. Jesus enters Jerusalem riding a young donkey, evoking the motif of [the Messiah's donkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Messiah%27s_Donkey "The Messiah's Donkey") from the [Book of Zechariah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Zechariah "Book of Zechariah"), in the humble king of the Jews comes to the city in this manner. As he proceeds, people spread cloaks and small branches of trees ([palm fronds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_branch "Palm branch")) on the road before him and chant lines from Psalm 118:25–26.

Jesus next expels the money changers from the Temple, accusing them of turning it into a den of thieves through their commercial activities. Most scholars agree that it is overwhelmingly likely that Jesus did something in the temple and mentioned its destruction. In John, the Cleansing of the Temple occurs at the beginning of Jesus's ministry instead of at the end. Ancient compositional practices involved such chronological displacement and compression, with even reliable biographers like [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch "Plutarch") displaying them.

Jesus comes into conflict with the Jewish elders, such as when they [question his authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority_of_Jesus_questioned "Authority of Jesus questioned") and when he [criticizes them and calls them hypocrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woes_of_the_Pharisees "Woes of the Pharisees"). [Judas Iscariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judas_Iscariot "Judas Iscariot"), one of the [twelve apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_apostles "Twelve apostles"), secretly strikes a bargain with the Jewish elders, agreeing to betray Jesus to them for [30 silver coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30_silver_coins "30 silver coins").

The Gospel of John recounts two other feasts in which Jesus taught in Jerusalem before the Passion Week. In [Bethany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethany "Bethany"), a village near Jerusalem, Jesus [raises Lazarus from the dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_of_Lazarus "Raising of Lazarus"). This potent sign increases the tension with authorities, who conspire to kill him. [Mary of Bethany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_of_Bethany "Mary of Bethany") anoints Jesus's feet, foreshadowing his entombment. Jesus then makes his messianic entry into Jerusalem. The cheering crowds greeting Jesus as he enters Jerusalem add to the animosity between him and the establishment. In John, Jesus has already cleansed the Second Temple during an earlier Passover visit to Jerusalem. John next recounts Jesus's Last Supper with his disciples.

#### Last Supper

The Last Supper is the final meal that Jesus shared with his twelve apostles in Jerusalem before his crucifixion. The Last Supper is mentioned in all four canonical gospels; Paul's [First Epistle to the Corinthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Epistle_to_the_Corinthians "First Epistle to the Corinthians") also refers to it. During the meal, [Jesus predicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_predicts_his_betrayal "Jesus predicts his betrayal") that one of his apostles will betray him. Despite each Apostle's assertion that he would not betray him, Jesus reiterates that the betrayer would be one of those present. Matthew 26:23–25 and John 13:26–27 identify Judas as the traitor.

In the Synoptics, Jesus takes bread, breaks it, and gives it to the disciples, saying, "This is my body, which is given for you." He then has them all drink from a cup, saying, "This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood." The Christian [sacrament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament "Sacrament") or [ordinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinance_(Christian) "Ordinance (Christian)") of the [Eucharist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist "Eucharist") is based on these events. Although the Gospel of John does not include a description of the bread-and-wine ritual during the Last Supper, most scholars agree that John 6:22–59 (the [Bread of Life Discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_of_Life_Discourse "Bread of Life Discourse")) has a eucharistic character and resonates with the [institution narratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_the_Eucharist#Institution_narratives "Origin of the Eucharist") in the Synoptic Gospels and in the Pauline writings on the Last Supper.

In all four gospels, Jesus predicts that Peter will deny knowledge of him three times before the [cock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooster "Rooster") crows the next morning. In Luke and John, the prediction is made during the Supper. In Matthew and Mark, the prediction is made after the Supper; Jesus also predicts that all his disciples will desert him. The Gospel of John provides the only account of [Jesus washing his disciples' feet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maundy_(foot_washing) "Maundy (foot washing)") after the meal. John also includes a long sermon by Jesus, preparing his disciples (now without Judas) for his departure. Chapters 14–17 of the Gospel of John are known as the [Farewell Discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farewell_Discourse "Farewell Discourse") and are a significant source of [Christological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christological "Christological") content.

#### Agony in the Garden, betrayal, and arrest

In the Synoptics, Jesus and his disciples go to the garden [Gethsemane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gethsemane "Gethsemane"), where Jesus prays to be spared his coming ordeal. Then Judas comes with an armed mob, sent by the chief priests, [scribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribe#Judaism "Scribe") and elders. He [kisses Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiss_of_Judas "Kiss of Judas") to identify him to the crowd, which then [arrests Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrest_of_Jesus "Arrest of Jesus"). In an attempt to stop them, an unnamed disciple of Jesus uses a sword to cut off the ear [of a man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malchus "Malchus") in the crowd. After Jesus's arrest, his disciples go into hiding, and Peter, when questioned, thrice [denies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denial_of_Peter "Denial of Peter") knowing Jesus. After the third denial, Peter hears the cock crow and recalls Jesus's prediction about his denial. Peter then weeps bitterly.

In John 18:1–11, Jesus does not pray to be spared his crucifixion, as the gospel portrays him as scarcely touched by such human weakness. The people who arrest him are [Roman soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Roman_army "Imperial Roman army") and Temple guards. Instead of being betrayed by a kiss, Jesus proclaims his identity, and when he does, the soldiers and officers fall to the ground. The gospel identifies Peter as the disciple who used the sword, and Jesus rebukes him for it.

#### Trials by the Sanhedrin, Herod, and Pilate

After his arrest, Jesus is taken late at night to the private residence of the high priest, [Caiaphas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caiaphas "Caiaphas"), who had been installed by Pilate's predecessor, the Roman procurator [Valerius Gratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerius_Gratus "Valerius Gratus"). The [Sanhedrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanhedrin "Sanhedrin") was a Jewish judicial body. The gospel accounts differ on the [details of the trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanhedrin_trial_of_Jesus "Sanhedrin trial of Jesus"). In Matthew 26:57, Mark 14:53, and Luke 22:54, Jesus is taken to the house of the high priest, Caiaphas, where he is [mocked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mocking_of_Jesus "Mocking of Jesus") and beaten that night. Early the next morning, the chief priests and scribes lead Jesus away into their council. John 18:12–14 states that Jesus is first taken to [Annas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annas "Annas"), Caiaphas's father-in-law, and then to the high priest.

During the trials Jesus speaks very little, mounts no defence, and gives very infrequent and indirect answers to the priests' questions, prompting an officer to slap him. In Matthew 26:62, Jesus's unresponsiveness leads Caiaphas to ask him, "Have you no answer?". In Mark 14:61, the high priest then asks Jesus, "Are you the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed One?". Jesus replies, "[I am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_am_(biblical_term) "I am (biblical term)")", and then predicts the coming of the Son of Man. This provokes Caiaphas to tear his own robe in anger and to accuse Jesus of blasphemy. In Matthew and Luke, Jesus's answer is more ambiguous: in Matthew 26:64, he responds, "You have said so", and in Luke 22:70 he says, "You say that I am."

The Jewish elders take Jesus to [Pilate's Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilate%27s_Court "Pilate's Court") and ask the Roman governor, [Pontius Pilate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontius_Pilate "Pontius Pilate"), to judge and condemn Jesus for various allegations: subverting the nation, opposing the payment of tribute, claiming to be Christ, a king, and claiming to be the son of God. The use of the word "king" is central to the discussion between Jesus and Pilate. In John 18:36, Jesus states, "My kingdom is not from this world", but he does not unequivocally deny being the King of the Jews. In Luke 23:7–15, Pilate realizes that Jesus is a Galilean, and thus comes under the jurisdiction of [Herod Antipas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Antipas "Herod Antipas"), the [Tetrarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodian_Tetrarchy "Herodian Tetrarchy") of Galilee and Perea. Pilate sends Jesus to Herod to be tried, but Jesus says almost nothing in response to Herod's questions. Herod and his soldiers mock Jesus, put an expensive robe on him to make him look like a king, and return him to Pilate, who then calls together the Jewish elders and announces that he has "not found this man guilty".

Observing a Passover custom of the time, Pilate allows one prisoner chosen by the crowd to be released. He gives the people a choice between Jesus and a murderer called [Barabbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barabbas "Barabbas") ([בר-אבא](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D7%91%D7%A8-%D7%90%D7%91%D7%90 "wikt:בר-אבא") or *Bar-abbâ*, "son of the father", from the common given name *[Abba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abba_(given_name) "Abba (given name)")*: 'father'). Persuaded by the elders, the mob chooses to release Barabbas and crucify Jesus. Pilate writes a sign in Hebrew, Latin, and Greek that reads "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" (abbreviated as [INRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INRI "INRI") in depictions) to be affixed to Jesus's cross, then [scourges Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagellation_of_Christ "Flagellation of Christ") and sends him to be crucified. The soldiers place a [crown of thorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_thorns "Crown of thorns") on Jesus's head and ridicule him as the King of the Jews. They beat and taunt him before taking him to [Calvary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvary "Calvary"), also called Golgotha, for crucifixion.

#### Crucifixion and entombment

Jesus's crucifixion is described in all four canonical gospels. After the trials, Jesus is led to Calvary [carrying his cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Carrying_the_Cross "Christ Carrying the Cross"); the route traditionally thought to have been taken is known as the [Via Dolorosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Dolorosa "Via Dolorosa"). The three Synoptic Gospels indicate that [Simon of Cyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_of_Cyrene "Simon of Cyrene") assists him, having been compelled by the Romans to do so. In Luke 23:27–28, Jesus tells the women in the multitude of people following him not to weep for him but for themselves and their children. At Calvary, Jesus is offered a sponge soaked in a concoction usually offered as a [painkiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painkiller "Painkiller"). According to Matthew and Mark, he refuses it.

The soldiers then crucify Jesus and [cast lots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortes_(ancient_Rome) "Sortes (ancient Rome)") for his clothes. Above Jesus's head on the cross is Pilate's multilingual inscription, "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews." Soldiers and passersby [mock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mocking_of_Jesus "Mocking of Jesus") him about it. Two convicted thieves are crucified along with Jesus. In Matthew and Mark, both thieves mock Jesus. In Luke, [one of them](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impenitent_thief "Impenitent thief") rebukes Jesus, while [the other](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penitent_thief "Penitent thief") defends him. Jesus tells the latter: "today you will be with me in Paradise." The four gospels mention the presence of [a group of female disciples of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_at_the_crucifixion "Women at the crucifixion") at the crucifixion. In John, Jesus sees his mother Mary and the [beloved disciple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beloved_disciple "Beloved disciple") and tells him to take care of her.

In John 19:33–34, Roman soldiers break the two thieves' legs to hasten their death, but not those of Jesus, as he is already dead. Instead, [one soldier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longinus "Longinus") pierces Jesus's side with a [lance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Lance "Holy Lance"), and [blood and water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Holy_Wounds "Five Holy Wounds") flow out. The Synoptics report a [period of darkness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_darkness "Crucifixion darkness"), and the heavy curtain in the Temple is torn when Jesus dies. In Matthew 27:51–54, an earthquake breaks open tombs. In Matthew and Mark, terrified by the events, a Roman [centurion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centurion "Centurion") states that Jesus was the Son of God.

On the same day, [Joseph of Arimathea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea "Joseph of Arimathea"), with Pilate's permission and with [Nicodemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicodemus "Nicodemus")'s help, [removes Jesus's body from the cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_from_the_Cross "Descent from the Cross"), wraps it in a clean cloth, and buries it in a new [rock-hewn tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepulchre "Sepulchre"). In Matthew 27:62–66, on the following day the chief Jewish priests ask Pilate for the tomb to be secured, and with Pilate's permission the priests place seals on the large stone covering the entrance.

### Resurrection and ascension

The Gospels do not describe the moment of the [resurrection of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus "Resurrection of Jesus"). They describe the discovery of his [empty tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_tomb "Empty tomb") and several appearances of Jesus, with distinct differences in each narrative.

In the four Gospels, [Mary Magdalene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Magdalene "Mary Magdalene") goes to the tomb on Sunday morning, alone or with one or several [other women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrrhbearers "Myrrhbearers"). The tomb is empty, with the stone rolled away, and there are one or two angels, depending on the accounts. In the Synoptics, the women are told that Jesus is not here and that he is risen. In Mark and Matthew, the angel also instructs them to tell the disciples to meet Jesus in Galilee. In Luke, Peter visits the tomb after he is told it is empty. In John, he goes there with the beloved disciple. Matthew mentions Roman guards at the tomb, who report to the priests of Jerusalem what happened. The priests bribe them to say that the disciples [stole Jesus's body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stolen_body_hypothesis "Stolen body hypothesis") during the night.

The four Gospels then describe various appearances of Jesus in his resurrected body. Jesus first reveals himself to Mary Magdalene in Mark 16:9 and John 20:14–17, along with "the other Mary" in Matthew 28:9, while in Luke the first reported appearance is to [two disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_to_Emmaus_appearance "Road to Emmaus appearance") heading to [Emmaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmaus "Emmaus"). Jesus then reveals himself to the eleven disciples, in Jerusalem or in Galilee. In Luke 24:36–43, he eats and shows them his [tangible wounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Holy_Wounds "Five Holy Wounds") to prove that he is not a spirit. He also shows them to [Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle "Thomas the Apostle") to end [his doubts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubting_Thomas "Doubting Thomas"), in John 20:24–29. In the Synoptics, Jesus [commissions the disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Commission "Great Commission") to spread the gospel message to all nations, while in [John 21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_21 "John 21"), he [tells Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_of_Peter "Restoration of Peter") to take care of his sheep.

Jesus's [ascension into Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entering_Heaven_alive "Entering Heaven alive") is described in Luke 24:50–53, Acts 1:1–11, and mentioned in 1 Timothy 3:16. In the Acts of the Apostles, forty days after the Resurrection, as the disciples look on, "he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight". 1 Peter 3:22 states that Jesus has "gone into heaven and is at the right hand of God".

The Acts of the Apostles describes several appearances of Jesus after his Ascension. In Acts 7:55, [Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Stephen "Saint Stephen") gazes into heaven and sees "Jesus standing at the right hand of God" just before his death. On the road to [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus "Damascus"), the Apostle [Paul is converted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_Paul_the_Apostle "Conversion of Paul the Apostle") to Christianity after seeing a blinding light and hearing a voice saying, "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting." In Acts 9:10–18, Jesus instructs [Ananias of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananias_of_Damascus "Ananias of Damascus") in a vision to heal Paul. The [Book of Revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Revelation "Book of Revelation") includes a revelation from Jesus concerning the [last days of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_eschatology "Christian eschatology").

## Early Christianity

After Jesus's life, his followers, as described in the first chapters of the [Acts of the Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles "Acts of the Apostles"), were all Jews either by birth or [conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_to_Judaism "Conversion to Judaism"), for which the biblical term "[proselyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proselyte "Proselyte")" is used, and referred to by historians as [Jewish Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Christian "Jewish Christian"). The early Gospel message was spread [orally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_gospel_traditions "Oral gospel traditions"), probably in Aramaic, but almost immediately also in [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek "Koine Greek"). The [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament")'s Acts of the Apostles and [Epistle to the Galatians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistle_to_the_Galatians "Epistle to the Galatians") record that the first Christian community was [centred in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Christian_church "First Christian church") and its leaders included [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter "Saint Peter"), [James, the brother of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_(brother_of_Jesus) "James (brother of Jesus)"), and [John the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Apostle "John the Apostle").

After [his conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_Paul_the_Apostle "Conversion of Paul the Apostle"), [Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle "Paul the Apostle") spread the teachings of Jesus to various [non-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Jewish "Non-Jewish") communities throughout the eastern Mediterranean region. Paul's influence on Christian thinking is said to be more significant than that of any other [New Testament author](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorship_of_the_New_Testament "Authorship of the New Testament"). By the end of the [1st century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_1st_century "Christianity in the 1st century"), Christianity began to be recognized internally and externally as a [separate religion from Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_of_early_Christianity_and_Judaism "Split of early Christianity and Judaism") which itself was refined and developed further in the centuries after the [destruction of the Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(70_AD) "Siege of Jerusalem (70 AD)").

Numerous quotations in the New Testament and other Christian writings of the first centuries indicate that early Christians generally used and revered the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") (the [Tanakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh")) as [religious text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_text "Religious text"), mostly in the Greek ([Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint "Septuagint")) or Aramaic ([Targum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targum "Targum")) translations. Early Christians wrote many religious works, including the ones included in the [canon of the New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_of_the_New_Testament "Canon of the New Testament"). The canonical texts, which have become the main sources used by historians to try to understand the historical Jesus and sacred texts within Christianity, were probably written between AD 50 and 120.

## Historical views

Prior to [the Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment"), the Gospels were usually regarded as accurate historical accounts, but since then scholars have emerged who question the reliability of the Gospels and draw a distinction between the Jesus described in the Gospels and the Jesus of history. Since the 18th century, three separate scholarly quests for the historical Jesus have taken place, each with distinct characteristics and based on different research criteria, which were often developed during the quest that applied them. While there is widespread scholarly agreement on the existence of Jesus, and a basic consensus on the general outline of his life, the portraits of Jesus constructed by various scholars often differ from each other, and from the image portrayed in the gospel accounts.

Approaches to the historical reconstruction of the life of Jesus have varied from the "maximalist" approaches of the 19th century, in which the gospel accounts were accepted as reliable evidence wherever it is possible, to the "minimalist" approaches of the early 20th century, where hardly anything about Jesus was accepted as historical. In the 1950s, as the second quest for the historical Jesus gathered pace, the minimalist approaches faded away, and in the 21st century, minimalists such as [Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Price "Robert M. Price") are a small minority. Although a belief in the [inerrancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_inerrancy "Biblical inerrancy") of the Gospels cannot be supported historically, many scholars since the 1980s have held that, beyond the few facts considered to be historically certain, certain other elements of Jesus's life are "historically probable". Modern scholarly research on the historical Jesus thus focuses on identifying the most probable elements.

### Judea and Galilee in the 1st century

In AD 6, Judea, [Idumea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idumea "Idumea"), and [Samaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaria "Samaria") were transformed from a [client state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client_kingdoms_in_ancient_Rome "Client kingdoms in ancient Rome") of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") into an imperial province, also called [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea_(Roman_province) "Judea (Roman province)"). A Roman [prefect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefect "Prefect"), rather than a client ruler, governed the land. The prefect governed from [Caesarea Maritima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesarea_Maritima "Caesarea Maritima"), leaving Jerusalem to be run by the [High Priest of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_High_Priests_of_Israel "List of High Priests of Israel"). As an exception, the prefect came to Jerusalem during religious festivals, when religious and patriotic enthusiasm sometimes inspired unrest or uprisings. [Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilee "Galilee") with [Perea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perea "Perea") was a Herodian client state under the rule of [Herod Antipas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Antipas "Herod Antipas") since 4 BC. Galilee was evidently prosperous, and poverty was limited enough that it did not threaten the social order.
[Philip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_the_Tetrarch "Philip the Tetrarch") (d. AD 34), half-brother of Herod Antipas, ruled as [Tetrarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodian_tetrarchy "Herodian tetrarchy") yet another Herodian client state to the north and east of the sea of Galilee that included [Gaulanitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaulanitis "Gaulanitis"), [Batanea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batanea "Batanea"), and [Iturea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iturea "Iturea"); it was mostly non-Jewish. South of this on the east bank of the Jordan was the [Decapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decapolis "Decapolis"); a collection of Hellenistic city-states that were clients of the Roman Empire. North of Galilee were the cities of [Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyre,_Lebanon "Tyre, Lebanon") and [Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidon "Sidon") which were in the Roman province of [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Syria "Roman Syria"). Though non-Jewish lands surrounded the mostly Jewish territories of Judea and Galilee, Roman law and practice allowed Jews to remain separate legally and culturally.

This was the era of [Hellenistic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Judaism "Hellenistic Judaism"), which combined [Jewish religious tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism") with elements of [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic "Hellenistic") culture. Until the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Fall of the Western Roman Empire") and the [Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests") of the Eastern Mediterranean, the main centres of Hellenistic Judaism were [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria") (Egypt) and [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch "Antioch") (now Southern Turkey), the two main [Greek colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonies "Greek colonies") of the [Middle East and North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_and_North_Africa "Middle East and North Africa") area, both founded at the end of the 4th century BC in the wake of the conquests of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great"). Hellenistic Judaism also existed in [Jerusalem during the Second Temple Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_during_the_Second_Temple_Period "Jerusalem during the Second Temple Period"), where there was conflict between Hellenizers and traditionalists (sometimes called [Judaizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaizers "Judaizers")). The [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") was translated from [Biblical Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Hebrew "Biblical Hebrew") and [Biblical Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Aramaic "Biblical Aramaic") into [Jewish Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Koine_Greek "Jewish Koine Greek"); the [Targum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targum "Targum") translations into Aramaic were also generated during this era, both due to the decline of knowledge of Hebrew.

Jews based their faith and religious practice on the [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah "Torah"), five books said to have been given by God to [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses"). The three prominent religious parties were the [Pharisees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharisees "Pharisees"), the [Essenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essenes "Essenes"), and the [Sadducees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadducees "Sadducees"). Together these parties represented only a small fraction of the population. Most Jews looked forward to a time when God would deliver them from their pagan rulers, possibly through war against the Romans.

### Sources

New Testament scholars face a formidable challenge when they analyse the canonical Gospels. The Gospels are not biographies in the modern sense, and the authors explain Jesus's theological significance and recount his public ministry while omitting many details of his life. [James Dunn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Dunn_(theologian) "James Dunn (theologian)") has argued that the accounts of his teachings and life were [initially conserved by oral transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_gospel_traditions "Oral gospel traditions"), which was the source of the written Gospels. The Gospels are commonly seen as literature that is based on oral traditions, Christian preaching, and Old Testament exegesis with the consensus being that they are a variation of Greco-Roman biography; similar to other ancient works such as Xenophon's [*Memoirs of Socrates*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorabilia_(Xenophon) "Memorabilia (Xenophon)").

The reports of supernatural events associated with Jesus's death and resurrection make the challenge even more difficult. Scholars regard the Gospels as compromised sources of information because the writers were trying to glorify Jesus. Ed Sanders argues that surviving textual sources provide more reliable details for Jesus's thoughts than they do for the thoughts of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great"), owing to the texts discussing Jesus being authored closer in time to the events they relate to. Biographies written about Alexander the Great during his own lifetime (who lived 330 years earlier) have all been lost, but are known of through references in biographies written by later authors. Although the texts about Jesus contain ideas from both Jesus and his later followers, it is possible to distinguish which parts originate from Jesus's own view, and which were ideas from his later followers.

Scholars use several criteria, such as the [criterion of independent attestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_of_independent_attestation "Criterion of independent attestation"), the [criterion of coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_criticism#Coherence "Biblical criticism"), and the [criterion of discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_of_discontinuity "Criterion of discontinuity") to judge the historicity of events. The historicity of an event also depends on the reliability of the source; indeed, the Gospels are not independent nor consistent records of Jesus's life. The [Synoptics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_Gospels "Synoptic Gospels"), especially Mark, the earliest written gospel, have been considered the most reliable sources of information about Jesus for many decades. John, the latest written gospel, differs considerably from the Synoptic Gospels, and has been considered less reliable, although John's gospel is seen as having more reliability than previously thought or sometimes even more reliable than the synoptics since the [third quest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quest_for_the_historical_Jesus#Third_quest "Quest for the historical Jesus").

Some scholars (such as the [Jesus Seminar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Seminar "Jesus Seminar")) believe that the non-canonical [Gospel of Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Thomas "Gospel of Thomas") might be an independent witness to many of Jesus's parables and aphorisms. For example, Thomas confirms that Jesus blessed the poor and that this saying circulated independently before being combined with similar sayings in the [Q source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q_source "Q source"). The majority of scholars are sceptical about this text and believe it should be dated to the 2nd century AD. Other select non-canonical Christian texts may also have value for historical Jesus research.

Early non-Christian sources that attest to the historical existence of Jesus include the works of the historians [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus "Josephus") and [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus "Tacitus"). Josephus scholar [Louis Feldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Feldman "Louis Feldman") has stated that "few have doubted the genuineness" of Josephus's reference to Jesus in [book 20](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Antiquities_of_the_Jews/Book_XX#Chapter_9 "s:The Antiquities of the Jews/Book XX") of the *[Antiquities of the Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquities_of_the_Jews "Antiquities of the Jews")*, and it is disputed only by a small number of scholars. Tacitus referred to Christ and his execution by Pilate in [book 15](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Annals_(Tacitus)/Book_15#44 "s:The Annals (Tacitus)/Book 15") of his work *[Annals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_(Tacitus) "Annals (Tacitus)")*. Scholars generally consider Tacitus's reference to the execution of Jesus to be both authentic and of historical value as an independent Roman source.

Non-Christian sources are valuable as they show that even neutral or hostile parties never show any doubt that Jesus existed. They present a rough picture of Jesus that is compatible with that found in the Christian sources: that Jesus was a teacher, had a reputation as a miracle worker, had a brother James, and died a violent death.

Archaeology helps scholars better understand Jesus's social world. For example, it indicates that [Capernaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capernaum "Capernaum"), a city important in Jesus's ministry, was poor and small, without even a [forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_(Roman) "Forum (Roman)") or an [agora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agora "Agora"). This archaeological discovery resonates well with the scholarly view that Jesus advocated reciprocal sharing among the destitute in that area of Galilee.

### Chronology

Jesus was a Galilean Jew, born around the beginning of the 1st century, who died in AD 30 or 33 in [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea_(Roman_province) "Judea (Roman province)"). The general scholarly consensus is that Jesus was a contemporary of John the Baptist and was crucified as ordered by the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, who held office from AD 26 to 36.

The Gospels offer several indications concerning the year of Jesus's birth. Matthew 2:1 associates the birth of Jesus with the reign of [Herod the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great "Herod the Great"), who died around 4 BC, and Luke 1:5 mentions that Herod was on the throne shortly before the birth of Jesus, although this gospel also associates the birth with the [Census of Quirinius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_of_Quirinius "Census of Quirinius") which took place ten years later. Luke 3:23 states that Jesus was "about thirty years old" at the start of his [ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Jesus "Ministry of Jesus"), which according to Acts 10:37–38 was preceded by John the Baptist's ministry, which was recorded in Luke 3:1–2 to have begun in the 15th year of [Tiberius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius "Tiberius")'s reign (AD 28 or 29). By collating the gospel accounts with historical data and using various other methods, most scholars arrive at a [date of birth for Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_of_the_birth_of_Jesus "Date of the birth of Jesus") between 6 and 4 BC, but some propose estimates that include a wider range.

The date range for Jesus's ministry has been estimated using several different approaches. One of these applies the reference in Luke 3:1–2, Acts 10:37–38, and the dates of Tiberius's reign, which are well known, to give a date of around AD 28–29 for the start of Jesus's ministry. Another approach estimates a date around AD 27–29 by using the statement about the temple in John 2:13–20, which asserts that the temple in Jerusalem was in its 46th year of construction at the start of Jesus's ministry, together with Josephus's statement that the temple's reconstruction was started by Herod the Great in the 18th year of his reign. A further method uses the date of the [death of John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_John_the_Baptist "Death of John the Baptist") and the marriage of [Herod Antipas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Antipas "Herod Antipas") to [Herodias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodias "Herodias"), based on the writings of Josephus, and correlates it with Matthew 14:4 and Mark 6:18. Given that most scholars date the marriage of Herod and Herodias as AD 28–35, this yields a date about AD 28–29.

Various approaches have been used to estimate the year of the crucifixion of Jesus. Most scholars agree that he died in AD 30 or 33. The Gospels state that the event occurred during the prefecture of Pilate. The date for the [conversion of Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_Paul "Conversion of Paul") (estimated to be AD 33–36) acts as an upper bound for the date of Crucifixion. The dates for Paul's conversion and ministry can be determined by analysing the [Pauline epistles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_epistles "Pauline epistles") and the Acts of the Apostles. Astronomers have tried to estimate the precise date of the Crucifixion by analysing lunar motion and calculating historic dates of [Passover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passover "Passover"), a festival based on the lunisolar [Hebrew calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_calendar "Hebrew calendar"). The most widely accepted dates derived from this method are 7 April AD 30, and 3 April AD 33 (both [Julian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_calendar "Julian calendar")).

### Historicity of events

Nearly all historians (both modern and historical) agree that Jesus was a real person who historically existed. Scholars have reached a limited consensus on the basics of Jesus's life.

#### Family

Many scholars including biblical ones agree that Joseph, Jesus's father, died before Jesus began his ministry. Joseph is not mentioned in the Gospels during Jesus's ministry. Joseph's death would explain why in Mark 6:3, Jesus's neighbours refer to Jesus as the "son of Mary" (sons were usually identified by their fathers).

According to Theissen and Merz, it is common for extraordinary [charismatic leaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charismatic_leaders "Charismatic leaders"), such as Jesus, to come into conflict with their ordinary families. In Mark, Jesus's family comes to get him, fearing that he is mad (Mark 3:20–34), and this account is thought to be historical because early Christians would probably not have invented it. After Jesus's death, many members of his family joined the Christian movement. Jesus's brother [James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James,_brother_of_Jesus "James, brother of Jesus") became a leader of the Jerusalem Church.

[Géza Vermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9za_Vermes "Géza Vermes") says that the doctrine of the [virgin birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_birth_of_Jesus "Virgin birth of Jesus") arose from theological development rather than from historical events.
Other scholars take it as significant that the virgin birth is [attested](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_of_multiple_attestation "Criterion of multiple attestation") by two separate gospels, Matthew and Luke.

[E. P. Sanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._P._Sanders "E. P. Sanders") and Marcus Borg note that the [birth narratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_of_Jesus "Nativity of Jesus") in [Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew "Gospel of Matthew") and [Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Luke "Gospel of Luke") are ahistorical and the clearest cases of invention in the Gospel narratives of Jesus's life. [Dale Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Allison "Dale Allison") and [W. D. Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._D._Davies "W. D. Davies") argue that Matthew presents a unified and preexisting infancy narrative based on haggadic legends about [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses"), though they maintain that elements in the story such as the names of Mary and Joseph and Jesus being in Nazareth during [Herod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great "Herod the Great")'s reign are historical. Both accounts have Jesus born in [Bethlehem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem "Bethlehem"), in accordance with Jewish salvation history, and both have him growing up in Nazareth, but Sanders points out that the two report different explanations for how that happened. Luke's account of a worldwide census is not plausible, while Matthew's account is more plausible, but the story reads as though it was invented to identify Jesus as a new Moses, and the historian [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus "Josephus") reports Herod the Great's brutality without ever mentioning that [he massacred little boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_the_Innocents "Massacre of the Innocents"). Scholars including [Gerd Theissen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_Theissen "Gerd Theissen") and [Annette Merz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annette_Merz "Annette Merz") conclude that Jesus was born in [Nazareth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazareth "Nazareth"), and the Bethlehem birth narrative was developed to align Jesus's story with scripture foretelling that the messiah would be born in Bethlehem. The differences found in the gospel accounts are typical of ancient historical biographies. The contradictions were apparent to early Christians, with harmonizations present in the [infancy gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infancy_gospels "Infancy gospels") of [Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infancy_Gospel_of_Thomas "Infancy Gospel of Thomas") and the [Gospel of James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_James "Gospel of James"), which are dated to the 2nd century AD.

Conservative scholars argue that despite the uncertainty of the details, the gospel birth narratives trace back to historical, or at least much earlier pre-gospel traditions. For instance, according to [Ben Witherington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Witherington "Ben Witherington"):

> What we find in Matthew and Luke is not the story of ... a [god] descending to earth and, in the guise of a man, mating with a human woman, but rather the story of a miraculous conception without the aid of any man, divine or otherwise. As such, this story is without precedent either in Jewish or pagan literature.

Sanders says that the genealogies of Jesus are based not on historical information but on the author's desire to show that Jesus was the universal Jewish saviour. In any event, once the doctrine of the virgin birth of Jesus became established, that tradition superseded the earlier tradition that he was descended from [David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David "David") through Joseph. The Gospel of Luke reports that Jesus was a [blood relative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_relative "Blood relative") of John the Baptist, but scholars generally consider this connection to be invented.

#### Baptism

Most modern scholars consider Jesus's baptism to be a historical fact, along with his crucifixion. The theologian [James D. G. Dunn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._G._Dunn "James D. G. Dunn") states that they "command almost universal assent" and "rank so high on the 'almost impossible to doubt or deny' scale of historical facts" that they are often the starting points for the study of the historical Jesus. Scholars adduce the [criterion of embarrassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_of_embarrassment "Criterion of embarrassment"), saying that early Christians would not have invented a baptism that might imply that Jesus committed [sins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin "Sin") and wanted to [repent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repent "Repent"). According to Theissen and Merz, Jesus was inspired by John the Baptist and took over from him many elements of his teaching.

#### Ministry in Galilee

Most scholars hold that Jesus lived in Galilee and Judea and did not preach or study elsewhere. They agree that Jesus debated with Jewish authorities on the subject of God, performed some healings, taught in [parables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable "Parable") and gathered followers. Jesus's Jewish critics considered his ministry to be scandalous because he feasted with sinners, fraternized with women, and allowed his followers to pluck grain on the Sabbath. According to Sanders, it is not plausible that disagreements over how to interpret the Law of Moses and the Sabbath would have led Jewish authorities to want Jesus killed.

According to Ehrman, Jesus taught that a coming kingdom was everyone's proper focus, not anything in this life. He taught about the Jewish Law, seeking its true meaning, sometimes in opposition to traditions. Jesus put love at the centre of the Law, and following that Law was an apocalyptic necessity. His ethical teachings called for forgiveness, not judging others, loving enemies, and caring for the poor. Funk and Hoover note that typical of Jesus were [paradoxical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox "Paradox") or surprising turns of phrase, such as advising one, when struck on the cheek, [to offer the other cheek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turning_the_other_cheek "Turning the other cheek") to be struck as well.

The Gospels portray Jesus teaching in well-defined sessions, such as the Sermon on the Mount in the Gospel of Matthew or the parallel [Sermon on the Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon_on_the_Plain "Sermon on the Plain") in Luke. While these teaching sessions include authentic teachings of Jesus, Theissen and Merz contend that the scenes were invented by the evangelists to frame these teachings, originally recorded without context. Le Donne, however, rejects the [form-critical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_criticism "Form criticism") notion that smaller units of traditions held a defined stage of circulation before the gospels' composition. While Jesus's miracles fit within the social context of [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history "Ancient history"), he defined them differently. First, he attributed them to the faith of those healed. Second, he connected them to [end times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_times "End times") prophecy.

Jesus chose [twelve disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_disciples "Twelve disciples") (the "Twelve"). According to Bart Ehrman, Jesus's promise that the Twelve would rule is historical, because the Twelve included [Judas Iscariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judas_Iscariot "Judas Iscariot"). In Ehrman's view, no Christians would have invented a line from Jesus, promising rulership to the disciple who betrayed him.

In Mark, the disciples play hardly any role other than a negative one. While others sometimes respond to Jesus with complete faith, his disciples are puzzled and doubtful. They serve as a [foil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foil_(literature) "Foil (literature)") to Jesus and to other characters. The failings of the disciples are probably exaggerated in Mark, and the disciples make a better showing in Matthew and Luke. Recent studies tend to suggest that Mark is not as negative towards [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter "Saint Peter") as a previous generation of scholars thought.

Sanders says that Jesus's mission was not about [repentance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repentance "Repentance"), although he acknowledges that this opinion is unpopular. He argues that repentance appears as a strong theme only in Luke, that repentance was John the Baptist's message, and that Jesus's ministry would not have been scandalous if the sinners he ate with had been repentant. According to Theissen and Merz, Jesus taught that God was generously giving people an opportunity to repent.

#### Role

Jesus referred to himself as a "[son of man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_man "Son of man")" in the colloquial sense of "a person", but scholars do not know whether he also meant himself when he referred to the heavenly "Son of Man". Paul the Apostle and other early Christians interpreted the "Son of Man" as the risen Jesus. [Dale Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Allison "Dale Allison") argues Jesus identified himself as the son of man in Daniel, rejecting the notion of another eschatological figure.

The Gospels refer to Jesus not only as a messiah but in the absolute form as "the Messiah" or, equivalently, "the Christ". In early Judaism, this absolute form of the title is not found, but only phrases such as "his messiah". The tradition is ambiguous enough to leave room for debate as to whether Jesus defined his [eschatological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatological "Eschatological") role as that of the Messiah. The Jewish messianic tradition included many different forms, some of them focused on a messiah figure and others not. Based on the Christian tradition, [Gerd Theissen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_Theissen "Gerd Theissen") advances the hypothesis that Jesus saw himself in messianic terms but did not claim the title "Messiah". Bart Ehrman argues that Jesus did consider himself to be the Messiah, albeit in the sense that he would be the king of the new political order that God would usher in, not in the sense that most people today think of the term.

#### Passover and crucifixion in Jerusalem

Around AD 30, Jesus and his followers travelled from Galilee to Jerusalem to observe *Pesach* ([Passover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passover "Passover")). Jesus caused a disturbance in the [Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple "Second Temple"), which was the centre of Jewish religious and civil authority. Sanders associates it with Jesus's prophecy that the Temple would be totally demolished. Jesus held a last meal with his disciples, which is the origin of the [Sacrament of the Holy Eucharist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist "Eucharist"). According to [John P. Meier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_P._Meier "John P. Meier"), Jesus having a final meal with his disciples is generally accepted among scholars, and belongs to the framework of the narrative of Jesus' life, with a majority viewing Mark 14 as substantially historical. The meal appears to have pointed to Jesus's place in the Kingdom of God when Jesus probably knew he was to be killed, although he may have still hoped that God might intervene.

The Gospels say that Jesus was betrayed to the authorities by a disciple, and many scholars consider this report to be highly reliable. He was executed on the orders of Pontius Pilate, the Roman prefect of Judaea. Pilate most likely saw Jesus's reference to the Kingdom of God as a threat to Roman authority and worked with the Temple elites to have Jesus executed. The Sadducean high-priestly leaders of the Temple more plausibly had Jesus executed for political reasons than for his teaching. They may have regarded him as a threat to stability, especially after he caused a disturbance at the Second Temple. Other factors, such as Jesus's triumphal entry into Jerusalem, may have contributed to this decision. Most scholars consider Jesus's crucifixion to be factual because early Christians would not have invented the painful death of their leader.

#### After crucifixion

After Jesus's death, his followers said he was restored to life, although the exact details of their experiences are unclear. The gospel reports contradict each other; Sanders suggests competition among those claiming to have seen him first rather than deliberate fraud, while [White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._Michael_White "L. Michael White") emphasizes differences in the agendas of the evangelists. Differences between accounts were a feature of ancient biographies, such as the accounts of [Otho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otho "Otho") in Suetonius and Plutarch. Another common hypothesis among historians is that all reported perceptions of Jesus are [confabulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confabulated "Confabulated") or a case of [mistaken identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewitness_identification#Causes_of_eyewitness_error "Eyewitness identification"). The followers of Jesus formed a community to wait for his return and the founding of his kingdom.

### Scholarly portrayals of Jesus

Modern research on the historical Jesus has not led to a unified picture of the historical figure, partly because of the variety of academic traditions represented by the scholars. Given the scarcity of historical sources, it is generally difficult for any scholar to construct a portrait of Jesus that can be considered historically valid beyond the basic elements of his life. The portraits of Jesus constructed in these quests often differ from each other, and from the image portrayed in the Gospels.

Jesus is seen as the founder of, in the words of Sanders, a "renewal movement within Judaism". One of the criteria used to discern historical details in the "[third quest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quest_for_the_historical_Jesus#Third_quest "Quest for the historical Jesus")" is the criterion of plausibility, relative to Jesus's Jewish context and to his influence on Christianity. A disagreement in contemporary research is whether Jesus was [apocalyptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypticism "Apocalypticism"). Most scholars conclude that he was an apocalyptic preacher, like [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist "John the Baptist") and [Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle "Paul the Apostle"). Certain prominent North American scholars, such as [Burton Mack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burton_Mack "Burton Mack") and John Dominic Crossan, advocate for a non-[eschatological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatological "Eschatological") Jesus, one who is more of a Cynic [sage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sage_(philosophy) "Sage (philosophy)") than an apocalyptic preacher. In addition to portraying Jesus as an apocalyptic prophet, a charismatic healer or a [cynic philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynicism_(philosophy) "Cynicism (philosophy)"), some scholars portray him as the true messiah or an [egalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarian "Egalitarian") prophet of [social change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_change "Social change"). The attributes described in the portraits sometimes overlap, and scholars who differ on some attributes sometimes agree on others.

Since the 18th century, scholars have occasionally put forth that Jesus was a political national messiah, but the evidence for this portrait is negligible. Likewise, the proposal that Jesus was a [Zealot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zealot "Zealot") does not fit with the earliest strata of the Synoptic tradition.

### Language, ethnicity, and appearance

Jesus grew up in Galilee and much of his ministry took place there. The languages spoken in Galilee and Judea during the 1st century AD included [Jewish Palestinian Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Palestinian_Aramaic "Jewish Palestinian Aramaic"), [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew "Hebrew"), and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek "Koine Greek"), with Aramaic being predominant. There is substantial consensus that Jesus gave most of his teachings in Aramaic in the [Galilean dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_dialect "Galilean dialect"). Other than Aramaic and Hebrew, it is likely that he was also able to speak Greek.

Modern scholars agree that Jesus was a Jew of 1st-century [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea "Judea"). *[Ioudaios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ioudaios "Ioudaios")* in New Testament Greek is a term which in the contemporary context may refer to religion ([Second Temple Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple_Judaism "Second Temple Judaism")), ethnicity (of Judea), or both. In a review of the state of modern scholarship, [Amy-Jill Levine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amy-Jill_Levine "Amy-Jill Levine") writes that the entire question of ethnicity is "fraught with difficulty", and that "beyond recognizing that 'Jesus was Jewish', rarely does the scholarship address what being 'Jewish' means".

The New Testament gives no description of the physical appearance of Jesus before his death—it is generally indifferent to racial appearances and does not refer to the features of the people it mentions. Jesus probably looked like a typical Jewish man of his time and place; standing around 166 cm (5 ft 5 in) tall with a thin but fit build, [olive-brown skin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_skin "Olive skin"), brown eyes and short, dark hair. He also probably had a beard that was not particularly long or heavy.

### Christ myth theory

The Christ myth theory is the hypothesis that Jesus of Nazareth never existed; or that if he did, he had virtually nothing to do with the founding of Christianity and the accounts in the [gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospels "Gospels"). Stories of Jesus's birth, along with other key events, have so many mythic elements that some scholars have suggested that Jesus himself was a myth.

[Bruno Bauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno_Bauer "Bruno Bauer") (1809–1882) taught that the first Gospel was a work of literature that produced history rather than described it. According to [Albert Kalthoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Kalthoff "Albert Kalthoff") (1850–1906), a social movement produced Jesus when it encountered Jewish messianic expectations. [Arthur Drews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Drews "Arthur Drews") (1865–1935) saw Jesus as the concrete form of a myth that predated Christianity. Despite arguments put forward by authors who have questioned the existence of a [historical Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Jesus "Historical Jesus"), virtually all scholars of antiquity accept that Jesus was a historical figure and consider the myth theory to be fringe.

## Religious perspectives

Jesus's teachings and the retelling of his life story have significantly influenced the course of [human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history "Human history"), and have directly or indirectly affected the lives of billions of people, even non-Christians, worldwide. He is considered by many people to be the most influential figure to have ever lived, finding a significant place in numerous cultural contexts.

Apart from his own disciples and followers, the Jews of Jesus's day generally [rejected him as the messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rejection_of_Jesus "Rejection of Jesus"), as does Judaism today. Christian theologians, [ecumenical councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_council "Ecumenical council"), reformers and others have written extensively about Jesus over the centuries. [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination "Christian denomination") have often been defined or characterized by their descriptions of Jesus. Meanwhile, [Manichaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeans "Manichaeans"), [Gnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnostics "Gnostics"), [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims"), [Druzes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze "Druze"), the [Baháʼís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%ADs "Baháʼís"), and others have found prominent places for Jesus in their religions.

### Christianity

Jesus is the central figure of Christianity. Although Christian views of Jesus vary, it is possible to summarize the key beliefs shared by the major [denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination "Christian denomination"), as stated in their [catechetical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catechism "Catechism") or [confessional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessionalism_(religion) "Confessionalism (religion)") texts. Christian views of Jesus are derived from the texts of the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament"), including the [canonical gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_gospels "Canonical gospels") and letters such as the [Pauline epistles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_epistles "Pauline epistles") and the [Johannine writings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannine_writings "Johannine writings"). These documents outline the key Christian beliefs about Jesus, including his divinity, humanity, and earthly life, and that he is the Christ and the Son of God. Despite their many shared beliefs, not all Christian denominations agree on all doctrines, and both [major and minor differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism "East–West Schism") on teachings and beliefs have persisted throughout Christianity for centuries.

The New Testament states that the [resurrection of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus "Resurrection of Jesus") is the foundation of the Christian faith. Christian doctrine holds that through his sacrificial death and resurrection, humans can be [reconciled with God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconciliation_(theology) "Reconciliation (theology)") and are thereby offered [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation_in_Christianity "Salvation in Christianity") and the promise of [eternal life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternal_life_(Christianity) "Eternal life (Christianity)"). Recalling the words of John the Baptist in the [gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John "Gospel of John"), these doctrines sometimes refer to Jesus as the [Lamb of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb_of_God "Lamb of God"), who was crucified to fulfil his role as the servant of God. Jesus is thus seen as the [new and last Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Adam "New Adam"), whose obedience contrasts with [Adam's disobedience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_man "Fall of man"). Christians view Jesus as a role model, whose God-focused life believers are encouraged to imitate.

Most branches of Christianity believe that Jesus is both human and the Son of God. While there has been [theological debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christological_controversies "Christological controversies") over his nature, Trinitarian Christians generally believe that Jesus is the Logos, God's incarnation and [God the Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Son "God the Son"), both fully divine and fully human. The doctrine of the Trinity is not universally accepted among Christians. With the [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation "Reformation"), Christians such as [Michael Servetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Servetus "Michael Servetus") and the [Socinians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socinian "Socinian") started questioning the ancient creeds that had established Jesus's two natures. Nontrinitarian Christian groups include [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints "The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints"), [Unitarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarianism "Unitarianism") and [Jehovah's Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah%27s_Witnesses "Jehovah's Witnesses").

Christians revere not only Jesus but also [his name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_of_Jesus "Name of Jesus"). Devotions to the [Holy Name of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Name_of_Jesus "Holy Name of Jesus") go back to the earliest days of Christianity. These devotions and feasts exist in both [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity "Eastern Christianity") and [Western Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christianity "Western Christianity").

### Christian Gnosticism

In [Christian Gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Gnosticism "Christian Gnosticism") (now a largely extinct religious movement), Jesus was sent from the [divine realm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleroma#Gnosticism "Pleroma") and provided the secret knowledge ([gnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosis "Gnosis")) necessary for salvation. It is important to note that Gnosticism is not a homogeneous religion, but an umbrella term used by modern scholars to describe diverse religious and philosophical ideas and systems that emerged in the late first century among [early Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christianity "Early Christianity") sects and other religious movements.

Most Gnostics believed that Jesus was a human who became possessed by the spirit of "the Christ" at his baptism. This spirit left Jesus's body during the crucifixion but was rejoined to him when he was raised from the dead. Some Gnostics were [docetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docetics "Docetics"), believing that Jesus did not have a physical body, but only appeared to possess one. The Gnostic Jesus can both differ greatly from the Christian Jesus, but also build on him. For instance, the [Testimony of Truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimony_of_Truth "Testimony of Truth"), a Gnostic Christian text found in the [Nag Hammadi library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nag_Hammadi_library "Nag Hammadi library") buried around 400 AD, explains that the [serpent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpents_in_the_Bible "Serpents in the Bible") in Genesis 3 who instructs [Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam "Adam") and [Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eve "Eve") is Jesus.

### Judaism

Judaism rejects the idea of Jesus (or any future Jewish messiah) being God, or a mediator to God, or part of a Trinity. It holds that Jesus is not the messiah, arguing that he neither fulfilled the messianic prophecies in the [Tanakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh") nor embodied the personal qualifications of the Messiah. Jews argue that Jesus did not fulfil prophecies to build the [Third Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Temple "Third Temple"), gather Jews back to Israel, bring world peace, and unite humanity under the God of Israel. Furthermore, according to Jewish tradition, there were no prophets after [Malachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malachi "Malachi"), who delivered his prophecies in the 5th century BC.

Judaic criticism of Jesus is long-standing and includes a [range of stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_the_Talmud "Jesus in the Talmud") found in the [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud "Talmud"), written and compiled between the 3rd and 5th centuries. In one such story, *[Yeshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshu "Yeshu") HaNozri* ('Jesus the Nazarene'), a lewd apostate, is executed by the Jewish high court for spreading idolatry and practising magic. According to some, the form *Yeshu* is an [acronym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acronym "Acronym") which in Hebrew reads "may his name and memory be blotted out". The majority of contemporary scholars consider that this material provides no information on the historical Jesus. The *[Mishneh Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mishneh_Torah "Mishneh Torah")*, a late 12th-century work of [Jewish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_law "Jewish law") written by [Moses Maimonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_Maimonides "Moses Maimonides"), states that Jesus is a "stumbling block" who makes "the majority of the world to err and serve a god other than the Lord".

Mediaeval Hebrew literature contains the anecdotal "Episode of Jesus" (known also as *[Toledot Yeshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledot_Yeshu "Toledot Yeshu")*), in which Jesus is described as being the son of Joseph, the son of [Pandera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius_Julius_Abdes_Pantera "Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera") (see: [*Episode of Jesus*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:Story_of_Jesus "s:Translation:Story of Jesus")). The account portrays Jesus as an imposter.

### Manichaeism

[Manichaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism "Manichaeism"), an ancient religious movement, became one of the earliest organized religions outside of Christianity to honour Jesus as a significant figure. Within the Manichaean belief system, Jesus is revered alongside other prominent prophets such as [Zoroaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster "Zoroaster"), [Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha"), and [Mani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mani_(prophet) "Mani (prophet)") himself.

### Islam

A major figure in Islam, Jesus (often referred to by his Quranic name *[*ʿĪsā*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isa_(name) "Isa (name)")*) is considered to be a [messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_and_messengers_in_Islam "Prophets and messengers in Islam") of [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam") and the messiah ([*al-Masīḥ*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masih_(title) "Masih (title)")) who was sent to guide the [Children of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelites "Israelites") (*Banī Isrāʾīl*) with a new scripture, the Gospel (referred to in Islam as [*Injīl*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_in_Islam "Gospel in Islam")). The form *ʿĪsē* is a pre-Islamic [phonosemantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phono-semantic_matching "Phono-semantic matching") correspondence with the [Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic "Safaitic") name *ʿsy*, attested in Arabian inscriptions. Muslims regard the gospels' accounts in the New Testament as partially authentic, and believe that Jesus's original message was altered (*[taḥrīf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%E1%B8%A5r%C4%ABf "Taḥrīf")*) and that [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_in_Islam "Muhammad in Islam") came later to revive it. Belief in Jesus (and all other [messengers of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_in_Islam "Prophets in Islam")) is a requirement for being a [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim "Muslim"). The Quran mentions Jesus by name 25 times—more often than Muhammad—and emphasizes that Jesus was a mortal human who, like all other prophets, had been divinely chosen to spread God's message. While the Quran affirms the Virgin birth of Jesus, he is considered to be neither an incarnation nor the [son of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_God_(Christianity) "Son of God (Christianity)"). Islamic texts emphasize a strict notion of [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") (*[tawḥīd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taw%E1%B8%A5%C4%ABd "Tawḥīd")*) and forbid the association of partners with God, which would be [idolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirk_(Islam) "Shirk (Islam)").

The Quran describes the annunciation to Mary ([*Maryam*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_in_Islam "Mary in Islam")) by the Holy Spirit that she is to give birth to Jesus while remaining a virgin. It calls the virgin birth a miracle that occurred by the will of God. The Quran ([21:91](https://quran.com/21?startingVerse=91) and [66:12](https://quran.com/66?startingVerse=12)) states that God breathed [his spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_(Islam) "Holy Spirit (Islam)") into Mary while she was chaste. Jesus is called a "spirit from God" because he was born through the action of the Spirit, but that belief does not imply [his pre-existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-existence_of_Christ "Pre-existence of Christ").

To aid in his ministry to the Jewish people, Jesus was given the ability to perform miracles, by permission of God rather than by his own power. Through his ministry, Jesus is seen as a [precursor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precursor_(religion) "Precursor (religion)") to Muhammad. In the Quran ([4:155–160](https://quran.com/4?startingVerse=155)) it is said that the Jews claim to have killed Jesus in a similar manner to previous prophets sent to them, but that this was only made to appear that way, and that they neither killed nor crucified him and that he was raised into the heavens by God and that he will testify against the Jews during the [Day of Resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgement_Day_in_Islam "Judgement Day in Islam").

According to most classic [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni "Sunni") and [Twelver Shi'ite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelver_Shi%27ite "Twelver Shi'ite") interpretations of these verses, the likeness of Jesus was cast upon a [substitute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_hypothesis "Substitution hypothesis") (most often one of his disciples), who was crucified in Jesus's stead. Some mediaeval Muslims, including the [*ghulāt*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulat "Ghulat") writing under the name of [al-Mufaddal ibn Umar al-Ju'fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mufaddal_ibn_Umar_al-Ju%27fi "Al-Mufaddal ibn Umar al-Ju'fi"), the [Brethren of Purity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brethren_of_Purity "Brethren of Purity"), various [Isma'ili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma%27ili "Isma'ili") philosophers, and the Sunni mystic [al-Ghazali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali "Al-Ghazali"), affirmed the historicity of Jesus's crucifixion. These thinkers held the [docetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docetic "Docetic") view that, although Jesus's human body had died on the cross, his spirit had survived and ascended into heaven, so that his death was only an appearance. Nevertheless, to Muslims it is the ascension rather than the crucifixion that constitutes a major event in the life of Jesus. There is no mention of his resurrection on the third day, and his death plays no special role in [Islamic theories of salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soteriology#Islam "Soteriology"). Jesus is a central figure in [Islamic eschatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_eschatology "Islamic eschatology"): Muslims believe that [he will return to Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming#Islam "Second Coming") at the [end of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatology "Eschatology") and defeat the [Antichrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antichrist "Antichrist") (*[ad-Dajjal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad-Dajjal "Ad-Dajjal")*) by killing him.

According to the Quran, the coming of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") (also called "Ahmad") was predicted by Jesus:

> And ˹remember˺ when Jesus, son of Mary, said, "O children of Israel! I am truly Allah's messenger to you, confirming the Torah which came before me, and giving good news of a messenger after me whose name will be Aḥmad." Yet when the Prophet came to them with clear proofs, they said, "This is pure magic."

— [Surah As-Saf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As-Saff "As-Saff") [61:6](https://quran.com/61?startingVerse=6)

Through this verse, early Arab Muslims claimed legitimacy for their new faith in the existing religious traditions and the predictions of Jesus.

#### Ahmadiyya

The [Ahmadiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmadiyya "Ahmadiyya") Muslim Community has [several teachings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Ahmadiyya_Islam "Jesus in Ahmadiyya Islam") about Jesus. Ahmadis believe that he was a mortal man who survived his crucifixion and died a natural death at the age of 120 in [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir "Kashmir"), India, and is buried at [Roza Bal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roza_Bal "Roza Bal").

### Druze

In the [Druze faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_faith "Druze faith"), Jesus is considered and revered as one of the seven spokesmen or prophets (*natiq*), defined as messengers or intermediaries between God and mankind, along with figures including [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses"), [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") and [Muhammad ibn Isma'il](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Isma%27il "Muhammad ibn Isma'il"), each of them sent at a different period of history to preach the message of God. In Druze tradition, Jesus is known under three titles: the True Messiah (*al-Masih al-Haq*), the Messiah of all Nations (*Masih al-Umam*), and the Messiah of Sinners. This is due, respectively, to the belief that Jesus delivered the true Gospel message, the belief that he was the Saviour of all nations, and the belief that he offers forgiveness.

### Baháʼí Faith

In the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith"), Jesus is considered one of the [Manifestations of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifestation_of_God_(Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith) "Manifestation of God (Baháʼí Faith)"), defined as divine messengers or prophets sent by God to guide humanity, along with other religious figures such as Moses, [Krishna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna "Krishna"), [Zoroaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster "Zoroaster"), [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha "Buddha"), Muhammad, and [Baháʼu'lláh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BCu%27ll%C3%A1h "Baháʼu'lláh"). Baháʼís believe that these religious founders or leaders have contributed to the [progressive revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_revelation_(Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD) "Progressive revelation (Baháʼí)") by bringing spiritual and moral values to humanity in their own time and place. As a Manifestation of God, Jesus is believed to reflect God's qualities and attributes, but is not considered the only saviour of humanity nor the incarnation of God. Baháʼís believe in the virgin birth, but see the resurrection and the miracles of Jesus as symbolic.

### Other

Hinduism has no established set of beliefs and no universal or common view of Jesus. However, a lot of Hindus, including religious and political leaders, tend to variously consider Jesus as either a [Āchārya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acharya "Acharya"), [Sadhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadhu "Sadhu") or [Avatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar "Avatar"). Some [Hare Krishnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Society_for_Krishna_Consciousness "International Society for Krishna Consciousness") also claim that Jesus Christ is an exemplary Hare Krishna [devotee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_devotional_movements "Hindu devotional movements"), and was [predicted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prediction "Prediction") and [prophesied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecy "Prophecy") in the [Bhavishya Purana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavishya_Purana "Bhavishya Purana").

[Paramahansa Yogananda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramahansa_Yogananda "Paramahansa Yogananda"), an Indian [guru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru "Guru"), taught that Jesus was the reincarnation of [Elisha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha "Elisha") and a student of John the Baptist, the reincarnation of [Elijah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elijah "Elijah"). Some [Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist "Buddhist"), including [Tenzin Gyatso, the 14th Dalai Lama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenzin_Gyatso,_the_14th_Dalai_Lama "Tenzin Gyatso, the 14th Dalai Lama"), regard Jesus as a [bodhisattva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva "Bodhisattva") who dedicated his life to the welfare of people. The [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age "New Age") movement entertains a wide variety of views on Jesus.

[Theosophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophists "Theosophists"), from whom many New Age teachings originated, refer to Jesus as the [Master Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_Jesus "Master Jesus"), a spiritual reformer, and they believe that Christ, after [various incarnations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation "Reincarnation"), occupied the body of Jesus.
In the [Anthroposophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthroposophy "Anthroposophy") founded by [Rudolf Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Steiner "Rudolf Steiner"), Jesus Christ is a central balancing force mediating between the two opposing [polarities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualism "Dualism") of [evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evil "Evil"), namely the fanatical exalted [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism") of [Lucifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucifer "Lucifer"), and the cold [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism "Materialism") of [Ahriman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahriman "Ahriman").

[Atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist") reject Jesus's divinity, but have different views about him—from challenging [his mental health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_health_of_Jesus "Mental health of Jesus") to emphasizing his "moral superiority" ([Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins")).

## Artistic depictions

As in other [Early Christian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christian_art "Early Christian art"), the earliest depictions date to the late 2nd or early 3rd century, and surviving images are found in the [Catacombs of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catacombs_of_Rome "Catacombs of Rome"). Some of the earliest depictions of Jesus at the [Dura-Europos church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dura-Europos_church "Dura-Europos church") date to before 256. A wide range of depictions of Jesus appeared during the next two millennia, influenced by cultural settings, political circumstances and theological contexts.

The depiction of Christ in pictorial form was highly [controversial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aniconism_in_Christianity "Aniconism in Christianity") in the early Church. From the 5th century, flat painted [icons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon "Icon") became popular in the Eastern Church. The [Byzantine Iconoclasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm "Byzantine Iconoclasm") acted as a barrier to developments in the East, but by the 9th century, art was permitted again. The [Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation "Protestant Reformation") brought renewed resistance to imagery, but total prohibition was atypical, and Protestant objections to images have tended to reduce since the 16th century. Although large images are generally avoided, few Protestants now object to book illustrations depicting Jesus. The use of depictions of Jesus is advocated by the leaders of denominations such as [Anglicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicans "Anglicans") and [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholics "Catholics") and is a key element of the [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox "Eastern Orthodox") tradition.

In Eastern Christian art, the [Transfiguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfiguration_of_Jesus_in_Christian_art "Transfiguration of Jesus in Christian art") was a major theme, with every Eastern Orthodox monk trained in icon painting having to prove his craft by painting an icon depicting it. Icons receive the external marks of veneration, such as kisses and prostration, and they are thought to be powerful channels of divine grace.

In Western Europe, the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") brought forth artists who focused on depictions of Jesus; [Fra Angelico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fra_Angelico "Fra Angelico") and others followed [Giotto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giotto "Giotto") in the systematic development of uncluttered images. Before the Protestant Reformation, the [crucifix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifix "Crucifix") was common in Western Christianity. It is a model of the cross with Jesus crucified on it. The crucifix became the central ornament of the altar in the 13th century, a use that has been nearly universal in Roman Catholic churches since then.

## Associated relics

The total destruction that ensued with the [siege of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(70_AD) "Siege of Jerusalem (70 AD)") by the Romans in AD 70 made the survival of items from 1st-century Judea very rare, and almost no direct records survive about the history of Judaism from the last part of the 1st century to the 2nd century. Biblical scholar [Margaret M. Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_M._Mitchell "Margaret M. Mitchell") writes that, although [Eusebius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius "Eusebius") (4th century) reports (*[Ecclesiastical History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_History_(Eusebius) "Church History (Eusebius)")* III 5.3) that the early Christians left Jerusalem for [Pella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pella,_Jordan "Pella, Jordan") just before Jerusalem was subjected to the final lockdown, we must accept that no items from the early Jerusalem Church have survived. Paranormal investigator [Joe Nickell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Nickell "Joe Nickell") writes, "as investigation after investigation has shown, not a single, reliably authenticated relic of Jesus exists."

Throughout the history of Christianity, [relics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic "Relic") attributed to Jesus have been claimed, but doubt has been cast on them. The 16th-century Catholic theologian [Erasmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus "Erasmus") wrote sarcastically about the proliferation of relics and the number of buildings that could have been constructed from the wood claimed to be from the [cross used in the Crucifixion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross "True Cross"). Similarly, while experts debate whether Jesus was crucified with three nails or four, at least thirty [holy nails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_nail "Holy nail") are venerated as relics across Europe.

Some relics, such as purported remnants of the [crown of thorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_thorns "Crown of thorns") placed on the head of Jesus, receive only a modest number of [pilgrims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage "Christian pilgrimage"), while the [Shroud of Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shroud_of_Turin "Shroud of Turin") (which is associated with an approved [Catholic devotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_devotion "Catholic devotion") to the [Holy Face of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Face_of_Jesus "Holy Face of Jesus")), has received millions, including the popes [John Paul II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Paul_II "John Paul II") and [Benedict XVI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_XVI "Benedict XVI").
