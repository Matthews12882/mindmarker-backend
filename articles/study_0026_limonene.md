# Limonene

**Limonene** ([/ˈlɪmənˌiːn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")) is a slightly yellow-green liquid [aliphatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliphatic "Aliphatic") hydrocarbon classified as a cyclic [monoterpene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoterpene "Monoterpene"), and is the major component in the [fragrance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aroma_compound "Aroma compound") and [essential oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_oil "Essential oil") of [citrus fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrus_fruit "Citrus fruit") peels, taking its name from Italian *limone* '[lemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemon "Lemon")'.

Limonene is a [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_(chemistry) "Chirality (chemistry)") molecule, and most biological sources produce just one [enantiomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantiomer "Enantiomer") ([isomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomer "Isomer")). The (+)-isomer, *d*-limonene, which is the [(*R*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahn%E2%80%93Ingold%E2%80%93Prelog_priority_rules "Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority rules")-enantiomer, occurs more commonly in nature in citrus fruit peels, the principal commercial source, from which it is obtained commercially by two primary methods: [centrifugal separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_separation "Centrifugal separation") and [steam distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_distillation "Steam distillation"). *d*-Limonene is used as a flavoring agent in food manufacturing, in chemical synthesis as a [precursor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precursor_(chemistry) "Precursor (chemistry)") to [carvone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carvone "Carvone"), and as a renewables-based [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent") in [cleaning products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleaning_product "Cleaning product"). It has a "citrus, orange, fresh, sweet, peely" aroma.

The less common (−)-[isomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomer "Isomer"), *l*-limonene, which is the (*S*)-enantiomer, has a [piny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine "Pine"), [turpentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turpentine "Turpentine")-like odor, and is found in the edible parts of such plants as [caraway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caraway "Caraway"), [dill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dill "Dill"), and [bergamot orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergamot_orange "Bergamot orange").

## In plants

Limonene is a major component of the aromatic scents and [resins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resins "Resins") characteristic of numerous [coniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coniferous "Coniferous") and [broadleaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadleaved "Broadleaved") trees: red and silver maple (*[Acer rubrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acer_rubrum "Acer rubrum")*, *[Acer saccharinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acer_saccharinum "Acer saccharinum")*), cottonwoods (*[Populus angustifolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populus_angustifolia "Populus angustifolia")*), aspens (*[Populus grandidentata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populus_grandidentata "Populus grandidentata")*, *[Populus tremuloides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populus_tremuloides "Populus tremuloides")*) sumac (*[Rhus glabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhus_glabra "Rhus glabra")*), spruce (*[Picea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picea "Picea")* spp.), various pines (e.g., *[Pinus echinata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_echinata "Pinus echinata")*, *[Pinus ponderosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_ponderosa "Pinus ponderosa")*, *[Pinus leucodermis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_leucodermis "Pinus leucodermis")*), Douglas fir (*[Pseudotsuga menziesii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudotsuga_menziesii "Pseudotsuga menziesii")*), larches (*[Larix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larix "Larix")* spp.), true firs (*[Abies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abies "Abies")* spp.), hemlocks (*[Tsuga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuga "Tsuga")* spp.), cedars (*[Cedrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cedrus "Cedrus")* spp.), various [Cupressaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupressaceae "Cupressaceae"), and juniper bush (*[Juniperus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniperus "Juniperus")* spp.).[*[failed verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*] It contributes to the characteristic odor of [orange peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_oil "Orange oil"), [orange juice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_juice "Orange juice") and other [citrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrus "Citrus") fruits. To optimize recovery of valued components from citrus peel waste, (+)-limonene is typically removed.

## Chemical reactions

Limonene is a relatively stable [monoterpene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoterpene "Monoterpene") and can be distilled without decomposition, although at elevated temperatures it [cracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_(chemistry) "Cracking (chemistry)") to form [isoprene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isoprene "Isoprene"). It oxidizes easily in moist air to produce [carveol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carveol "Carveol"), [carvone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carvone "Carvone"), and limonene oxide. With sulfur, it undergoes dehydrogenation to [*p*-cymene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-Cymene "P-Cymene").

Limonene occurs commonly as the (*R*)-enantiomer, but [racemizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racemization "Racemization") at 300 °C. When warmed with [mineral acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_acid "Mineral acid"), limonene isomerizes to the [conjugated diene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugated_system "Conjugated system") α-[terpinene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terpinene "Terpinene") (which can also easily be converted to [*p*-cymene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-Cymene "P-Cymene")). Evidence for this isomerization includes the formation of [Diels–Alder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diels%E2%80%93Alder_reaction "Diels–Alder reaction") [adducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adduct "Adduct") between α-terpinene adducts and [maleic anhydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maleic_anhydride "Maleic anhydride").

It is possible to effect reaction at one of the double bonds selectively. Anhydrous [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride "Hydrogen chloride") reacts preferentially at the disubstituted alkene, whereas [epoxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoxidation "Epoxidation") with [*m*-CPBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-Chloroperoxybenzoic_acid "Meta-Chloroperoxybenzoic acid") occurs at the trisubstituted alkene.

In another synthetic method [Markovnikov addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markovnikov%27s_rule "Markovnikov's rule") of [trifluoroacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifluoroacetic_acid "Trifluoroacetic acid") followed by [hydrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolysis "Hydrolysis") of the acetate gives [terpineol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terpineol "Terpineol").

The most widely practiced conversion of limonene is to [carvone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carvone "Carvone"). The three-step reaction begins with the [regioselective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regioselective "Regioselective") addition of [nitrosyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrosyl_chloride "Nitrosyl chloride") across the trisubstituted double bond. This species is then converted to the [oxime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxime "Oxime") with a [base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_(chemistry) "Base (chemistry)"), and the [hydroxylamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxylamine "Hydroxylamine") is removed to give the [ketone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketone "Ketone")-containing carvone.

### Biosynthesis

In nature, limonene is formed from [geranyl pyrophosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geranyl_pyrophosphate "Geranyl pyrophosphate"), via cyclization of a [neryl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerol "Nerol") [carbocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation "Carbocation") or its equivalent as shown. The final step involves loss of a proton from the cation to form the [alkene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene "Alkene").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Uses

As the main fragrance of citrus peels, D-limonene is used in food manufacturing and some medicines, such as a [flavoring agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavoring_agent "Flavoring agent") to mask the bitter taste of [alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaloids "Alkaloids"), and as a fragrance in [perfumery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfumery "Perfumery"), aftershave lotions, bath products, and other [personal care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_care "Personal care") products. (+)-Limonene is also used as a [botanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botanical "Botanical") [insecticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide "Insecticide"). (+)-Limonene is used in the organic herbicides. It is added to cleaning products, such as hand cleansers, to give a lemon or orange fragrance (see [orange oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_oil "Orange oil")) and for its ability to dissolve oils. In contrast, (−)-limonene has a [piny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine "Pine"), [turpentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turpentine "Turpentine")-like odor.

Limonene is used as a [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent") for cleaning purposes, such as [adhesive remover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhesive_remover "Adhesive remover"), or the removal of oil from machine parts, as it is produced from a renewable source (citrus essential oil, as a byproduct of [orange juice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_juice "Orange juice") manufacture). It is used as a [paint stripper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paint_stripper "Paint stripper") and is also useful as a fragrant alternative to turpentine. Limonene is also used as a solvent in some [model airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_airplane "Model airplane") glues and as a constituent in some paints. Commercial air fresheners, with air propellants, containing limonene are used by [stamp collectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philately "Philately") to remove self-adhesive postage stamps from envelope paper.

Limonene is also used as a solvent for fused filament fabrication based [3D printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_printing "3D printing"). Printers can print the plastic of choice for the model, but erect supports and binders from [high impact polystyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_impact_polystyrene "High impact polystyrene") (HIPS), a [polystyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polystyrene "Polystyrene") plastic that is easily soluble in limonene.

In preparing [tissues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tissue_(biology) "Tissue (biology)") for [histology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histology "Histology") or histopathology, D-limonene is often used as a less toxic substitute for [xylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylene "Xylene") when clearing dehydrated specimens. Clearing agents are liquids miscible with alcohols (such as [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol") or [isopropanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isopropanol "Isopropanol")) and with melted [paraffin wax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraffin_wax "Paraffin wax"), in which specimens are embedded to facilitate cutting of thin sections for [microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopy "Microscopy").

Limonene, from orange peel oil, is also combustible and has been considered as an alternative petrochemical fuel additive and [biofuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel "Biofuel").

## Safety and research

Applied to skin, limonene may cause irritation from [contact dermatitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_dermatitis "Contact dermatitis"), but otherwise appears to be safe for human use. Limonene is [flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammable "Flammable") as a liquid or vapor and toxic to [aquatic life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_life "Aquatic life").

### Cancer

There is no evidence that the limonene in peel oils of citrus fruits affects the onset or progress of cancer, with one US national agency stating, "There is no consistent evidence that people with cancer who consume limonene—either in supplement form or by eating citrus fruits—get better or are more likely to be cured".
