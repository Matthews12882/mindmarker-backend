# India

**India**, officially the **Republic of India**, is a country in [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia"). It is the world's [seventh-largest country by area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area "List of countries and dependencies by area") and its [most populous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population "List of countries and dependencies by population"). Bounded by the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean") on the south, the [Arabian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Sea "Arabian Sea") on the southwest, and the [Bay of Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Bengal "Bay of Bengal") on the southeast, it shares land borders with [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan") to the west, [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China"), [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal "Nepal"), and [Bhutan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhutan "Bhutan") to the north, and [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh") and [Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myanmar "Myanmar") to the east. In the Indian Ocean, India is near [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") and the [Maldives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maldives "Maldives").

[Modern humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_humans "Modern humans") arrived on the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent") from [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") by 55,000 years ago. [Settled life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") emerged in the western margins of the [Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River "Indus River") [river basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_basin "River basin") 9,000 years ago, evolving into the [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation "Indus Valley Civilisation") of the third millennium BCE. By 1200 BCE, an [archaic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-language "Proto-language") of [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"), an [Indo-European language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages "Indo-European languages"), [diffused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-cultural_diffusion "Trans-cultural diffusion") into India from the northwest, its [earliest hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rig_Veda "Rig Veda") containing [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion "Historical Vedic religion") of what would become [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"). The pre-existing [Dravidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravidian_languages "Dravidian languages") and other languages were gradually superseded in India's [northern plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain#Ganges_Plain "Indo-Gangetic Plain"). Across the central stretch of these plains, [rapid urbanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Urbanisation "Second Urbanisation") took place, and after 500 BCE a [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste "Caste")-based, [hereditary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heredity "Heredity") form of [social stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stratification "Social stratification") began to solidify as [Hinduism took fuller shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Hinduism#Roots_of_Hinduism "History of Hinduism"). During this period, [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Buddhism_in_India "History of Buddhism in India") and [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jainism "History of Jainism") arose, proclaiming [social orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_order "Social order") unlinked to heredity. The subsequent emergence of the loose-knit [Maurya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya_Empire "Maurya Empire") and [Gupta Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_Empire "Gupta Empire") gave rise to wide-ranging cultural and scientific creativity. However, these eras saw a steady decline in women's status, alongside the eventual institutionalisation of [untouchability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untouchability "Untouchability"). In [South India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India"), across the first millennium CE, the [Middle kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_kingdoms_of_India#The_Deccan_plateau_and_South "Middle kingdoms of India") transmitted Dravidian language scripts and cultural traditions to [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"). Well before the millennium's end, [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_India "History of the Jews in India"), [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India "Christianity in India"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_India "Islam in India"), and [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism_in_India "Zoroastrianism in India") had become established on India's southern and western coasts.

Early in the [2nd millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_millennium "2nd millennium"), Muslim armies from [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia") intermittently overran India's northern plains, eventually establishing the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate") and drawing India into the [cosmopolitan networks of medieval Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age"). The [Vijayanagara Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vijayanagara_Empire "Vijayanagara Empire") built a powerful [military-fiscal state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal-military_state "Fiscal-military state") that fostered a flourishing, distinctively South Indian Hindu culture. During this era, [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism "Sikhism") emerged in the [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab "Punjab"), promoting an [egalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarianism "Egalitarianism") devotion that transcended traditional Hindu and Muslim boundaries. The [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") ushered in two centuries of economic expansion and relative peace, leaving a lasting legacy of [administrative centralisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire#Administration_and_state "Mughal Empire") and [cultural synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_architecture "Mughal architecture"). The [expanding rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_rule_in_India "Company rule in India") of the British [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company "East India Company") consolidated territorial control over the subcontinent while transforming India into a colonial economy. Crown rule, or the [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj "British Raj"), began in 1858. Although equality before the law promised to Indians was slow to materialise, both [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidencies_and_provinces_of_British_India "Presidencies and provinces of British India") and, to a lesser extent, the [princely states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princely_state "Princely state") saw [technological changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution") as [modern education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood%27s_despatch "Wood's despatch") and more organised public life took root. [A nationalist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_independence_movement "Indian independence movement") emerged in India, the first in the non-European [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire"). Noted for nonviolent resistance after 1920, it was a major factor in ending British rule. In 1947, the British Indian Empire was [partitioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India "Partition of India") into the independent [dominions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion#India_and_Pakistan "Dominion") of a Hindu-majority [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_of_India "Dominion of India") and a Muslim-majority [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_of_Pakistan "Dominion of Pakistan"). The partition brought large-scale loss of life and unprecedented migration.

The [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India "Constitution of India") was adopted in 1950 and declared India to be a [federal republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_republic "Federal republic") governed through a democratic [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system "Parliamentary system"); it guarantees [freedom of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech"), [freedom of press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_press "Freedom of press"), and [religious pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_pluralism "Religious pluralism"). Amendments enacted from [1951](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India "First Amendment of the Constitution of India") onwards strengthened affirmative action programmes to reduce [caste inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system_of_India "Caste system of India"). The [reorganization of states in 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_Reorganisation_Act,_1956 "States Reorganisation Act, 1956") along linguistic lines institutionalised [regional language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_languages_in_India "Regional languages in India") pride and led to the growth of [regional literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_literature#In_modern_Indian_languages "Indian literature") and [cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_India "Cinema of India"). During the 1970s, India reversed agricultural stagnation through the [Green Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution_in_India "Green Revolution in India"), achieving national [food security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_security "Food security"). The country also altered the regional power balance through [support for Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War#Indian_involvement "Bangladesh Liberation War") and enacted [constitutional reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-sixth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India "Twenty-sixth Amendment of the Constitution of India") to end [princely privileges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_purse_in_India "Privy purse in India"). India's population grew from 361 million in 1951 to approximately 1.464 billion in 2025. Economic reforms of the 1990s integrated India's economy with the world's. By the 2010s, the [Indian economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India "Economy of India") had a stably [high rate of growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate "List of countries by real GDP growth rate"). A large middle class and a communications boom gained momentum. However, challenges remain, including widening [income gaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality "Economic inequality"), [gender inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_inequality_in_India "Gender inequality in India") and [environmental degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_India "Environmental issues in India"). In recent years, India has seen a political shift toward [majoritarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majoritarianism "Majoritarianism"). A [nuclear-weapon state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction "India and weapons of mass destruction") with high [military expenditure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Armed_Forces "Indian Armed Forces"), India is among the world's [largest economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India "Economy of India") and a [founding member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_India "Foreign relations of India") of the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations"), while [its cultural legacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_India "Culture of India") encompasses 45 [UNESCO World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_India "List of World Heritage Sites in India"). India's [megadiverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megadiverse_country "Megadiverse country") land features four [biodiversity hotspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_hotspots "Biodiversity hotspots"). [Its wildlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_of_India "Wildlife of India") is supported in [protected habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_areas_of_India "Protected areas of India") and traditionally viewed with cultural tolerance.

## Etymology

According to the *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")*, the English proper noun "India" derives most immediately from the [Classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin "Classical Latin") *India*, a reference to a loosely-defined historical region of Asia stretching from [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia") to the borders of China. Further [etymons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymon "Etymon") are: [Hellenistic Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Greek "Hellenistic Greek") *India* (Ἰνδία); [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") *Indos* (Ἰνδός), or the River [Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus "Indus"); Achaemenian [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian "Old Persian") *[Hinduš](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%C5%A1 "Hinduš")* (an eastern province of the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire")); and [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") *Sindhu*, or "river," but specifically the Indus river, and by extension its well-settled basin. The [Ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks "Ancient Greeks") referred to South Asians as *Indoi*, 'the people of the Indus'.

The term *[Bharat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_for_India#Bharat "Names for India")* (*Bhārat*; pronounced [[ˈbʱaːɾət]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Hindi_and_Urdu "Help:IPA/Hindi and Urdu") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hi-Bharat.ogg "File:Hi-Bharat.ogg")), mentioned in both [Indian epic poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_epic_poetry "Indian epic poetry") and the [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India "Constitution of India"), is used in its variations by [many Indian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_for_India "Names for India"). A modern rendering of the historical name *Bharatavarsha*, which applied originally to [North India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_India "North India"), *Bharat* gained increased currency from the mid-19th century as a native name for India.

*[Hindustan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustan "Hindustan")* ([[ɦɪndʊˈstaːn]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Hindi_and_Urdu "Help:IPA/Hindi and Urdu") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hindustan.ogg "File:Hindustan.ogg")) is a [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian "Middle Persian") name for India that became popular by the 13th century, and was used widely since the era of the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire"). The meaning of *Hindustan* has varied, referring to a region encompassing the northern [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent") (present-day northern India and [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan")) or to India in its near entirety.

## History

### Ancient India

Based on [coalescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalescent_theory "Coalescent theory") of [Mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA "Mitochondrial DNA") and [Y Chromosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y_Chromosome "Y Chromosome") data, the earliest extant [lineages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lineage_(evolution) "Lineage (evolution)") of [modern humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens") on the Indian subcontinent had reached there from Africa between 80,000 and 50,000 years ago, and with high likelihood by 55,000 years ago. [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") and [chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic "Chalcolithic") cultures arose in the western margins of the [Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River "Indus River") [river basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_basin "River basin") around [Mehrgarh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrgarh "Mehrgarh") in [Balochistan, Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochistan,_Pakistan "Balochistan, Pakistan") after 7000 BCE. These gradually evolved into the [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation "Indus Valley Civilisation"), which flourished during 2500–1900 BCE in Pakistan and western India. Centred around cities such as [Mohenjo-daro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohenjo-daro "Mohenjo-daro"), [Harappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harappa "Harappa") and [Dholavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dholavira "Dholavira"), its characteristic features included [steatite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soapstone "Soapstone") [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation#Seals "Indus Valley Civilisation"), a [written script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_script "Indus script"), [urban planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harappan_architecture "Harappan architecture"), terracotta human figures and animal statuettes. Between 1500 BCE and 1200 BCE, an [archaic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-language "Proto-language") of [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"), an [Indo-European language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_language "Indo-European language"), [diffused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-cultural_diffusion "Trans-cultural diffusion") into India from the northwest. Its evidence today is found in the [Rig Veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rig_Veda "Rig Veda")—the [scripture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scripture "Scripture") associated with the [historical Vedic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion "Historical Vedic religion"), one of the progenitors of what later became [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism").
The settling of the [Ganges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_river "Ganges river") [river plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_plain "River plain") took place during the next millennium, when swathes of the river system's adjoining regions were deforested and prepared for agriculture. The [Dravidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravidian_languages "Dravidian languages") and [other](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages") languages of India were supplanted in the north, creating a broad [language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family "Language family")-divide, with the [Indo-Aryan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages "Indo-Aryan languages") being spoken mainly in the [north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_India "North India") and [west](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_India "West India"), and the Dravidian in some parts of [east India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India "East India") and most of the [south](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India").

A second [urbanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanisation "Urbanisation") took place in India by the middle-[first-millennium BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_millennium_B.C. "First millennium B.C."). In fortified cities in the central [Ganges plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain#Ganges_Plain "Indo-Gangetic Plain"), [social differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_differentiation "Social differentiation") by [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste "Caste"), or [varna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varna_(Hinduism) "Varna (Hinduism)"), emerged.
Also, by the mid-millennium, two new religions arose: [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jainism "History of Jainism"), based on the teachings of [Mahavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavira "Mahavira"), and [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Buddhism_in_India "History of Buddhism in India"), based on those of [the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha "The Buddha"). Both stressed non-violence and abjured animal sacrifices conducted in [Brahmanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion#Brahmanism "Historical Vedic religion") and birth into a fixed hereditary *varna*. By living ethically, lay people could rise socially and morally in these religions. The rise of the two religions was a backdrop to the emergence of the first loose-knit, geographically extensive power in South Asia, the [Maurya Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya_Empire "Maurya Empire").
During the rule of the founder's grandson, [Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka "Ashoka") (ca. 268–232 BCE), the empire briefly controlled the subcontinent's major urban hubs and arteries, except in the deep south.
The empire's period was notable for creativity in art, architecture, inscriptions, and produced texts. Following his [conquest of Kalinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalinga_War "Kalinga War"), in which his forces caused immense loss of life, Ashoka adopted [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism"), and subsequently commissioned numerous [rock and pillar edicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka "Edicts of Ashoka") throughout the Maurya Empire to promote ethical conduct, non-violence, and social welfare.

By the 4th and 5th centuries CE, the [Gupta Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_Empire "Gupta Empire") had created a system of administration and taxation in the greater Ganges Plain; this system became a model for later Indian kingdoms. Under the Guptas, a renewed Hinduism based on devotion, rather than the management of ritual, began to assert itself. The renewal was reflected in a flowering of art, literature, and science. However, the eras of the two empires also witnessed a steady erosion of women's rights,
and, by the end, the institutionalisation of [untouchability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untouchability "Untouchability"). In South India, the [Sangam literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangam_literature "Sangam literature") of the [Tamil language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language "Tamil language") reveals that, between 200 BCE and 200 CE, the southern peninsula was ruled by the [Cheras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chera_dynasty "Chera dynasty") and the [Cholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_dynasty "Chola dynasty"), along the western and eastern plains, respectively, of the [Kaveri river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaveri_river "Kaveri river") valley, and the [Pandyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandya "Pandya") farther south along the [Vaigai river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaigai_river "Vaigai river") valley. By the sixth century, the [Pallavas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallava "Pallava") had grown into a regional power. Simultaneously, Buddhism and Jainism, which had favoured a conservative [transactionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactionalism "Transactionalism"), were replaced by kingly devotion to the gods of particular places, which became a characteristic of the [Bhakti movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti_movement "Bhakti movement"). The Pallavas, in particular, [traded extensively with the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Roman_trade_relations "Indo-Roman trade relations") and with [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia "Western Asia") and [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia").

### Medieval India

The Indian early medieval age, from 600 to 1200 CE, was defined by regional kingdoms and cultural diversity. During this time, as land was cleared for agriculture, pastoral peoples and new non-traditional ruling classes were integrated into [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste "Caste") society. The caste system consequently began to show regional differences. In the 6th and 7th centuries, the first [devotional hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti "Bhakti") were composed in Tamil. They were emulated across India, leading to a [resurgence of Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti_movement "Bhakti movement") and laying the groundwork for the subcontinent's [regional languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_India "Languages of India"). Indian royalty and the temples they supported drew large numbers of citizens to the capital cities, which grew into economic hubs. By the 8th and 9th centuries, South Indian culture and political systems spread across [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia").
In the second half of the first millennium CE, [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_India "History of the Jews in India"), [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India "Christianity in India"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_India "Islam in India") and [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism_in_India "Zoroastrianism in India") established roots on India's southern and western coasts.

After the 10th century, [Muslim Central Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_peoples#Iranian,_Indian,_Arabic,_and_Anatolian_expansion "Turkic peoples") nomadic clans—using [swift-horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courser_(horse) "Courser (horse)") cavalry and raising vast armies united by ethnicity—repeatedly overran South Asia's north-western plains, leading eventually to the establishment of the Islamic [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate") in 1206, and drawing India into the [Islamic cosmopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age"). The Sultanate, which came to control much of North India and made many forays into South India, initially disrupted Indian elites, but largely left its vast non-Muslim subject population to its own laws and customs.
By repeatedly repulsing [Mongol raiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire") in the 13th century, the sultanate saved India from the devastation visited on [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia "West Asia") and [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"),
setting the scene for centuries of [migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration "Human migration") of fleeing soldiers, scholars, mystics, and artisans from those regions into the subcontinent, thereby creating a syncretic Indo-Islamic culture in the north.
The sultanate's raiding and weakening of the regional kingdoms of South India paved the way for the indigenous [Vijayanagara Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vijayanagara_Empire "Vijayanagara Empire").
Embracing a strong [Shaivite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaivism "Shaivism") tradition and building upon the military technology of the sultanate, the empire came to control a large portion of peninsular India, and influenced South Indian society long after.

### Early modern India

In the early 16th century, northern India—then mainly under Muslim rulers—fell again to the superior mobility and firepower of a new generation of Central Asian warriors. The resulting [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") did not stamp out the local societies it came to rule. Instead, it balanced and pacified them through new administrative practices and diverse and inclusive ruling elites. This led to more systematic, centralised, and uniform rule. By departing from tribal bonds and exclusive Islamic identity, particularly under Akbar, the Mughals united their vast empire through personal loyalty to a near-divine emperor, expressed through a shared Persianised culture.

The Mughal state derived most revenue from agriculture. Its economic policies mandated tax payments in a well-regulated silver currency, and integrated peasants and artisans into larger markets. The relative peace the empire maintained across much of the 17th century helped drive India's economic expansion. Mughal ruling classes fostered extensive patronage of the arts, including [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_painting "Mughal painting"), literary forms, textiles, and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_architecture "Mughal architecture"). Newly coherent social groups in northern and western India, such as the [Marathas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_Empire "Maratha Empire"), the [Rajputs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajput "Rajput"), and the [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_Empire "Sikh Empire"), gained governing ambitions during Mughal rule. Through collaboration or adversity, the Mughals gave them recognition and military experience. Expanding commerce gave rise to new Indian commercial and political elites along the coasts of southern and eastern India. From the late 17th century, the Mughal Empire gradually began to disintegrate, and these local elites and communities frequently gained greater autonomy and self-governance.

By the early 18th century, the lines between commercial enterprise and political sovereignty had become increasingly blurred. European chartered companies solidified their presence in India through fortified coastal outposts; most notable was the [English East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company "East India Company"). Its control of the seas, greater resources, and more advanced military training and technology led it to assert its military strength increasingly and caused it to become attractive to a portion of the Indian elite; these factors were crucial in allowing the company to gain control over the [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal "Bengal") region by 1765 and sideline the other European companies. Its further access to the riches of Bengal and the subsequent increased strength and size of its army enabled it to annexe or subdue most of India by the 1820s. India no longer exported manufactured goods as it long had, but instead supplied the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire") with raw materials. Many historians consider this to be the onset of India's colonial period. By this time, with its economic power severely curtailed by the British Parliament and having effectively been made an arm of British administration, the East India Company began more consciously to enter non-economic arenas, including education, social reform, and culture.

### Modern India

The 1848 appointment of [Lord Dalhousie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Broun-Ramsay,_1st_Marquess_of_Dalhousie "James Broun-Ramsay, 1st Marquess of Dalhousie") as the Governor General of the East India Company set the stage for changes essential to a modern state: consolidating and [demarcating sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Trigonometrical_Survey "Great Trigonometrical Survey"), monitoring the population through censuses, [educating citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood%27s_despatch "Wood's despatch") and [rationalising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalization_(sociology) "Rationalization (sociology)") the bureaucracy. Technological advancements—among them, railways, canals, and the telegraph—were introduced within years of their introduction in Europe, with the East India Company guaranteeing [fixed financial returns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_on_investment "Return on investment") to attract British private investors. Simultaneously, the proponents of these public works [lobbied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobbying "Lobbying") the British government for support, presenting them as necessities for military logistics and security and Indian social and cultural reform. Thus, the [Upper Doab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doab "Doab")'s [Ganges Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_Canal "Ganges Canal")—conceived by [Proby Cautley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proby_Cautley "Proby Cautley") under [Lord Auckland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Auckland "Lord Auckland") and completed under Dalhousie—was promoted to Company's [Court of Directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Directors "Board of Directors") in London as both a vital relief response to the [Agra famine of 1837–1838](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agra_famine_of_1837%E2%80%931838 "Agra famine of 1837–1838") and a revenue-generating irrigation and navigation project. The canal, which brought agricultural prosperity to some areas, ultimately caused soil salinisation in others.

Disaffection with the company also grew during this time, culminating in the [Indian Rebellion of 1857](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857 "Indian Rebellion of 1857"), which swept across the central [Gangetic plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangetic_Plain "Gangetic Plain"), and extended into Central India. Shaking the foundations of Company rule, the uprising was provoked by diverse resentments and perceptions, including invasive British-style social reforms, harsh land taxes, and summary treatment of some wealthy landowners and princes. Yet the presumed causes of the uprising did not translate uniformly into actual rebellion. The rebel leaders proclaimed no coherent articles of faith that presaged a new political system. Crucially, many regions of India—including the [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab "Punjab"), the [Madras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madras_Presidency "Madras Presidency") and [Bombay presidencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay_Presidency "Bombay Presidency")—did not join the rebellion, thereby ensuring its ultimate defeat; neither did the [Bengali intelligentsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_Renaissance "Bengal Renaissance"), nor the major [princely states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princely_state "Princely state"). After the rebellion was suppressed in 1858, the East India Company was disbanded, and the British government assumed  [direct administration of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj "British Raj").
Proclaiming a [unitary state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_state "Unitary state") and a gradual but limited British-style parliamentary system, the new rulers also protected princes and landed gentry as a feudal safeguard against future unrest. In the decades following, a more organised public life gradually emerged across India,
eventually leading to the founding of the [Indian National Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_National_Congress "Indian National Congress") in 1885.

Rapid commercialisation of agriculture in the second half of the 19th century brought severe economic setbacks, leaving many small farmers dependent on the uncertainties of distant markets. This period saw a rise in the frequency of large-scale [famines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_in_India "Famine in India"); moreover, despite the risks of infrastructure development being borne by Indian taxpayers, little industrial employment was generated for the local population. However, commercial cropping, especially in the newly canal-irrigated Punjab, did increase food production for internal consumption.
The expanding railway network provided critical famine relief, notably reduced the cost of moving goods, and helped nascent Indian-owned industry.
The famines were systematically documented, leading to the publication of the [Indian Famine Codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Famine_Codes "Indian Famine Codes") and reports of the Indian Famine Commissions of 1880, 1897, and 1901.

During [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), more than [one million Indians served](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Army_during_World_War_I "Indian Army during World War I") and Indian land taxes made substantial financial contributions to the [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I "Allies of World War I") war effort. After the war, the British enacted [limited constitutional reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montagu%E2%80%93Chelmsford_Reforms "Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms") but also [repressive legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowlatt_act "Rowlatt act"), galvanising [Mahatma Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi "Mahatma Gandhi"), newly arrived from South Africa, to launch a [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent "Nonviolent") [non-cooperation movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-cooperation_movement_(1919%E2%80%931922) "Non-cooperation movement (1919–1922)"). In the late 1920s and early 1930s, ideas from [Bolshevism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolshevism "Bolshevism") and [anarchism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism "Anarchism") electrified a growing militancy across the subcontinent and injected a new urgency into the Congress's civil-disobedience movement. Starting in the mid-1930s, the British introduced legislative reforms including special representation for India's [untouchables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untouchability "Untouchability"), and the [Government of India Act of 1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_India_Act_of_1935 "Government of India Act of 1935"), a blueprint for a [federated state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation "Federation") with extensive provincial autonomy; the Indian National Congress won victories in the resulting elections. The next decade was punctuated by escalating crises: the [British Viceroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hope,_2nd_Marquess_of_Linlithgow "Victor Hope, 2nd Marquess of Linlithgow")'s unilateral declaration of [India's entry into World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_in_World_War_II "India in World War II"), the resignation of the provincial Congress ministries, the party's [1942 push for non-cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quit_India_Movement "Quit India Movement"), the subsequent jailing of its leaders, and an upsurge of [Muslim nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_nationalism_in_South_Asia "Muslim nationalism in South Asia"). After the war, the [Muslim League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-India_Muslim_League "All-India Muslim League")'s sweeping success in the [1946 provincial elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1946_Indian_provincial_elections "1946 Indian provincial elections") was a critical turning point; by securing the Muslim-reserved seats, it established a Muslim mandate for Pakistan that led eventually to the [partition of British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India "Partition of India"). Two new nations, Pakistan and India, came into existence on 14–15 August 1947, accompanied by widespread loss of life and an unprecedented migration.

The [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India "Constitution of India") was adopted in 1950 and declared India to be a [federal republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_republic "Federal republic") governed through a democratic [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system "Parliamentary system"); it guaranteed [freedom of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech"), [freedom of press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_press "Freedom of press"), and [religious pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_pluralism "Religious pluralism"). Amendments enacted from [1951](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India "First Amendment of the Constitution of India") onwards strengthened affirmative action programmes to dismantle [caste system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system_of_India "Caste system of India") inequalities. The [States Reorganisation Act of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_Reorganisation_Act,_1956 "States Reorganisation Act, 1956"), which redrew provincial boundaries along linguistic lines, institutionalised [regional language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_languages_in_India "Regional languages in India") pride and led to the growth of regional language literature and cinema. In 1971, the creation of [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh") altered the regional power balance; the abolition of [princely privileges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_purse_in_India#Abolition "Privy purse in India") removed the last vestiges of India's feudal order. During this time, India's population grew rapidly: it rose from 361 million in 1951, to approximately 1.464 billion in 2025, surpassing China's in 2021. Economic reforms of the 1990s integrated India's economy with the world's, reducing government regulations on domestic and foreign private businesses. By the mid-2010s, India's [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India "Economy of India") was [rapidly growing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate "List of countries by real GDP growth rate"), most notably in [information technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology_in_India "Information technology in India") services. India had a large, consumption-driven middle class and witnessed a communications boom. However, India's economic growth has been accompanied by widening [income gaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_gap "Income gap"), gender inequality, and degradation of the environment. In recent years, India has witnessed a shift toward [majoritarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majoritarianism "Majoritarianism") and [Hindu nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_nationalism "Hindu nationalism"), which has led to [democratic erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding "Democratic backsliding").

## Geography

### Geology

India accounts for the bulk of the Indian subcontinent, lying atop the [Indian tectonic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Plate "Indian Plate"), a part of the [Indo-Australian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Australian_Plate "Indo-Australian Plate"). India's defining geologic processes began approximately 70 million years ago, when the Indian Plate, then part of the southern supercontinent [Gondwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana "Gondwana"), began a north-eastward [drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics") caused by [seafloor spreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafloor_spreading "Seafloor spreading") to its south-west, and later, south and south-east. Simultaneously, the vast [Tethyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean "Tethys Ocean") [oceanic crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_crust "Oceanic crust"), to its northeast, began to [subduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction "Subduction") under the [Eurasian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Plate "Eurasian Plate"). The [Indian continental crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_plate "Indian plate") was obstructed and was sheared horizontally; while its lower crust and mantle slid beneath Eurasia, the upper layer piled up in [sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nappe "Nappe") ahead of the subduction zone. This mountain-building [orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orogeny "Orogeny") created the Himalayas. The middle and stiffer layer continued to push into Tibet, causing [crustal thickening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_tectonics "Thrust tectonics") of the [Tibetan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau "Tibetan Plateau"). Immediately south of the emerging Himalayas, plate movement created a vast crescent-shaped [trough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trough_(geology) "Trough (geology)") that rapidly filled with river-borne sediment and now constitutes the [Indo-Gangetic Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain "Indo-Gangetic Plain"). The original Indian plate makes its first appearance above the sediment in the ancient [Aravalli range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aravalli_range "Aravalli range"), which extends from the [Delhi Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Ridge "Delhi Ridge") in a southwesterly direction. To the west lies the [Thar Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thar_Desert "Thar Desert"), whose eastern spread is checked by the Aravallis.

### Physical geography

The Indian Plate survives as [peninsular India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India"), the oldest and geologically most stable part of the country. It extends as far north as the [Satpura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satpura_Range "Satpura Range") and [Vindhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vindhya_Range "Vindhya Range") ranges in central India. These parallel chains run from the Arabian Sea coast in Gujarat in the west to the coal-rich [Chota Nagpur Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chota_Nagpur_Plateau "Chota Nagpur Plateau") in Jharkhand in the east. To the south, the peninsular landmass, the [Deccan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_Plateau "Deccan Plateau"), is flanked on the west and east by coastal ranges known as [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Ghats "Western Ghats") and [Eastern Ghats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Ghats "Eastern Ghats"); the plateau contains the country's oldest rock formations, some over one billion years old. Geographically, India lies to the north of the equator between 6° 44′ and 35° 30′ north latitude and 68° 7′ and 97° 25′ east longitude.

Major Himalayan-origin rivers that substantially flow through India include the [Ganges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges "Ganges") and the [Brahmaputra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmaputra_River "Brahmaputra River"), both of which drain into the [Bay of Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Bengal "Bay of Bengal"). Important tributaries of the Ganges include the [Yamuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamuna "Yamuna") and the [Kosi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosi_River "Kosi River"). The Kosi's extremely low gradient, caused by long-term silt deposition, leads to severe floods and course changes. Major peninsular rivers, whose steeper gradients prevent extensive flooding, include the [Godavari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godavari_River "Godavari River"), the [Mahanadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahanadi_River "Mahanadi River"), the [Kaveri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaveri_River "Kaveri River"), and the [Krishna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna_River "Krishna River"), which also drain into the Bay of Bengal; and the [Narmada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narmada_River "Narmada River") and the [Tapti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapti_River "Tapti River"), which drain into the [Arabian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Sea "Arabian Sea").

India's coastline measures 7,517 kilometres (4,700 mi) in length; of this distance, 5,423 kilometres (3,400 mi) belong to peninsular India and 2,094 kilometres (1,300 mi) to the Andaman, Nicobar, and Lakshadweep island chains. According to the Indian naval hydrographic charts, the [mainland coastline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_India "Coastal India") consists of 43% sandy beaches, 11% rocky shores including cliffs, and 46% [mudflats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudflat "Mudflat") or marshy shores. Coastal features include the marshy [Rann of Kutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rann_of_Kutch "Rann of Kutch") of western India and the alluvial [Sundarbans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarbans "Sundarbans") delta of eastern India, the latter shared with Bangladesh. India has two [archipelagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago "Archipelago"): the [Lakshadweep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshadweep "Lakshadweep") [coral atolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoll "Atoll") off the south-western coast, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, a volcanic chain in the [Andaman Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Sea "Andaman Sea").

### Climate

In [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification "Köppen climate classification") the regions of India are: [Dry-winter, humid sub-tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#Cwa:_Dry-winter_humid_subtropical_climate "Köppen climate classification") (CWa, largely the river plains of the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Punjab rivers); [Tropical savanna with dry winters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#Aw:_Tropical_savanna_climate_with_dry_winters "Köppen climate classification") (Aw, large parts of peninsular India except the [Deccan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_Plateau "Deccan Plateau") and the [Western Ghats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Ghats "Western Ghats")); [Hot semi-arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#BSh:_Hot_semi-arid "Köppen climate classification") (BSh, Deccan plateau, parts of Gujarat, eastern Rajasthan, Punjab, and Western Uttar Pradesh); [Hot Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#BWh:_Hot_deserts "Köppen climate classification") (BWh, northern Gujarat and western Rajasthan); [Tropical Monsoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#Am:_Tropical_monsoon_climate "Köppen climate classification") (Am, Western Ghats), [Dry winter sub-topical highland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#Cwb:_Dry-winter_subtropical_highland_climate "Köppen climate classification") (CWb, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, northern Bengal and upper [northeast India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_India "Northeast India")), [Cold desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#BWk:_Cold_deserts "Köppen climate classification") (BWk, Eastern Ladakh), [Tropical rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#Af:_Tropical_rainforest_climate "Köppen climate classification") (Af, [Sundarbans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarbans_Biosphere_Reserve "Sundarbans Biosphere Reserve"), [Andaman and Nicobar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_and_Nicobar_Islands "Andaman and Nicobar Islands"); [Warm summer hemiboreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#Dfb/Dwb/Dsb:_Warm_summer_humid_continental/hemiboreal_climates "Köppen climate classification") (Dsb, upper Himachal Pradesh and Kashmir below the Himalayas), and [Ice cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification#EF:_Ice_cap_climate "Köppen climate classification") in the [Western Himalayas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Himalayas "Western Himalayas").

Monsoon weather systems play a significant role in India's climate. In turn, the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau play an important role in creating the South Asian monsoon, which accounts for 75 to 80 per cent of India's annual rain. In winter, the Tibetan Plateau (average altitude 4500 m) acts like a tower of ice and splits the [westerlies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerlies "Westerlies"), both the low-level and, by friction, the high-altitude [jet streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream "Jet stream"). The southern branch rounds the Himalayas. Just beyond, as it slows down and creates a [convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_zone "Convergence zone"), or backup, the air [sinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsidence_(atmosphere) "Subsidence (atmosphere)"), creating dry, northeasterly surface winds over India. This maintains dry, cool, Indian winters. After these winds slow down, sub-tropical westerlies guide extratropical [Western disturbances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_disturbance "Western disturbance") from the [Mediterranean Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Region "Mediterranean Region") and the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") across northwestern India, bringing rain for winter crops and replenishing the glaciers in the [Karakorams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karakoram "Karakoram").

By May, the sun bakes the Tibetan Plateau. Rising warm air generates a high-altitude [anticyclone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-pressure_area "High-pressure area"). This creates the [Tropical Easterly Jet Stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_Easterly_Jet "Tropical Easterly Jet") over southern India, which draws moisture-laden southwesterly surface winds off the Indian Ocean. The Western Ghats intercept these winds, which release their moisture as [orographic rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_types#Orographic "Precipitation types"), leaving a large swath of peninsular India in the mountains' [rain shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_shadow "Rain shadow"). India’s interior plains rely on [monsoon depressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsoon_trough#Monsoon_depressions "Monsoon trough") for their rain. These low-pressure storm systems form over the Bay of Bengal and are steered clockwise and inland by the anticyclonic jet stream. They cause summer rainfall across India from June to September. However, rainfall fluctuates in cycles, alternating between active phases characterised by heavy rain and dry breaks.

### Biodiversity

India is a [megadiverse country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megadiverse_country "Megadiverse country"), a term employed for 17 countries that host high [biological diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_diversity "Biological diversity") and contain many species [indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_(ecology) "Indigenous (ecology)"), or [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic "Endemic"), to them. India is the [habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat "Habitat") for 8.6% of all [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal "Mammal"), 13.7% of [bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird "Bird") species, 7.9% of [reptile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptile "Reptile") species, 6% of [amphibian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibian "Amphibian") species, 12.2% of [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish "Fish") species, and 6.0% of all [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") species. Fully a third of Indian plant species are endemic. India also overlaps four of the world's 34 [biodiversity hotspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_hotspot "Biodiversity hotspot"), or regions that display significant habitat loss in the presence of high endemism.

India's densest forests, such as the [tropical moist forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_and_subtropical_moist_broadleaf_forest "Tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forest") of the [Andaman Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Islands "Andaman Islands"), the [Western Ghats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Ghats "Western Ghats"), and [Northeast India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_India "Northeast India"), occupy about 3% of its land area. *Moderately dense forest*, whose canopy density is between 40% and 70%, occupies 9.39% of India's land area. It predominates in the [temperate coniferous forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_coniferous_forest "Temperate coniferous forest") of the [Himalayas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalaya "Himalaya"), the moist deciduous *[sal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shorea_robusta "Shorea robusta")* forest of eastern India, and the dry deciduous [teak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teak "Teak") forest of central and southern India. India has two natural zones of [thorn forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deserts_and_xeric_shrublands "Deserts and xeric shrublands"), one in the [Deccan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_Plateau "Deccan Plateau"), immediately east of the Western Ghats, and the other in the western part of the Indo-Gangetic plain, now turned into rich agricultural land by irrigation, its features no longer visible. Among the Indian subcontinent's notable indigenous trees are the [astringent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astringent "Astringent") *[Azadirachta indica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azadirachta_indica "Azadirachta indica")*, or *neem*, which is widely used in rural Indian [herbal medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbal_medicine "Herbal medicine"), and the luxuriant *[Ficus religiosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ficus_religiosa "Ficus religiosa")*, or *peepul*, which is displayed on the ancient seals of [Mohenjo-daro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohenjo-daro "Mohenjo-daro"), and under which [the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha") is recorded in the [Pali canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81li_Canon "Pāli Canon") to have sought enlightenment.

Many Indian species have descended from those of [Gondwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana "Gondwana"), the southern [supercontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercontinent "Supercontinent") from which India separated more than 100 million years ago. India's subsequent collision with Eurasia set off a mass exchange of species. However, [volcanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_Traps "Deccan Traps") and [climatic changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change "Climate variability and change") later caused the extinction of many endemic Indian forms. Still later, mammals entered India from Asia through two [zoogeographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoogeography "Zoogeography") passes flanking the Himalayas. This lowered endemism among India's mammals, which stands at 12.6%, contrasting with 45.8% among reptiles and 55.8% among amphibians. Among endemics are the vulnerable [hooded leaf monkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilgiri_Langur "Nilgiri Langur") and the threatened [Beddome's toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duttaphrynus_beddomii "Duttaphrynus beddomii") of the Western Ghats.

According to the [IUCN Red List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN_Red_List "IUCN Red List"), India contains 1,021 threatened animal species—99 [mammal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal "Mammal"), 82 [bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird "Bird"), 105 [reptile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptile "Reptile"), 139 [amphibian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibian "Amphibian"), 332 [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish "Fish"), 10 [mollusk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusk "Mollusk"), and 254 other [invertebrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertebrate "Invertebrate")—727 threatened plant species, and 4 threatened [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungi "Fungi") species. Among them are the endangered [Bengal tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_tiger "Bengal tiger") and the [Ganges river dolphin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_river_dolphin "South Asian river dolphin"). [Critically endangered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critically_endangered "Critically endangered") species include the [gharial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gharial "Gharial"), a [crocodilian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crocodilian "Crocodilian"); the [great Indian bustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Indian_bustard "Great Indian bustard"); and the [Indian white-rumped vulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-rumped_vulture "White-rumped vulture"), which has become nearly extinct by having ingested the carrion of [diclofenac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diclofenac "Diclofenac")-treated cattle. Before they were extensively used for agriculture and cleared for human settlement, the thorn forests of Punjab were mingled at intervals with open grasslands that were grazed by large herds of [blackbuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbuck_Conservation_Area "Blackbuck Conservation Area") preyed on by the [Asiatic cheetah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asiatic_cheetah "Asiatic cheetah"); the blackbuck, no longer extant in Punjab, is now severely endangered in India, and the cheetah is extinct. The pervasive and ecologically devastating human encroachment of recent decades has critically endangered Indian wildlife. In response, the system of [national parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parks_of_India "National parks of India") and [protected areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_areas_of_India "Protected areas of India"), first established in 1935, was expanded substantially. In 1972, India enacted the [Wildlife Protection Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_Protection_Act_of_1972 "Wildlife Protection Act of 1972") and [Project Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Tiger "Project Tiger") to safeguard crucial wilderness; the Forest Conservation Act was enacted in 1980 and amendments added in 1988. India hosts [more than five hundred wildlife sanctuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_sanctuaries_of_India "Wildlife sanctuaries of India") and [eighteen biosphere reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere_reserves_of_India "Biosphere reserves of India"), four of which are part of the [World Network of Biosphere Reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Network_of_Biosphere_Reserves "World Network of Biosphere Reserves"); its [eighty-nine wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Ramsar_sites_in_India "List of Ramsar sites in India") are registered under the [Ramsar Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsar_Convention "Ramsar Convention").

## Government and politics

**Constitution:** The [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India "Constitution of India") was drafted by the [Constituent Assembly of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituent_Assembly_of_India "Constituent Assembly of India") between 1946 and 1949, despite the political disruptions of the [Partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India "Partition of India"). The [Government of India Act 1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_India_Act_1935 "Government of India Act 1935") was used as a model and framework. Long passages from the Act are included. The Indian Constitution merges the opposing principles of [federalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism "Federalism"), which disperses political authority, and [parliamentarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentarianism "Parliamentarianism"), which centralises legislative power. The federal structure features a strong central government that controls defence, foreign affairs, railways, ports, and currency. The President, the constitutional head of state, has reserve powers for taking over the administration of a state. The Constitution also includes features not found in the 1935 Act. The definition of fundamental rights is based on the [Constitution of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States "Constitution of the United States"), and the constitutional directives, or goals of endeavour, are based on the [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland "Constitution of Ireland").
Among two [Constitutional institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_institution "Constitutional institution") are the [Supreme Court of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_India "Supreme Court of India") and the [Election Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Commission_of_India "Election Commission of India"). The Constitution also recommends the *[panchayat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchayati_raj_in_India "Panchayati raj in India")* (village committees). [Untouchability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untouchability "Untouchability") is illegal (Article 17) and caste distinctions are derecognised (Articles 15(2) and 16(2)). On 26 January 1950, the promulgation of the Indian Constitution transformed India into a republic within the [Commonwealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations "Commonwealth of Nations").

**Citizenship:** During Constituent Assembly debates, India's founders strongly preferred *[jus soli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jus_soli "Jus soli")*, or birthright citizenship. They viewed birth on the soil as a deeply democratic principle, contrasting it sharply with *[jus sanguinis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jus_sanguinis "Jus sanguinis")*—descent-based citizenship—which they rejected as racial and outdated. While India's Constitution originally carved out exceptions for those who migrated to India after [Partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India "Partition of India"), the [Citizenship Act of 1955](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_nationality_law "Indian nationality law") formally established birthplace as the foundation of Indian citizenship.

This foundation began to shift in the 1980s, when [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bongal_Kheda "Bongal Kheda") and [political unrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assam_Movement "Assam Movement") in the [northeastern state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_India "Northeast India") of [Assam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assam "Assam") targeted [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_language "Bengali language")-speaking migrants. The resulting legislative amendments steadily eroded *jus soli*. India's [new citizenship laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship_(Amendment)_Act,_2019 "Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019") increasingly favour bloodline over birthplace, erecting steep legal obstacles for new or marginalised groups seeking to achieve [substantive equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substantive_equality "Substantive equality").

**Houses of Parliament:** The [Lok Sabha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lok_Sabha "Lok Sabha"), India’s lower house of parliament, consists of 543 members directly elected by [universal suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_suffrage "Universal suffrage"), with specific seats reserved to guarantee fair representation for [Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Castes_and_Scheduled_Tribes "Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes"). However, its legislative productivity has steadily waned over the decades; the average number of annual working days dropped from 121 in the 1950s to just 68 between 2000 and 2023, a decline compounded by rising disruptive protests during sessions.

The [Rajya Sabha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajya_Sabha "Rajya Sabha"), India’s upper house, serves as a [federal chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation "Federation") whose members are chosen by [state legislatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidhan_Sabha "Vidhan Sabha") under a system that weights representation by population while ensuring a baseline for smaller regions. Yet, historical data reveals that most members serve only a single term. This high turnover suggests that party leadership often uses these federal seats as instruments of [political patronage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patronage#Politics "Patronage") to reward loyalty rather than build long-term legislative expertise.

**Cabinet:** Officially known in India as the [Union Council of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Council_of_Ministers "Union Council of Ministers"), the [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_(government) "Cabinet (government)") is the principal institution of governance. It is traditionally defined by three principles: collective responsibility, political homogeneity, and a prime minister who acts as [first among equals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primus_inter_pares "Primus inter pares"). [Jawaharlal Nehru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jawaharlal_Nehru "Jawaharlal Nehru")'s early administrations, from 1946 until the mid-1950s, prominently featured confident, independent-minded ministers. However, after the [1957 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1957_elections_in_India "1957 elections in India"), the Congress Party's overwhelming mandate shifted governance toward a centralised, prime-ministerial model.

After Nehru's death in 1964, during [Lal Bahadur Shastri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lal_Bahadur_Shastri "Lal Bahadur Shastri")'s brief tenure, there was a partial swing back to cabinet-based governance. Yet by 1969, under [Indira Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi "Indira Gandhi"), the central government operated largely in the prime minister's shadow. This extreme centralisation ultimately led to decisions like [the Emergency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emergency_(India) "The Emergency (India)")—a suspension of India's civil liberties from 1975 to 1977. In contrast, the subsequent [Janata Party administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Morarji_Desai "Premiership of Morarji Desai") of [Morarji Desai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morarji_Desai "Morarji Desai") (1977–1979) swung back toward a cabinet-driven model, though it lacked ideological cohesion. Following Indira Gandhi's assassination in 1984, [Rajiv Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajiv_Gandhi "Rajiv Gandhi")'s administration was marked by an overwhelming legislative majority that allowed the prime minister to reshuffle cabinet portfolios at will.

Between 1989 and 2014, India experienced eleven coalition cabinets where the ideal of political homogeneity was severely tested by competing party demands. During this era, alliance partners bargained for specific portfolios and dictated their own appointments, significantly weakening the prime minister's authority. Only three prime ministers served a full five-year term or more during this volatile period: [P.V. Narasimha Rao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P.V._Narasimha_Rao "P.V. Narasimha Rao") (1991–1996), [Atal Bihari Vajpayee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atal_Bihari_Vajpayee "Atal Bihari Vajpayee") (1999–2004), and [Manmohan Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manmohan_Singh "Manmohan Singh") (2004–2014). More recently, the [National Democratic Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Democratic_Alliance "National Democratic Alliance") majorities in 2014 and 2019 have once again consolidated substantial authority in the hands of the prime minister, [Narendra Modi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narendra_Modi "Narendra Modi").

**Political Parties:** Since the mid-1980s—marking the second half of India's postcolonial history—the [Bharatiya Janata Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharatiya_Janata_Party "Bharatiya Janata Party"), or BJP, has grown from a marginal party into a political giant. Scholars interpret this rapid expansion as a form of [hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemony "Hegemony") where [Hindu nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_nationalism "Hindu nationalism") has largely sidelined traditional [secularism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism "Secularism") in India. Along with its ideological draw, the party's deep pockets and heavy hand on the [mainstream media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media_in_India "Mass media in India") and watchdogs—like the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_India "Supreme Court of India") and the [Election Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Commission_of_India "Election Commission of India")—have hemmed in opposition parties. This political hold relies largely on Narendra Modi's top-down leadership and his broad popularity with Indian voters.

Unlike the BJP, the Congress Party relied on a [middle-of-the-road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrism "Centrism") platform and a loose organisation, but it has struggled to fit a changing, [right-leaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-right_politics "Centre-right politics") [voter base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_base "Political base"). The Congress Party shifts between compromising its beliefs and directly attacking [Hindutva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindutva "Hindutva"); this has blocked its path to winning back its traditional [civic nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civic_nationalism "Civic nationalism"). Fearing voter [backlash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backlash_(sociology) "Backlash (sociology)"), Congress leaders have largely avoided a united stand for secularism and [social justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_justice "Social justice").

From the late 1980s to 2014, local or [regional parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_India#State_parties "List of political parties in India") wielded heavy clout nationwide by anchoring [coalition governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_government "Coalition government") and holding the keys to making or breaking central rule. During this period of fractured politics, breakaway groups from national parties like Congress and the Janata Dal ruled most Indian states. While the ruling party still reaches out to local groups to pass laws in the upper house, these homegrown parties no longer hold the power to shake or topple the central government.

**Caste politics:** After independence, the power of the vote gradually shifted local power, beginning with the rise of [intermediate agrarian castes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Other_Backward_Class "Other Backward Class") that displaced traditional [forward castes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_caste "Forward caste") across southern and western India. In the late 1960s and 1970s, this social upheaval spread northward, causing caste-driven rivalries and political divides in states like Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. The implementation of the [Mandal Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandal_Commission "Mandal Commission") recommendations in 1990 led to this movement facing an increasingly assertive and independent [Dalit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalit "Dalit") political movement.

**Women in politics:** Women's representation in India's Lok Sabha and state assemblies remains stubbornly low, leaving the nation well behind the global average. Political parties act as powerful gatekeepers; they rarely select female candidates, ensuring that women have never made up more than ten per cent of the ballot in any national election. While women tend to win at higher rates when actually nominated, their presence in the lower house has plateaued at just 14 per cent, with state-level numbers sinking even lower.

**Muslim representation:** The 1947 Partition of the subcontinent shattered the geographic and political weight of Indian Muslims. Overnight, they shifted from a powerful political bloc with clear majorities in two vast regions—the Punjab and Bengal—to a scattered minority. Because they are so widely dispersed, Muslims are easily outnumbered in almost every state legislature. While they make up over 14 per cent of India's population, their presence in Parliament remains tokenised. In the 2024 general election, they won a near-historic low of 4.4 per cent, or 24 seats in the Lok Sabha This is not a recent shift; since Independence, Muslim representation has consistently plateaued near the 5 per cent mark, locking the community into a permanent state of political marginalisation.

**State Coercion and Political Violence:** While India remains a democratic society, successive governments have relied on [state coercion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coercion#Political_theory "Coercion"), pushing through unilateral policies with minimal public input. This heavy-handedness is backed by a politicised and [unaccountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accountability "Accountability") police force that routinely uses [preventive detention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_detention "Preventive detention") laws to silence critics of the ruling regime. Political violence in India ranges from [localised lynchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching#India "Lynching") and armed campaign clashes to deep-seated borderland insurgencies. Among these, only the conflicts in [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir_conflict "Kashmir conflict") and the [Northeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_insurgency_in_Northeast_India "Timeline of insurgency in Northeast India") have historically commanded sustained national attention. Still, they remain politically isolated compared to the explosive violence that erupts within state elections. In states like Kerala and West Bengal, election campaigns are openly militarised; mainstream parties run permanent enforcement wings and [goon networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goon_squad "Goon squad") as standard parts of their daily party machines. Elsewhere, especially in the [Hindi Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi_Belt "Hindi Belt"), politicians deliberately trigger religious riots in the months leading up to a vote.

In recent years, India has experienced [democratic erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding "Democratic backsliding"), in which a combination of institutional passivity, negligence, and political meddling has crippled the ability of the Supreme Court, the Election Commission, and other federal agencies to restrain an executive branch and a ruling party partial to [majoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majoritarianism "Majoritarianism") [Hindu nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_nationalism "Hindu nationalism").

### Administrative divisions

India is a federal union comprising 28 [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_union_territories_of_India "States and union territories of India") and 8 [union territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_territory "Union territory"). All states, as well as the union territories of [Jammu and Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(union_territory) "Jammu and Kashmir (union territory)"), [Puducherry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puducherry_(union_territory) "Puducherry (union territory)") and the [National Capital Territory of Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi "Delhi"), have elected legislatures and governments following the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system "Westminster system"). The remaining five union territories are directly ruled by the central government through appointed administrators. In 1956, under the [States Reorganisation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_Reorganisation_Act,_1956 "States Reorganisation Act, 1956"), states were reorganised on a linguistic basis. Over a quarter of a million local government bodies operate at the city, town, district, block, and village levels.

### Foreign and military policy

India’s foreign policy history is divided into four distinct periods, the first beginning with [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement "Non-Aligned Movement") at independence and ending with the 1962 Sino-Indian border war. The second phase, lasting until the end of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), maintained traditional non-aligned rhetoric but shifted toward a realistic approach through a strategic and military alignment with the Soviet Union. The collapse of the Soviet bloc in 1991 began a third period, forcing policymakers to re-evaluate the nation's external strategy in a newly unipolar world. The fourth and current phase began in 2014 under Prime Minister Narendra Modi, who has deliberately sought to pivot away from his predecessors' intellectual legacy. Despite a lack of prior diplomatic background, the current leadership has pursued a highly active and pragmatic foreign policy, particularly in [engaging global powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrilateral_Security_Dialogue "Quadrilateral Security Dialogue") and the [Middle Eastern countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Eastern_foreign_policy_of_the_Narendra_Modi_government "Middle Eastern foreign policy of the Narendra Modi government"), including Israel. This realism initially extended to China, where early diplomatic outreach persisted despite [localised border incursions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020%E2%80%932021_China%E2%80%93India_skirmishes "2020–2021 China–India skirmishes"). However, a deadly [military clash in the Galwan Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020%E2%80%932021_China%E2%80%93India_skirmishes#Galwan_Valley_clash "2020–2021 China–India skirmishes") in 2020 forced India to abandon a stable partnership with China. The current administration has also shown little interest in the [Non-Aligned Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement "Non-Aligned Movement"), breaking with past conventions by skipping multiple summits. Nevertheless, strategic autonomy remains central to national doctrine, as shown by the refusal to condemn the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present) "Russo-Ukrainian war (2022–present)").

India remains acutely sensitive to its land borders, viewing both Pakistan and China as persistent threats that utilise small-scale territorial land grabs to alter the status quo. To counter a simultaneous two-front war, India dedicates most of its [defence resources and personnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_India "Armed Forces of India") to [land defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_warfare "Land warfare"). This prioritisation limits the funding available for the [Indian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Navy "Indian Navy") to [project maritime power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_projection "Power projection") across the broader [Indo-Pacific region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Pacific_region "Indo-Pacific region"). India's [nuclear warhead inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction "India and weapons of mass destruction") remains officially classified, but its steady accumulation of [fissile material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile_material "Fissile material") gives it the capacity to match the arsenal sizes of major nuclear powers by the end of the 2020s.

## Economy

After independence in 1947, India shifted from the colonial economic model to a focus on [industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialisation "Industrialisation") and [poverty reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_reduction "Poverty reduction"). This transition brought a large expansion of government authority. [Government spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_spending "Government spending") as a share of [gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product "Gross domestic product") (GDP) increased from 3 to 5 per cent in 1931 to 22 per cent by 1981. [Public administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_administration "Public administration") and [state-owned enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State-owned_enterprise "State-owned enterprise") became major employers. To manage this [market intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_intervention "Market intervention"), India developed—during the 1950s—a comprehensive, [federally managed statistical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Statistics_Office_(India) "Central Statistics Office (India)") to conduct large-scale [sample surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_(statistics) "Sampling (statistics)") for monitoring and assessing [living standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_of_living "Standard of living") or effects of interventions. The Indian state utilised [fiscal policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_policy "Fiscal policy") and [international assistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aid "Aid") to fund [capital-intensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_intensity "Capital intensity") industrialisation and [subsidise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsidy "Subsidy") basic [goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good "Good") such as [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food "Food"), [fertilisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertiliser "Fertiliser"), and [electric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_industry "Electric power industry"). However, by the 1970s, this heavy [public spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_spending "Government spending") became unsustainable. Domestic [economic growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_growth "Economic growth") slowed, leading to a [balance-of-payments crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_crisis "Currency crisis"), whereas government spending flattened around 28–30 per cent of GDP. [Government-led planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_Commission_(India) "Planning Commission (India)") yielded mixed results. Many of India’s big economic changes were not planned by [government bureaucrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucrat "Bureaucrat"). Thus, the [1970s Green Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution_in_India "Green Revolution in India"), mass [labour migration to the Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_diaspora_in_the_Middle_East "Indian diaspora in the Middle East"), and a [garment-industry boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_industry_in_India "Textile industry in India") all emerged from global market forces and [non-state actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-state_actor "Non-state actor"); the government simply adapted to them after they were underway.

A major shift occurred during the 1990s as India rejoined the global economy. The Indian government reduced [import tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Import_tariff "Import tariff") and enacted [pro-market reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_liberalization "Economic liberalization"). This phase was initially supported by [worker remittances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remittance "Remittance") from abroad and a sharp rise in exports of [labour-intensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_intensity "Labour intensity") goods like [textiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile "Textile"). Subsequently [service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector "Tertiary sector") and [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software "Software") exports accelerated as global [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_in_India "Telecommunications in India") costs fell. Overall, private business investment rose significantly as the Indian government shifted its economic priorities. By the mid-2010s, India's [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India "Economy of India") was [rapidly growing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate "List of countries by real GDP growth rate"). It led to gains in [poverty reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_reduction "Poverty reduction"), increased [national tax collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Revenue_Service "Indian Revenue Service"), and a surge in private investment. As the Indian government methodically withdrew from direct investment in industry, it simultaneously increased public spending on vital  [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_India "Transport in India") projects to keep pace with accelerating [urbanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanisation_in_India "Urbanisation in India"). This economic opening also [diversified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_diversity "Economic diversity") the domestic economy. New industries emerged, most notably in [information technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology_in_India "Information technology in India") services; the national [judicial infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary_of_India "Judiciary of India") slowly adapted. However, growth was accompanied by widening [income gaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_gap "Income gap"), with some data showing only limited gains in overall [welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare "Welfare") and everyday [quality of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_life "Quality of life"). Moreover, rapid development has exerted accelerated pressure on the environment. In response, activist political movements emerged to resist [environmental degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_India "Environmental issues in India").

### Industries

India is a worldwide centre for [information technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology "Information technology") and [business services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_process_management "Business process management"), which add 7.5% to GDP and over 35% to [services sold abroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_export "Service export"). This sector is sustained by a large English-speaking skilled labour force, with revenues surpassing $300 billion in Fiscal Year 2026. Although the industry once relied on basic [call centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_centre "Call centre") and simple software fixes, it has shifted toward [cloud computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing "Cloud computing"), [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), and international [tech hubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tech_hub "Tech hub"). Bangalore has grown from a cheap offshore site into a top centre for corporate research, making it one of the largest technology hubs outside Silicon Valley.

India's automotive sector comprises 7.1% of its economy. It produces 28 million vehicles every year of which the vast majority are two-wheelers (77%) followed by passenger cars (18%). In 2022, 8 per cent of Indian households owned a car, 55 per cent owned a bicycle, and 54 per cent a motor cycle or scooter. In 2023, India became the third-largest automobile market in the world, with buyers, many of whom are in their 30s, increasingly preferring SUVs.

India's pharmaceutical sector relies on a vast network of production facilities focused on low-cost manufacturing. Thousands of individual factories and domestic firms comprise this sector and together make India the main worldwide source of generic medications and vaccines by volume. However, the sector faces supply line vulnerabilities, especially a reliance on China for over 70% of its raw [active pharmaceutical ingredients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_ingredient "Active ingredient") (APIs). Although India faces international quality-control audits in its factories, adverse outcomes have dropped from 12 per cent in 2015 to 8 per cent in 2025, and 80% of those were in small firms not the top 20 companies.

India's total installed electricity capacity exceeds 532 GW, with non-fossil and renewable energy sources contributing more than 283.5 GW, or roughly 53%.
[The country's usage of coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_India "Coal in India") is a major cause of [India's greenhouse gas emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_India#Greenhouse_gas_emissions "Climate change in India"), but [its renewable energy capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_India "Renewable energy in India") is growing. India emits about 7% of global greenhouse gas emissions. This equates to about 2.5 tonnes of [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") per person per year, which is half the world average. Increasing [access to electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification "Electrification") and [clean cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_cooking "Clean cooking") with [liquefied petroleum gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquefied_petroleum_gas "Liquefied petroleum gas") have been priorities for energy in India.

## Demographics

India's population was 361 million in 1951. It grew to approximately 1.464 billion in 2025, surpassing China's population in 2021. 1,210,193,422 residents were reported in the [2011 provisional census report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Census_of_India "2011 Census of India"). The median age was 28.7 in 2020. Medical advances made in the last 50 years as well as increased agricultural productivity brought about by the "[Green Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution_in_India "Green Revolution in India")" have caused India's population to grow rapidly, though India's decennial rates of growth are decreasing: its population grew by 17.64% from 2001 to 2011, compared to 21.54% growth in the previous decade (1991–2001). The first post-colonial census, conducted in 1951, counted 361 million people. The [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy "Life expectancy") at birth has increased from 49.7 years in 1970–1975 to 72.0 years in 2023. The under-five [mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality "Infant mortality") rate for the country was 113 per 1,000 live births in 1994, and had declined to 41.1 per 1,000 live births by 2018.

The 2011 census reported a human sex ratio of 940 females per 1,000 males. [Female infanticide in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Female_infanticide_in_India "Female infanticide in India"), and lately [female foeticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Female_foeticide_in_India "Female foeticide in India"), have created lopsided gender ratios; the number of [missing women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missing_women "Missing women") in the country quadrupled from 15 million to 63 million during the period 1964–2014, faster than the population growth during the same period. According to an Indian government study, an additional 21 million girls are unwanted and do not receive adequate care. Despite a government ban on sex-selective foeticide, the practice persists.

Migration from rural to urban areas has been an important dynamic in India's recent history. The number of people living in urban areas grew by 31.2% between 1991 and 2001. In 2001, over 70% lived in rural areas. The level of urbanisation increased further from 27.81% in the 2001 census to 31.16% in the 2011 census. The overall population growth rate slowed because rural growth declined sharply after 1991. In the 2011 census, there were 53 [million-plus urban agglomerations in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_million-plus_urban_agglomerations_in_India "List of million-plus urban agglomerations in India"), among them [Mumbai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumbai "Mumbai"), [Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi "Delhi"), [Kolkata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolkata "Kolkata"), [Chennai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chennai "Chennai"), [Bangalore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangalore "Bangalore"), [Hyderabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyderabad "Hyderabad") and [Ahmedabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmedabad "Ahmedabad"), in decreasing order by population.

The [Constituent Assembly of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituent_Assembly_of_India "Constituent Assembly of India") chose the official language of India's federal government in September 1949 after three years of debate between two opposing camps. [Hindi language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi_language "Hindi language") protagonists wanted Hindi in the [Devanagari script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari_script "Devanagari script") to be the sole "national language" of India whereas delegates from South India preferred English to have a place in the Constitution. The compromise reached declared (i) Hindi to be the "official language" of India's federal government; (ii) English to be an associate official language for 15 years during which Hindi's formal lexicon would be developed; and (iii) the international form of [Hindu–Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system "Hindu–Arabic numeral system") to be the official numerals. The compromise resolution became articles 343–351 of India's constitution, which came into effect on 26 January 1950. In 1965, after bitter opposition from [South India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India") to Hindi becoming the sole official language, a compromise was reached whereby English would continue to be an "associate official language" indefinitely.

The [Eighth Schedule of India's Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighth_Schedule_to_the_Constitution_of_India "Eighth Schedule to the Constitution of India") also recognises 22 languages, including Hindi but not English, which the government must develop. These are sometimes called "scheduled languages". This list includes major regional languages, but also others—such as [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"), which no longer has [first language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_language "First language") speakers in India, and [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu "Urdu"), which is not region-specific—because of their value to India's cultural heritage. ### Religion

Hinduism is the majority faith followed by nearly 80% of the population; Islam is the largest minority at over 14%. India also has populations of [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India "Christianity in India") and [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhs "Sikhs") in the tens of millions, [Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhists "Buddhists") and [Jains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jains "Jains") fewer than 10 million, and historical populations of [Zoroastrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsis "Parsis") in the tens of thousands and [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") less than ten thousand.

As of the 2011 census, India's last census, Hindus comprised a majority in 28 of India’s 35 states and union territories; this included the four most populous states: Uttar Pradesh (total population: 200 million; Hindu population: 80% of the state's total), Maharashtra (112 million, 80%), Bihar (104 million, 83%) and West Bengal (91 million, 71%). Muslims are in the majority in [Lakshadweep Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshadweep "Lakshadweep") (60,000, 97%) and [Jammu and Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(state) "Jammu and Kashmir (state)") (8.6 million, 68%). Indian Christians make up about 2.3% of India's population. [Saint Thomas Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Thomas_Christians "Saint Thomas Christians") number around 8.7 million, whereas [Indian Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism_in_India "Catholicism in India") number 11.8 million. Nearly 33% of [Adivasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adivasi "Adivasi") (or [Scheduled Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Tribe "Scheduled Tribe") of India's Constitution) are Christian. They are concentrated in three hill states of the northeast, [Mizoram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizoram "Mizoram"), [Meghalaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meghalaya "Meghalaya"), and [Nagaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagaland "Nagaland"), where they constitute between 70% and 90% of the population. As per the 2011 census, Sikhs comprised less than 2% of India's population, though they were a majority in Punjab state, where more than 87% of Sikhs were farmers. Indian Buddhists number 8.45 million, with 77%, or 6.5 million, living in the state of Maharashtra. Indian Jains number 4.45 million, and Zoroastrians (Parsis), 60,000. As of 2019, there were 3,000 [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") in India, down from 28,000 in 1950. Other religions in India (and population in the 2011 census) are [Sarnaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnaism "Sarnaism") (4.96 million) and [Gondi-Koyapunem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondi_people#Koyapunem "Gondi people") (1.03 million) and [Sari dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santal_people#Religion "Santal people") (510,000).

In 1951—India's first postcolonial census—the percentage breakdown of India's religions was: Hindu (84.1%), Muslims (9.8%), Christians (2.3%), Sikhs (1.9%), Buddhists (0.7%) and Jains (0.5%); sixty years later, in India's last census (2011), the percentages were: Hindus (79.8%), Muslims (14.2%), Christians (2.3%), Sikhs (1.7%), Buddhists (0.7%) and Jains (0.4%) In absolute numbers, during the period 1951–2011, India's religious groups grew in the following manner: Hindus (304 million to 966 million), Muslims (35 million to 172 million), Christians (8.3 million to 28 million), Sikhs (6.82 million to 20.83 million), Buddhists (2.67 million to 8.44 million) and Jains (1.66 million to 4.45 million). In the decade 1951–1961, the population growth by religions was: Hindus (20.7%), Muslims (32.7%), Christians (29%), Sikhs (10.3%), Buddhists (5.9%), Jains (3.7%); in the decade 2001–2011, the growth was: Hindus (16.7%), Muslims (24.7%), Christians (15.7%), Sikhs (16.1%), Buddhists (4.8%), and Jains (2.2%). All religions have registered declining growth rates.

[Birth rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_rate "Birth rate"), [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth "Population growth"), and access to education influence how communities mobilise politically to preserve their [places of worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_of_worship "Place of worship"), to manage [charitable trusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charitable_trust "Charitable trust"), and to protect their [family laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_law "Family law"). Differences in states' laws, especially those prohibiting [religious conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_conversion "Religious conversion")—usually from Hinduism to other faiths—colour the daily lives of minority communities. Providing [constitutional protections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutionalism "Constitutionalism") for [minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities "Minorities") in the face of [majoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majoritarian "Majoritarian") pressure remains a fraught challenge. India is a [secular state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_state "Secular state") and guarantees the [fundamental right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_right "Fundamental right") to [freedom of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion "Freedom of religion") ("Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship").

### Education

In 1911, there were three primary schools for every ten villages in [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India "British India"). Early literacy was low: It was 5% in 1891 (9% for men and 1% for women) and 7% in 1921 (12% for men and 2% for women). Historically, local diversity limited literacy growth in India because caste and religious fragmentation reduced private spending. Literacy rates rose to 18% in 1951 (27% for men and 9% for women) and 41% in 1981 (53% for men and 29% for women). By 2011, literacy reached 74.04% (82.14% for men and 65.46% for women). They ranged from Kerala's 93.91% to Bihar's 63.82%. Meanwhile, rural gains reached twice the level of urban, narrowing the rural-urban gap from 21.2 percentage points in 2001 to 16.1 in 2011.

The central, state, and local governments manage education in India. Every child between the ages of 6 and 14 has a right to education, which is free and compulsory. Traditionally, children had spent eight years in primary school before moving on to high school. In 2020, the [National Education Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Education_Policy_2020 "National Education Policy 2020") changed this division to one based on developmental stages: a foundational stage: five years of pre-school and [standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_stage "Educational stage") 1–2, for ages 3–8; a preparatory stage: standards 3–5; a middle stage: standards 6–8; and a secondary stage: standards 9–12. Critics argue that the new plan, although progressive, suffers from public underfunding and inadequate school infrastructure.

The first universities in India were founded in Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras in 1857, the last year of [East India Company rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_rule_in_India "Company rule in India"). India now has one of the world's largest post-secondary education systems. Recent college reforms envisage offering more flexible degrees, creating online learning tools, and inviting foreign universities to set up campuses in India. However, large gaps remain between rural and urban areas. School quality, campus building conditions, and computer access vary substantially based on a student's gender and family's wealth. Poor job prospects for graduates and uneven rule enforcement across different states have faced criticism from researchers and the public alike.

### Health

Since its 1947 independence, India has significantly reduced historically [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic "Endemic") diseases, though some remain intractable. The country was certified smallpox-free in April 1977 following two years of surveillance after its last *[Variola major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variola_major "Variola major")* case. It was officially certified polio-free by the [World Health Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organisation "World Health Organisation") on 27 March 2014, after successfully completing three consecutive years without recorded cases of [wild poliovirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_poliovirus "Wild poliovirus"). In contrast, cholera remains endemic to [Ganges-Meghna Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_Delta "Ganges Delta") [estuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary "Estuary"), resulting in seasonal outbreaks caused by monsoons and [water supply network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_network "Water supply network") shortcomings. [Tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis") is a major concern; India bears a quarter of the global tuberculosis [disease burden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_burden "Disease burden"). Historical [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria") cases have dropped from millions to hundreds of thousands annually, but India experiences localised seasonal increases caused by [drug-resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_resistance "Drug resistance") variants of the *[Plasmodium falciparum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_falciparum "Plasmodium falciparum")* and *[Plasmodium vivax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_vivax "Plasmodium vivax")* [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector "Disease vector").

[Patients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patient "Patient") in India often face a stark choice: use basic, free, [publicly funded health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publicly_funded_health_care "Publicly funded health care") or pay high [out-of-pocket fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out-of-pocket_expense "Out-of-pocket expense") for better-equipped care from private doctors and hospitals. This divide is particularly severe outside cities, as public clinics in rural areas rarely have enough doctors or nurses to meet local demands. When serious medical emergencies occur, poorer families are often forced to sell land or take out high-interest loans to pay for private care. This resulting medical debt pushes approximately 55 million people into poverty every year.

Long-term exposure to outdoor and indoor [air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution "Air pollution") adds a major environmental risk to [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health "Public health") in India. [Particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter "Particulate matter") emissions from [vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle "Vehicle") and [factories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory "Factory") are linked to a rise in [chronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_condition "Chronic condition") [respiratory diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_disease "Respiratory disease"), [strokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke "Stroke"), and [cardiovascular disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease "Cardiovascular disease"), together adding an estimated 1.7 million [premature deaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death#premature_death "Death") annually. India's early [COVID-19 lockdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_lockdowns "COVID-19 lockdowns") bought time to rapidly increase local testing and protective gear production; however, later waves of the virus overwhelmed the healthcare system, leading to severe shortages of oxygen and intensive care beds. Post-[pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic") [epidemiological studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology#Types_of_studies "Epidemiology") estimate that cumulative [excess deaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excess_death "Excess death") in India reached between 3.2 and 4.7 million.

## Culture

### Society

Although sometimes applied to other cultures and religions, [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste "Caste") is a fundamental [social institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_institution "Social institution") of India, most fully developed among [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus "Hindus"). All Hindus fall broadly into four castes, or *[varnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varna_(Hinduism) "Varna (Hinduism)")*: [Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmin "Brahmin"), or priests, at the top; below them [Kshatriya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kshatriya "Kshatriya"), or warriors; further below, [Vaishya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishya "Vaishya"), or merchants and farmers; and at the bottom, [Shudra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shudra "Shudra"), or the service class. Outside the caste system, and of traditional Hinduism, lie people formerly called "outcastes" or "[untouchables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untouchability "Untouchability")," and now [scheduled caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_caste "Scheduled caste") (a term used in India's constitution) or [Dalit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalit "Dalit"), a later self-description of [pride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pride "Pride"), meaning "broken" or "downtrodden". Each caste is further divided into sub-castes, or [jātis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C4%81ti "Jāti"), many of which are tied to occupations. However, the custom of [endogamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogamy "Endogamy"), or marrying within one's subcaste, makes caste a [hereditary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary "Hereditary") label rather than one of [occupational choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_choice_model "Occupational choice model") and has entrenched the caste system. The [Constituent Assembly of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituent_Assembly_of_India "Constituent Assembly of India") abolished untouchability in 1947, the Republic of India did so more formally in 1950, and India has since enacted other [anti-discrimination laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-discrimination_law "Anti-discrimination law") and social welfare initiatives related to caste. Still, caste-based inequality, discrimination, segregation, and [violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste-related_violence_in_India "Caste-related violence in India") persist.

Multi-generational [patrilineal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrilineality "Patrilineality") [joint families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_family "Joint family") have been the norm in India, though [nuclear families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_family "Nuclear family") are becoming common in [urban areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanisation_in_India "Urbanisation in India"). A very large majority of Indians have [their marriages arranged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arranged_marriage_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "Arranged marriage in the Indian subcontinent") by their parents or family elders. Marriage is thought to be for life, and the [divorce rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divorce_rate "Divorce rate") is extremely low, less than one in a thousand. Many women marry before reaching 18, which is their legal [marriageable age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriageable_age "Marriageable age"); [child marriages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_marriage_in_India "Child marriage in India") are not uncommon, especially in rural areas. In large parts of Hindu [northern India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_India "Northern India"), moreover, a form of territorial [exogamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exogamy "Exogamy") is observed in which a bride marries out of her natal village, and her parents do not visit her in her married home; the annual rite [raksha bandhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raksha_bandhan "Raksha bandhan"), during which married women return to their natal homes, has served both to affirm [bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_bonding "Human bonding") with their natal families and offer a recourse in times of marital stress.

Modern [Indian Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Muslim "Indian Muslim") society is highly diverse, divided by [local languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_India "Languages of India"), distinct cultural backgrounds, and internal [social classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class "Social class"). However, Indian Muslims have much lower college enrollment, less access to [banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_in_India "Banking in India") services, and far fewer [formal jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_employment "Formal employment") than average. Additionally, due to safety worries and unfair housing practices, many urban Muslims have been forced to move into separated neighbourhoods that often lack basic [public services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service "Public service"). Still, Muslim families regularly see higher rates of [child survival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_survival "Child survival") compared to other [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_India "Religion in India") and social groups. The [Saint Thomas Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Thomas_Christians "Saint Thomas Christians"), also known as Syrian Christians, trace their history to the arrival ashore in [South India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India") of St [Thomas the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle "Thomas the Apostle") in the 1st century CE, though inscriptions place their origins to the mid-first-millennium CE; they spread along the [Malabar Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabar_Coast "Malabar Coast") of the state of [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala "Kerala"), and have their distinctive [liturgical traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgy "Liturgy"). [Indian Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_India "Catholic Church in India") date their presence to 1500 soon after the arrival of [Vasco da Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasco_da_Gama "Vasco da Gama") in [Calicut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calicut "Calicut"). [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_in_India "Sikhism in India") is a monotheistic tradition founded by [Guru Nanak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Nanak "Guru Nanak") in the late 15th-century [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab "Punjab"); it grew into a tight-knit fellowship under later [gurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_gurus "Sikh gurus"), the last guru ceding authority to the holy book [Guru Granth Sahib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib "Guru Granth Sahib").

[Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Buddhism_in_India "History of Buddhism in India"), founded by [the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha "The Buddha") in the mid-5th century BCE in the middle [Ganges plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_plain "Ganges plain"), eventually spread beyond India to [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia") and [East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia"), even as it waned in its homeland; nevertheless, its [statues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha_statues "Buddha statues") and [stupas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stupa "Stupa")—stone realisations of the Buddha—remain prominent across [the subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"), drawing [pilgrims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrims "Pilgrims") from around the world. [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism_in_India "Jainism in India"), anciently contemporaneous with Buddhism, has two main sects, [Śvetāmbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Avet%C4%81mbara "Śvetāmbara"), whose monks and nuns wear robes and use bowls to receive [alms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alms "Alms"), and [Digambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara "Digambara"), whose monks go naked and cup their hands for alms. Jains today, regardless of sect, have aligned themselves with [environmentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentalism "Environmentalism"). The [Parsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsis "Parsis") follow [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism_in_India "Zoroastrianism in India"); they arrived on [India's western coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_South_West_India "Coastal South West India") from [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism_in_Iran "Zoroastrianism in Iran") late in the first millennium CE and rose in trade and industry, becoming early leaders of [Indian nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_nationalism "Indian nationalism"). The [Qissa-i Sanjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qissa-i_Sanjan "Qissa-i Sanjan") records their legendary arrival and welcome in India. [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_India "History of the Jews in India"), which had become established in India by the 8th-century CE, was never subject to [antisemitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism_in_India "Antisemitism in India") from Hindus, Christians, or Parsis, nor to [ethnic tensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_tensions "Ethnic tensions") from [Indian Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Muslims "Indian Muslims"); it has preserved distinctive Indo-Judaic customs.

India has had a long history of [religious pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_pluralism "Religious pluralism") that shapes its modern society. A large majority of Indian Muslims, [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India "Christianity in India"), and Sikhs participate in customs, or adhere to beliefs, that were once unique to Hinduism (e.g. *[karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma "Karma")*). Muslims, India's largest religious minority, have contributed significantly to a synthetic Indo-Islamic religious culture. [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism_in_India "Sufism in India"), the heterodox mystical movement in Islam found receptiveness among adherents of the medieval regional [Shaivite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaivism "Shaivism") and [Vaishnavite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism "Vaishnavism") traditions of [Bhakti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti "Bhakti"). Sikh [gurdwaras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurdwara "Gurdwara") and Sufi [dargahs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dargah "Dargah") often attract both visitors and worshippers from other religions. India has monastic traditions such as that of [Jain ascetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_ascetics "Jain ascetics") some of whom practice an extreme form of [nonviolence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolence "Nonviolence") (*[ahimsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa "Ahimsa")*) and Buddhist [communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangha "Sangha") that have [preserved ancient styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lineage_(Buddhism) "Lineage (Buddhism)") of [meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_meditation "Buddhist meditation"). This religious diversity coexists with religious [endogamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogamy "Endogamy")—people rarely marry outside their faith. There is also [religious segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_segregation "Religious segregation") in housing—religious groups cluster by choice or necessity. Thus, India's religious picture has both rich intermixing and clear divisions.

### Visual art

India has a very ancient tradition of art, which has exchanged many influences with the rest of [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia "Eurasia"), especially in the first millennium. During this period [Buddhist art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_art "Buddhist art") spread with Indian religions to [Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"), [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia") and [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), the last also greatly influenced by Hindu art. Thousands of [seals from the Indus Valley civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilisation#Seals "Indus Valley civilisation") of the third millennium BCE have been found, usually carved with animals, but also some with human figures. The [Pashupati seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal "Pashupati seal"), excavated in [Mohenjo-daro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohenjo-daro "Mohenjo-daro"), Pakistan, in 1928–29, is the best known. Virtually no art survives from a long period following the Indus Valley Civilisation. Almost all surviving ancient Indian art thereafter is in various forms of religious [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_sculpture "Indian sculpture") in durable materials, or coins. There was probably originally far more in wood, which is lost. In north India [Mauryan art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauryan_art "Mauryan art") is the first imperial movement.

Over the following centuries a distinctly Indian style of sculpting the human figure developed, with less interest in articulating precise anatomy than [ancient Greek sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_sculpture "Ancient Greek sculpture") but showing smoothly flowing forms expressing *prana* ("breath" or life-force). This is often complicated by the need to give figures multiple arms or heads, or represent different genders on the left and right of figures, as with the [Ardhanarishvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardhanarishvara "Ardhanarishvara") form of Shiva and [Parvati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parvati "Parvati").

Most of the earliest large sculpture is Buddhist, either excavated from Buddhist [stupas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stupa "Stupa") such as [Sanchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanchi "Sanchi"), [Sarnath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnath "Sarnath") and [Amaravati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaravati_Stupa "Amaravati Stupa"), or is rock cut [reliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief "Relief") at sites such as [Ajanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajanta_Caves "Ajanta Caves"), [Karla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karla_Caves "Karla Caves") and [Ellora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellora "Ellora"). Hindu and Jain sites appear rather later. In spite of this complex mixture of religious traditions, generally, the prevailing artistic style at any time and place has been shared by the major religious groups, and sculptors probably usually served all communities. [Gupta art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_art "Gupta art"), at its peak c. 300 CE – c. 500 CE, is often regarded as a classical period whose influence lingered for many centuries after; it saw a new dominance of Hindu sculpture, as at the [Elephanta Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephanta_Caves "Elephanta Caves"). Across the north, this became rather stiff and formulaic after c. 800 CE, though rich with finely carved detail in the surrounds of statues. But in the South, under the [Pallava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallava_dynasty "Pallava dynasty") and [Chola dynasties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_dynasty "Chola dynasty"), sculpture in both stone and bronze had a [sustained period of great achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_art_and_architecture#Sculpture_and_bronzes "Chola art and architecture"); the large bronzes with Shiva as [Nataraja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nataraja "Nataraja") have become an iconic symbol of India.

Ancient paintings have only survived at a few sites, of which the crowded scenes of court life in the [Ajanta Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajanta_Caves "Ajanta Caves") are some of the most important. Painted manuscripts of religious texts survive from Eastern India from 10th century onwards, most of the earliest being Buddhist and later Jain. These significantly influenced later artistic styles. The Persian-derived [Deccan painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_painting "Deccan painting"), starting just before the [Mughal miniature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_miniature "Mughal miniature"), between them give the first large body of secular painting, with an emphasis on portraits, and the recording of princely pleasures and wars. The style spread to Hindu courts, especially [among the Rajputs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajput_painting "Rajput painting"), and developed a variety of styles, with the smaller courts often the most innovative, with figures such as [Nihâl Chand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nih%C3%A2l_Chand "Nihâl Chand") and [Nainsukh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nainsukh "Nainsukh"). As a market developed among European residents, it was supplied by [Company painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_painting "Company painting") by Indian artists with considerable Western influence. In the 19th century, cheap [Kalighat paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalighat_painting "Kalighat painting") of gods and everyday life, done on paper, were urban [folk art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_art "Folk art") from [Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcutta "Calcutta"), which later saw the [Bengal School of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_School_of_Art "Bengal School of Art"), reflecting the art colleges founded by the British, the first movement in [modern Indian painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Indian_painting "Modern Indian painting").

### Mathematics

Significant mathematics began in India in the first millennium BCE. The *[Śulba Sūtras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulba_Sutras "Shulba Sutras")* (literally, "Aphorisms of the Chords" in [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit "Vedic Sanskrit")) (c. 700–400 BCE) contain the earliest extant verbal expression of the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem") (although very likely it had been known to the [Old Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Babylonian_dynasty "First Babylonian dynasty").) All mathematical works were orally transmitted until approximately 500 BCE; thereafter, they were transmitted both orally and in manuscript form. The oldest extant mathematical document produced on the Indian subcontinent is the birch bark [Bakhshali manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhshali_manuscript "Bakhshali manuscript") from the 7th century CE.

During the classical period of Indian mathematics (400 CE to 1200 CE), [Aryabhata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata "Aryabhata"), [Brahmagupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta "Brahmagupta"), [Bhaskara II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_II "Bhaskara II"), [Varāhamihira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var%C4%81hamihira "Varāhamihira"), and [Madhava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhava_of_Sangamagrama "Madhava of Sangamagrama") made important contributions. The [decimal number system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal "Decimal") in use today was first recorded in Indian mathematics. Indian mathematicians made early contributions to the study of the concept of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_(number) "0 (number)") as a number, [negative numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_numbers "Negative numbers"), [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic"), and [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra"). [Trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry") was further advanced in India, and the modern definitions of [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine "Sine") and [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine "Cosine") were developed there. These mathematical concepts were transmitted to the Middle East, China, and Europe. A later landmark in Indian mathematics was the development of the [series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_(mathematics) "Series (mathematics)") expansions for [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function "Trigonometric function") (sine, cosine, and [arc tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_tangent "Arc tangent")) by mathematicians of the [Kerala school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_school_of_astronomy_and_mathematics "Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics") in the 15th century CE. Their work, completed two centuries before the invention of [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") in Europe, provided the first example of a [power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series "Power series"). In the modern era, [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan "Srinivasa Ramanujan") made fundamental contributions to [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory").

### Music

India contains a wide array of musical practices, including many different [folk musics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_folk_music "Indian folk music") from different regions. [Indian classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_classical_music "Indian classical music") has Vedic origins, and split in the 13th century into the two main traditions of [Hindustani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_classical_music "Hindustani classical music") and [Carnatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnatic_music "Carnatic music") music. Hindustani is associated with North India and is more [improvisational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_improvisation "Musical improvisation"), featuring instruments such as the [sitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitar "Sitar") and [tabla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabla "Tabla"), and Carnatic is South Indian and more focused on written [compositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_composition "Musical composition") such as the *[kriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kriti_(music) "Kriti (music)")*, while both styles contain common elements such as the [raga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raga "Raga") melodic framework and [tala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tala_(music) "Tala (music)") rhythmic meter. Indian music has influenced western genres, including [rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_music "Rock music") and [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") musicians during the [1960s counterculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s "Counterculture of the 1960s").

[Indian film music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_film_music "Indian film music") is music written for [Indian cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_India "Cinema of India"), generally composed by [music directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Indian_film_music_directors "List of Indian film music directors") and sung by [playback singers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playback_singers "Playback singers"). Modern [Indian pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_pop "Indian pop") takes influences from classical, folk, and western [pop music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_music "Pop music").

### Dance

Dance in India draws heavily on [Indian classical dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_classical_dance "Indian classical dance") traditions. Many of these in turn arose in temples or other religious contexts. However, their sponsorship and promotion have continued in secular, modern India. India also has local and modern dance traditions. Whether a dance is classical is determined by the [Sangeet Natak Academi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangeet_Natak_Academi "Sangeet Natak Academi"), the Indian government's organisation for performing arts. Although more dances could perhaps meet the criteria for classical, the Akademi has chosen eight.

The best-known classical dance is Bharatanatyam, which began as temple dances performed by [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_people "Tamil people") [devadasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devadasi "Devadasi"). Identified with "prostitutes and courtesans", their dancing was formally banned in 1947. Concurrently, the dance was rehabilitated as a "pure" art form, with [Rukmini Devi Arundale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rukmini_Devi_Arundale "Rukmini Devi Arundale") as a prominent figure. A devdasi who went on to attain national and international prominence was [Thanjavur Balasaraswati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balasaraswati "Balasaraswati"). Some sources consider the dance-dramas [Chhau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chhau_dance "Chhau dance") of [Jharkhand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jharkhand "Jharkhand"), [West Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bengal "West Bengal"), and [Odisha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odisha "Odisha") and [Yakshagana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakshagana "Yakshagana") of [Karnataka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnataka "Karnataka") to also belong to the classical tradition.

Local dance traditions vary widely across India. In addition to the dance-dramas Chhau and Yakshagana, they include dance-dramas [Raslila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raslila "Raslila") of western [Uttar Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttar_Pradesh "Uttar Pradesh") and [Terukkuttu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terukkuttu "Terukkuttu") of Tamil Nadu; calendrical and festival dances such as the [Bhangra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhangra_(dance) "Bhangra (dance)") of Punjab, especially at [Vaisakhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaisakhi "Vaisakhi"), the onset of spring, and [Garba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garba_(dance) "Garba (dance)") of [Gujarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarat "Gujarat") during [Navratri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navratri "Navratri"); and tribal or [Adivasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adivasi "Adivasi") dances, such as those of the [Santal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santal "Santal") and [Toda people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toda_people "Toda people"), the latter, for example, in honour of the god [Ön](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toda_people#Religion "Toda people") who brought buffalo to earth.

Among 20th-century directions is the [modern dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_dance "Modern dance") of [Uday Shankar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uday_Shankar "Uday Shankar") in which classical styles were employed but not adhered to rigidly. Examples are dance-dramas based on the ancient Indian [animal fables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_fable "Animal fable"), *[Panchatantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchatantra "Panchatantra")*, and Nehru's mid-century meditation on Indian history, *[The Discovery of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Discovery_of_India "The Discovery of India")*. Dance has been an essential aspect of Indian films from the first [talkies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talkies "Talkies") of the 1930s. Individual and group dances in [Bollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bollywood "Bollywood"), for example, show a broad range of influences, including classical, local, and Western popular dance. Towards the end of the 20th century, innovations in [British South Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_South_Asian "British South Asian") music and dance, such as [Post-Bhangra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_the_United_Kingdom_(1990s)#Post-Bhangra "Music of the United Kingdom (1990s)"), fed back into dance in India.

### Clothing

From ancient times until the advent of the modern era, the most widely worn traditional dress in India was [draped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draped_garment "Draped garment"). For women, it took the form of a [sari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sari "Sari"), a single piece of cloth many yards long. The sari was traditionally wrapped around the lower body and the shoulder. In its modern form, it is combined with an underskirt, or Indian [petticoat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petticoat#Asian_petticoats "Petticoat"), and tucked in along the waistband for more secure fastening. It is also commonly worn with an Indian [blouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blouse "Blouse"), or [choli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choli "Choli"), which serves as the primary upper-body garment; the sari's end passes over the shoulder, covering the midriff and obscuring the upper body's contours. For men, a similar but shorter length of cloth, the [dhoti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhoti "Dhoti"), has served as a lower-body garment.

The use of stitched clothes became widespread after Muslim rule was established by the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate") (c. 1300 CE) and continued by the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") (c. 1525 CE). Among the garments introduced during this time and still commonly worn are: the [shalwars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shalwar_kameez "Shalwar kameez") and [pyjamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyjama "Pyjama"), both styles of trousers, and the tunics [kurta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurta "Kurta") and [kameez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kameez "Kameez"). Shalwars are atypically wide at the waist but narrow to a cuffed bottom. A drawstring holds them up, causing them to pleat at the waist. When the pants are cut quite narrow, on the [bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_(textile)#Bias "Grain (textile)"), they are called [churidars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churidar "Churidar"). The kameez is a long shirt or tunic. Its side seams left open below the waistline. The kurta is traditionally collarless and made of cotton or silk; it is worn plain or with embroidered decoration, such as [chikankari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chikankari "Chikankari"); and typically falls to either just above or just below the wearer's knees.

In the last 50 years, fashion in India has changed a great deal. In urban northern India, the sari is increasingly no longer everyday wear, though it remains popular on formal occasions. Younger urban women rarely wear the traditional shalwar kameez, favouring churidars or jeans. In office settings, ubiquitous air conditioning allows men to wear sports jackets year-round. For weddings and formal occasions, men in the middle and upper classes often wear bandhgala, or short [Nehru jackets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nehru_jackets "Nehru jackets"), with pants, with the groom and his [groomsmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groomsmen "Groomsmen") sporting [sherwanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherwani "Sherwani") and churidars.

### Cuisine

A typical Indian meal is built on a plain cereal, complemented by savoury dishes. The cooked cereal could be steamed rice; [chapati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapati "Chapati"), a thin unleavened bread; [idli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idli "Idli"), a steamed breakfast cake; or [dosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dosa_(food) "Dosa (food)"), a griddled pancake. The savoury dishes might include [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil "Lentil"), [pulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulses "Pulses"), vegetables, meat, poultry and fish commonly spiced with [ginger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginger_root "Ginger root") and [garlic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garlic "Garlic"), but also [coriander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander "Coriander"), [cumin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumin "Cumin"), [turmeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turmeric "Turmeric"), [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon "Cinnamon"), [cardamom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardamom "Cardamom") and others. In some instances, the ingredients may be mixed during the cooking process. India has distinctive vegetarian cuisines, each a feature of the geographical and cultural histories of its communities. About 20% to 39% of India's population consists of vegetarians. Although meat is eaten widely, the proportional consumption of meat is low.

The most significant import of cooking techniques into India during the last millennium occurred during the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire"), spreading into northern India from regions to its northwest, along with dishes such as [pilaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilaf "Pilaf"). Onions, garlic, almonds, and spices were added to the simple yogurt marinade of Persia. Rice was partially cooked and layered alternately with sauteed meat, the pot sealed tightly, and slow cooked according to another Persian cooking technique, to produce [biryani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biryani "Biryani"), a feature of festive dining in many parts of India.

The diversity of Indian food served worldwide has been partially concealed by the dominance of [Punjabi cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_cuisine "Punjabi cuisine"). The popularity of [tandoori chicken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tandoori_chicken "Tandoori chicken")—cooked in a [tandoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tandoor "Tandoor") oven, which had traditionally been used for baking bread in the rural Punjab and the Delhi region, especially among Muslims, but which is originally from [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia")—dates to the 1950s, and was caused in large part by an entrepreneurial response among people from the Punjab who had been displaced by the 1947 partition.

### Sports

Sport in modern India is a nexus of little-known indigenous traditions and commercial sporting events with worldwide reach. Traditional sports such as *[kabaddi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabaddi "Kabaddi")* and *[kho-kho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kho-kho "Kho-kho")* have experienced a resurgence, attracting large television audiences; on the other hand, *[kushti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushti "Kushti")* wrestling in rural *[akharas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akhara "Akhara")*—often regarded by its practitioners as not so much a sport as a physical and moral regimen—has continued to survive away from the exposure of broadcasting. [Cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket "Cricket") is India's most watched sport and a multi-billion dollar industry. The widespread popularity of chess among youth in [southern India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_India "Southern India") culminated in 18-year-old [Gukesh Dommaraju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gukesh_Dommaraju "Gukesh Dommaraju") of [Chennai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chennai "Chennai") winning the undisputed World Chess Champion title in 2024.

[British intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_rule_in_India "Company rule in India") reshaped India's athletic landscape. It introduced cricket, [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football "Association football") and [field hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey "Field hockey") and institutionalised [polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo "Polo")—a sport whose history is a case of reverse migration from India to the West. Today, at the rural [grassroots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassroots "Grassroots") level, government initiatives like "[Khelo India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khelo_India "Khelo India")" have democratised athletics for traditionally overlooked youth. Rural soccer programs have employed teamwork to break down old [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste "Caste") barriers.
State initiatives such as the [Target Olympic Podium Scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_Olympic_Podium_Scheme "Target Olympic Podium Scheme") have expanded resources for female athletes; meanwhile, the prominent international victories of figures like boxer [Mary Kom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Kom "Mary Kom") and badminton player [P. V. Sindhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._V._Sindhu "P. V. Sindhu") have challenged traditional gender stereotypes in sports.

[India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_national_cricket_team "India national cricket team") has won eight men's cricket [ICC tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_cricket#International_competitions "International cricket") titles: two [Cricket World Cups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup "Cricket World Cup"), three [T20 World Cups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Men%27s_T20_World_Cup "Men's T20 World Cup"), and three [Champions Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champions_Trophy "Champions Trophy") titles. [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_men%27s_national_field_hockey_team "India men's national field hockey team") has won a record eight men's field hockey gold medals in the [summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_Summer_Olympics "Field hockey at the Summer Olympics"), the last in [1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_1980_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men%27s_tournament "Field hockey at the 1980 Summer Olympics – Men's tournament").

* Indian batsman, [Virat Kohli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virat_Kohli "Virat Kohli"), after his [century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_(cricket) "Century (cricket)") at [Trent Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trent_Bridge "Trent Bridge"), 20 August 2018
* [Gukesh Dommaraju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gukesh_Dommaraju "Gukesh Dommaraju") from Chennai, the reigning world chess champion and the youngest ever
* [P. V. Sindhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._V._Sindhu "P. V. Sindhu"), after winning the silver medal in [Badminton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badminton "Badminton") at the [Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Summer_Olympics "2016 Summer Olympics") in [Rio de Janeiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro "Rio de Janeiro"), August 2016
* A young athlete after felicitating [Mary Kom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Kom "Mary Kom")—a [2012 London Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics "2012 Summer Olympics") boxing medal winner—New Delhi, 16 August 2012
