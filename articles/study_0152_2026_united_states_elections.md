# 2026 United States elections

[Elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections "Elections") are scheduled to be held, in large part, on November 3, 2026, in the United States. In these [midterm elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_midterm_election "United States midterm election"), scheduled to occur during [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States) "Republican Party (United States)") president [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump")'s second term, all 435 seats in the [U.S. House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._House_of_Representatives "U.S. House of Representatives") and 35 of the 100 seats in the [U.S. Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Senate "U.S. Senate") will be contested to determine the [120th United States Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/120th_United_States_Congress "120th United States Congress"). Thirty-nine state and territorial [U.S. gubernatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._gubernatorial "U.S. gubernatorial") elections, [attorney general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_attorney_general "State attorney general") elections, and numerous state and local elections, will also be contested.

The lead-up to the elections was marked by an inflation surge amidst the [United States affordability crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_affordability_crisis "United States affordability crisis"), including on [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline_and_diesel_usage_and_pricing "Gasoline and diesel usage and pricing"), [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_United_States "Healthcare in the United States"), and [food prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_prices "Food prices"). Polling indicated this was the top issue for voters ahead of the elections. The unpopular [2026 Iran war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war "2026 Iran war") and its impact on gas prices, along with [tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_second_Trump_administration "Tariffs in the second Trump administration"), contributed to high prices. Other key issues include opposition to [water, energy, and pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_AI "Environmental impact of AI")-intensive [AI data centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_data_center "AI data center"); [restrictions on abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_law "Abortion law"); [corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_in_the_United_States "Corruption in the United States"); and [foreign policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_the_United_States "Foreign policy of the United States"), including [relations with American allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration "Foreign policy of the second Trump administration") along with [proposed expansionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump "American expansionism under Donald Trump").

The [Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States) "Democratic Party (United States)") is anticipated by analysts to win the House, and the Senate has become competitive following strong polling in favor of Democrats in Republican territory. Analysts describe record low approval ratings for Trump as being a drag on down-ballot Republican races.

Trump called on Republican-led states to enact rare [mid-decade redistricting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_redistricting "2025–2026 United States redistricting") to boost the party's chances of holding power ahead of historical losses for the incumbent party during midterm elections. The moves prompted reciprocal redistricting from Democratic-led states through measures such as California's [Election Rigging Response Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Rigging_Response_Act "Election Rigging Response Act"). In April 2026, the Supreme Court ruled in *[Louisiana v. Callais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_v._Callais "Louisiana v. Callais")* that parts of the [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965 "Voting Rights Act of 1965") which enabled the creation of [majority-minority districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_majority-minority_United_States_congressional_districts "List of majority-minority United States congressional districts") were [unconstitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutionality "Constitutionality"). The ruling was expected to create additional Republican districts, and some scholars classified it as allowing for legal discrimination against minorities. The Trump administration made [repeated false claims of voter fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_denial_movement_in_the_United_States "Election denial movement in the United States") and used taxpayer dollars to fund Republican campaign advertisements.

## Background

In the [2024 United States presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_US_election "2024 US election"), Republican nominee [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump") defeated Democratic nominee [Kamala Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamala_Harris "Kamala Harris"). Key issues shaping the campaign included public concern for the post-pandemic recovery and rising [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation "Inflation").

[ABC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_(United_States) "ABC News (United States)"), [CBS News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS_News "CBS News"), [Fox News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_News "Fox News"), [NBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC_News "NBC News"), the [Associated Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_Press "Associated Press"), [MS Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS_Now "MS Now") and [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN "CNN") will use the Voter Poll by SSRS to gather voter preferences and opinions for the 2026 elections. From [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_elections "2018 United States elections") to [2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_elections "2024 United States elections"), ABC, NBC, CBS and CNN used data from the National Election Pool while the Associated Press and Fox News relied on [AP VoteCast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP_VoteCast "AP VoteCast").

The Republican Party performed worse than expected in the [November 2025 general elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_States_elections "2025 United States elections"). Democratic candidates [Abigail Spanberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abigail_Spanberger "Abigail Spanberger") of [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia "Virginia") and [Mikie Sherrill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikie_Sherrill "Mikie Sherrill") of [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey") won their gubernatorial races over their Republican opponents by larger than expected margins. Democrats performed strongly in the [concurrent state legislative elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_States_state_legislative_elections "2025 United States state legislative elections"), winning a supermajority in the [New Jersey General Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_General_Assembly "New Jersey General Assembly") and expanding their majority in the [Virginia House of Delegates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_House_of_Delegates "Virginia House of Delegates"). In [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"), Democrat [Zohran Mamdani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohran_Mamdani "Zohran Mamdani"), a self-identified [democratic socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism_in_the_United_States#Democratic_socialism "Progressivism in the United States"), was elected mayor in [a three-way race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_New_York_City_mayoral_election "2025 New York City mayoral election") against Republican [Curtis Sliwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Sliwa "Curtis Sliwa") and independent former governor [Andrew Cuomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Cuomo "Andrew Cuomo"), who ran as a moderate relative to Mamdani. The main theme of the elections was the [United States affordability crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_affordability_crisis "United States affordability crisis").

### 2026 election developments and analysis

Voters' views of Trump's presidency will likely be a factor in the election. During the 2025 off year elections, exit polls found that many voted in opposition to Trump or said that he was not a factor. Surveys released in late 2025 and early 2026 found that many viewed Trump's handling of the economy and immigration, two issues that propelled him to victory in the 2024 election, negatively. During elections held in his first term, Trump was on the minds of many who voted. In September 2026, Trump featured in a [two-day midterm convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Republican_National_Convention "2026 Republican National Convention") in Dallas to rally support and raise funds—historically, this type of event is reserved for presidential election years. Many Republicans on the ballot attended, but many also chose to forego the event, which RNC Chairman [Joe Gruters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Gruters "Joe Gruters") dubbed a "Trump-a-Palooza."

The outbreak of the [2026 Iran war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war "2026 Iran war") on February 28 introduced a further complicating factor, with rising gasoline prices and other economic challenges emerging as central electoral concerns. Analysts argued that gasoline prices represented the most direct channel through which the costs of the Iran war were transmitted to American voters, and that domestic electoral pressure from fuel costs was a primary political driver behind the Trump administration's push toward a ceasefire ahead of the November midterms. In response to the general unpopularity and economic concerns of the Iran war, several Republican candidates have distanced themselves from Trump and his approach to the war and have called for operations in Iran to end. Senate Minority Leader [Chuck Schumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Schumer "Chuck Schumer") compared the political environment in 2026 to the one in [2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_United_States_elections "2006 United States elections"), when dissatisfaction with [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush "George W. Bush")'s handling of the Iraq war led to many Democratic victories in the elections that year.

The nearly seven-month-old war in Iran became a political liability for some vulnerable Republican candidates ahead of the November midterms. In September 2026, two Republicans in tight Senate races — [Mike Rogers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Rogers_(Michigan_politician) "Mike Rogers (Michigan politician)") of Michigan and [Ashley Hinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashley_Hinson "Ashley Hinson") of Iowa — called for a swift end to the conflict, distancing themselves from President Trump. Hinson, who had voted six times against measures to curb Trump's war powers, said Iowans "shouldn't have to foot the bill at the pump or the checkout line for the war in Iran." Rogers, who had previously backed Trump's strikes, released a campaign ad urging that the war "end and end quickly." The shift came as Trump's approval rating fell to 32%, a career low, according to a [Reuters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuters "Reuters")/[Ipsos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipsos "Ipsos") poll, with Americans souring on both his handling of the economy and the war. [Diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_fuel "Diesel fuel") prices reached record highs, a particular concern in Iowa, where agriculture plays an outsized role in the state's economy. Other Republicans also moved to distance themselves: Iowa Representatives [Zach Nunn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zach_Nunn "Zach Nunn") and [Mariannette Miller-Meeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariannette_Miller-Meeks "Mariannette Miller-Meeks") reversed their stances and voted to limit Trump's war powers, while Representative Maria Elvira Salazar of Florida warned that Trump's immigration crackdown had gone too far. Democrats characterized the moves as election-year repositioning.

The administration's immigration policies, including [deportations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deportation_in_the_second_Trump_administration "Deportation in the second Trump administration") by [ICE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement "United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement") due to strengthened funding via the [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act "One Big Beautiful Bill Act"), were also scrutinized. Polling found that Hispanics, who were considered a key reason for Trump's victory in the 2024 election, overwhelmingly disapproved of Trump and had largely swung back towards Democrats due to economic concerns and dissatisfaction of Trump's handling of immigration and deportation. An October Reuters/Ipsos poll found that majorities said the cost of living was their top issue for the election and that 76% of Americans blamed Trump's policies for increasing inflation, including 57% of Republicans. Signs of an enthusiasm gap between Republican and Democratic voters were noted by analysts as possibly forecasting a Democratic "blue wave". In September 2026, American political analyst [Charlie Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Cook "Charlie Cook") described this phenomenon as a "red undertow", arguing that anticipated Democratic victories would come not from a rising Democratic tide, but a drowning Trump brand.

A month before the election, polling consistently found that Democratic candidates were leading key races for state governorships and those that would determine control of the Senate, with large swings towards Democratic candidates by independent voters. *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* highlighted how [Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas "Kansas"), a state that had not elected a Democratic Senator since the Second World War, had become a competitive battleground amid an unexpected surge of Democratic support deep in Republican territory. Democratic support came amidst multiple polls finding record-low approval ratings for Trump which some analysts considered was negatively impacting down-ballot races. Trump repeatedly told voters to pretend he was on the ballot to increase Republican enthusiasm which Democrats and some analysts considered a mistake owing to his low approval ratings.

In October, the Trump administration made efforts for [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine") to replace [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volodymyr_Zelenskyy "Volodymyr Zelenskyy") as [the nation's president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Ukraine "President of Ukraine") and struck a deal with Russia to purchase the country's fuel to bring down prices before the elections, and deepened [Russian-American cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia%E2%80%93United_States_relations "Russia–United States relations"). The move was criticized by Democrats for undercutting the [Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindsey_O._Graham_Sanctioning_Russia_and_Iran_Act_of_2026 "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026"), and some Republicans also criticized the deal.

#### Foreign interference

Foreign nations have sought to interfere with the 2026 elections. Since 2016, "the Kremlin has initiated online influence operations in every American federal election cycle" according to declassified intelligence documents. According to US intelligence officials, in 2026, Russia is targeting Americans with covert online disinformation campaigns to undermine confidence in the election and seed and promote domestic divisions with the goal to "inject chaos". No evidence exists that Russia has attempted to take aim at voting machinery or compromise the voting process. US intelligence officials have described the effort as less extensive and coordinated than prior years, with officials speculating Russia is less interested in midterm races than presidential contests and is distracted by the [Russo-Ukrainian war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war "Russo-Ukrainian war").

US intelligence has speculated Iran may have engaged in online influence efforts, and in August 2026 [Meta Platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta_Platforms "Meta Platforms") reported a small number of accounts on Facebook and Instagram originating from Iran spreading AI generated content with "anti-Republican views". US intelligence speculates that unlike prior years where China supported or opposed individual candidates based on their views, it may stay out of the 2026 elections due to fears of blowback.

On July 30, Trump alleged that China had collected 220 million American voter identification files including private information in 2022 and that it amounted to election interference. Trump then declassified intelligence records he presented as evidence the US election system falls "catastrophically short". Election experts pushed back on some of the claims, stating that voter registration files are public and easily accessible in most states and would not necessarily allow interference with the voting process. On September 28, Defense Secretary [Pete Hegseth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Hegseth "Pete Hegseth") announced the [Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Defense "Department of Defense") would launch an effort dedicated to combating foreign meddling in US elections that would use "advanced intelligence and cyber capabilities to identify, disrupt, and neutralize foreign interference". Democratic leaders of the House and Senate intelligence committees have expressed worry that Trump will use claims of foreign interference in an attempt to intervene in the midterm elections.

### Attempted election subversion by Republicans

The [second Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump "Second presidency of Donald Trump") has issued [executive orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_order "Executive order") and other [presidential directives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_directives "Presidential directives") instituting federal department and agency actions that have impacted [election administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_administration "Election administration") by state governments, federal [election security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_security "Election security") and [campaign finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_finance_in_the_United_States "Campaign finance in the United States") agencies, and offices within the [U.S. Justice Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice "United States Department of Justice") for enforcing [voting rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rights_in_the_United_States "Voting rights in the United States") protections. The administration's actions on [election law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_law "Election law") and voting rights have occurred alongside the [administration's targeting of political opponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeting_of_political_opponents_and_civil_society_under_the_second_Trump_administration "Targeting of political opponents and civil society under the second Trump administration") and [Republican Party efforts to disrupt voting after the 2024 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_efforts_to_disrupt_voting_after_the_2024_United_States_presidential_election "Republican Party efforts to disrupt voting after the 2024 United States presidential election"). Election lawyers at the [Brennan Center for Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brennan_Center_for_Justice "Brennan Center for Justice") and [Democracy Docket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_Docket "Democracy Docket") have described the administration's actions as [election subversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_subversion "Election subversion"), while surveys of U.S. political scientists conducted after Trump returned to office have found a consensus that the [United States is transitioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States "Democratic backsliding in the United States") towards [illiberal democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illiberal_democracy "Illiberal democracy") and [competitive authoritarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_regime#Competitive_authoritarian_regimes "Hybrid regime").

In January 2026, *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* reported that the second Trump administration had undertaken several tactics, including [mid-decade redrawing of congressional districts for partisan gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_redistricting "2025–2026 United States redistricting"), prosecution of political opponents, and a push to change voting methods and rules, that were meant to undermine confidence in the midterm elections. In October 2026, a *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times "New York Times")* analysis found Trump had attacked the integrity of US elections more than 70% of the days since he returned to office, particularly with false claims about "rigged" voting machines and fraudulent mail-in ballots.

Upon returning to office, the Trump administration has dismantled election security efforts across the federal government. Such cuts have included: firing or reassigning personnel at the [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybersecurity_and_Infrastructure_Security_Agency "Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency") focused on elections; defunding training programs for the Elections Infrastructure Information Sharing and Analysis Center; eliminating the FBI's foreign influence taskforce; downsizing and announcing closure of the [Foreign Malign Influence Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Malign_Influence_Center "Foreign Malign Influence Center"); and firing all members of the independent [Election Assistance Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Assistance_Commission "Election Assistance Commission").

Trump appointed prominent [2020 election deniers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempts_to_overturn_the_2020_United_States_presidential_election "Attempts to overturn the 2020 United States presidential election") into positions in government that could oversee the voting process. In the lead up to the election, pro-Trump conspiracy theorists and election deniers have won local races responsible for overseeing election results, and several election deniers are running for electoral offices in battleground states.

#### SAVE America Act

The [Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safeguard_American_Voter_Eligibility_Act "Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act"), also known as the SAVE Act or SAVE America Act, is a proposed [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") federal law that requires documentary proof of U.S. citizenship in order to register to vote in U.S. federal elections. The bill was introduced amid unsubstantiated claims that there was significant [election fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_fraud_in_the_United_States "Electoral fraud in the United States") in previous elections, including voting by undocumented immigrants, as part of the [immigration policies of the second Donald Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration "Immigration policy of the second Trump administration"). Opponents have claimed that the bill would disenfranchise eligible voters.

In March, the House of Representatives passed the SAVE America Act, an amended version of the previously proposed Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act. The bill, if approved by the Senate and signed into law by Trump, would have required voters to provide proof of citizenship in order to obtain or renew their voter registration. Voting rights experts said that such provisions would disenfranchise married women, racial minorities, low-income groups, and people who legally changed their name, among others. The bill ultimately died in the Senate that June, with four Republicans joining Democrats to block its inclusion.

#### Cancellation and nationalization of elections

In January, Trump floated the idea of illegally canceling the elections, citing an expected loss in the elections and a distrust in democratic systems. White House Press Secretary [Karoline Leavitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karoline_Leavitt "Karoline Leavitt") later stated that Trump was "speaking facetiously", and was "simply joking". In February, Trump stated that the elections should be nationalized to prevent voter fraud.

On February 27, *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* reported that several right-wing activists are working with the Trump administration to draft an executive order which, if signed, would effectively instate a national emergency and give Trump extended powers over the elections. Trump denied the existence of such an order.

According to [ABC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_(United_States) "ABC News (United States)"), Trump met prominent election denier [Tina Peters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Peters_(politician) "Tina Peters (politician)") on October 5 at the White House. Speaking to conservative podcaster [Joe Oltmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Oltmann "Joe Oltmann") on October 8, Peter's attorney stated that Trump "is not going to relinquish control again" if he believes the elections are rigged, and that "It's better if we're going to have a civil war of any sort that it be martial law with our side in control of the armaments."

#### Concerns over ICE at polling places

In February, [Steve Bannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Bannon "Steve Bannon") said the federal government was planning to send [ICE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement "United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement") to patrol polling stations in an attempt to prevent a Democratic victory, vowing to "never again [allow an election to be stolen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_denial_movement_in_the_United_States "Election denial movement in the United States")". In March, [Stateline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_Newsroom "States Newsroom") reported that legislation to "restrict immigration enforcement or the presence of federal forces" near polling places and other election sites has been offered or announced in [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California "California"), [Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecticut "Connecticut"), [New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico "New Mexico"), [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania"), [Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhode_Island "Rhode Island"), [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia "Virginia") and [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_(state) "Washington (state)"). The same month, the [Associated Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_Press "Associated Press") reported that several Democratic-led states including New Mexico and Virginia are taking steps to counter potential tactics to intimidate voters. In May, [Ned Lamont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ned_Lamont "Ned Lamont"), the governor of Connecticut, signed into law a bill that restricts immigration enforcement at locations including schools and places of worship. The legislation also prohibits law enforcement from wearing facial coverings or personal disguises.

#### Federal investigations and false claims of voter fraud

Internal documents reviewed by *The New York Times* found a widespread effort by the federal government to promote mistrust in the integrity of the 2026 election, with homeland security officials investigating over 150 nonprofit organizations they allege are helping noncitizens to vote. Trump and Republicans have repeatedly made false and unsubstantiated claims of massive voter fraud by noncitizens, with *The Times* describing Trump as making the case that "any Democratic election victory is suspicious on its face".

As part of investigations into election-integrity, the Justice Department has demanded voter registration data from states, suing 23 states and the District of Columbia that declined to provide unredacted voter rolls; all of the sued states were led by Democrats or had been won by Joe Biden in 2020. In January 2026, a federal judge blocked the Trump administration from threatening to withhold election funding from states that did not provide their voter information or alter their voter registration forms or voter systems. Also in January 2026, U.S. District Judge [David Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_O._Carter "David O. Carter") dismissed the department's lawsuit against California, which had sought personal information including partial Social Security numbers for roughly 23 million registered voters, ruling that the demands violated privacy laws and writing that federal centralization of the data would have a "chilling effect" on voter registration; a federal judge dismissed a similar lawsuit against Oregon later that month. The department appealed the California ruling to the [Ninth Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Ninth_Circuit "United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit"). In June 2026, federal prosecutors in Los Angeles publicly accused the state of blocking a voter roll audit and said they were conducting election fraud investigations while ballots from the state's June primary were still being counted.

The [Department of Homeland Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security "Department of Homeland Security") engaged in a widespread effort to investigate supposed voter fraud. Per internal documents released by the advocacy group [Democracy Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_Forward "Democracy Forward"), efforts have relied on the Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) division of [Immigration and Customs Enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement "United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement"), which CNN described as part of a "phased national initiative involving voter-roll acquisition, data analysis, investigative leads and field investigations". A June 1 HSI report documented the Justice Department had reviewed more than 50.6 million records and an additional 48.4 million records from [Heather Honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heather_Honey_(politician) "Heather Honey (politician)") obtained through "independent sources of information". The 50.6 million records were speculated to have been received by unredacted voter lists handed over by over a dozen Republican states months prior. By October 2026, the Justice Department had sued 30 states to hand over their voter information.

In July, Trump and DHS Secretary [Markwayne Mullin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markwayne_Mullin "Markwayne Mullin") alleged they had discovered 270,000 noncitizens on voter roles in Nevada, California, New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Letters sent to states days later acknowledged the numbers had been inflated, noting Trump had stated "there may be as many as", and the letters did not offer any proof of the claims. Mullin later claimed there were 15,903 noncitizen voters in Nevada, although the [Department of Homeland Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security "Department of Homeland Security") later admitted in a meeting with Nevada election officials the claim was unsubstantiated. DHS then stated 185 of the 15,903 were noncitizens of which Nevada disputed, saying after a state review all 185 were citizens and that DHS had "never provided Nevada with a list of 15,903 individuals" to begin with.

Ahead of the elections, Trump made false claims of voter fraud through mail-in ballots, calling it "corrupt" and "cheating", and encouraged Republican lawmakers to pass strict voter ID laws claiming it would "guarantee the midterms" for the GOP. In March 2026, Trump signed a new executive order on elections which would require the government to compile a list of eligible voters in each state and only allow the [United States Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Post_Office "United States Post Office") to send ballots to verified voters. Election experts described the effort as likely unconstitutional, and the announcement was met with promises to sue by voter rights groups and Democratic officials. On September 4, Judge [Indira Talwani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Talwani "Indira Talwani") issued a preliminary injunction against the USPS plan, finding it likely illegal and noting USPS had no authority to control mail-in voting, and Judge [Carl J. Nichols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_J._Nichols "Carl J. Nichols") issued another preliminary injunction against the plan on September 13. On September 14, the Supreme Court ruled against the Trump administration's attempt to screen mail-in ballots finding the government was "unlikely to succeed on the merits" and that it would implement chaos and confusion ahead of the elections. Following the ruling, state election officials were described as moving to restore faith in mail-in voting, and Trump later encouraged Republicans to vote by mail.

In September, the Supreme Court allowed the administration to use the [Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_Alien_Verification_for_Entitlements "Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements") system to check voter rolls for noncitizens, although federal law prohibits "systematically" removing voters from voter rolls 90 days before an election. The Trump administration had overhauled the system to combine social security and other data to create a master database of citizens per Executive Order 14248. Lawsuits and court rulings had previously blocked implementation of the system, with judges finding the overhaul had violated law in combining private information and had removed eligible voters from voter rolls.

Activists say they are preparing large-scale demonstrations if the Trump administration attempts to interfere with voting or the counting of ballots. At the same time, legal experts are publishing guidance for judges on how to respond if the government attempts to take control of ballots.

##### FBI raids

In January 2026, FBI agents executed a search warrant at the Fulton County, Georgia, election center, seizing approximately 600 boxes of ballots and other records from the 2020 presidential election, an action election law experts described as unprecedented federal intervention in locally administered elections. Federal investigations of the 2020 election subsequently expanded to several states: the [Office of the Director of National Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_National_Intelligence#ODNI "Director of National Intelligence") procured voting machines in Puerto Rico for examination in February, federal officials subpoenaed records of the partisan review of Maricopa County, Arizona's 2020 results in March, the Justice Department demanded ballots cast in Wayne County, Michigan, in April, and FBI agents questioned former poll workers in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, in May.

David Becker, executive director of The Center for Election Innovation & Research, said the investigations were "about the 2026 and 2028 elections" rather than 2020, characterizing them as intimidation of election officials. John Keller, former acting head of the Justice Department's Public Integrity Section, said the administration appeared to be normalizing federal investigation of state elections in preparation for potentially challenging future results.

In June 2026, FBI agents searched the Cleveland offices of the [Ohio Organizing Collaborative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_Organizing_Collaborative "Ohio Organizing Collaborative"), a voter registration and community organizing group, seizing computers and questioning staff and volunteers across Ohio as part of a Justice Department investigation into the group's voter registration efforts; the [Brennan Center for Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brennan_Center_for_Justice "Brennan Center for Justice") said the search fit a pattern of federal inquiries targeting voting infrastructure ahead of the midterm elections.

### *Louisiana v. Callais*

Analysts had predicted that a decision in *Callais* reducing the scope of the VRA could allow Republicans to gain a significant number of seats in the U.S. House of Representatives from redistricting in the Southeast, where the VRA has generally prevented Republican-controlled legislatures from maximizing their numbers of "safe" districts. *The New York Times* estimated that, if the Court weakened the VRA, up to twelve seats could shift from Democratic to Republican control.

When the decision in *Callais* was announced, media outlets characterized it as a "landmark" decision, predicting that the additional restrictions on Section 2 of the VRA would likely make challenges to redistricting significantly more difficult. Analysts expect it will allow southern states to eliminate [majority-minority districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_majority-minority_United_States_congressional_districts "List of majority-minority United States congressional districts"), changing election outcomes. The decision was seen by lawyers as the culmination of a long-time grudge of Roberts against the VRA, while media outlets considered the decision as rendering the VRA all but dead and [harming the country's democratic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States "Democratic backsliding in the United States"). The ruling was described by critics as starting a new era of [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws "Jim Crow laws"), allowing for legal discrimination of minorities in the United States. The court's opinion was expected to affect all levels of government including state and local governments.

An analysis from *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")* found that Justice Alito's claim that Black voter turnout had surpassed white voter turnout in two of the five most recent presidential elections, both nationally and in Louisiana, was based on misleading data and questionable methodology. Alito cited the brief from the [Department of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice "United States Department of Justice") which used "a proportion of the total population of each racial group over the age of 18"; however, that approach is "not favored by experts", who instead use "voter turnout as a proportion of the citizen voting age population or the voter eligible population." Following the latter analysis, the gap in voter turnout has actually widened, with Black voter turnout dropping since 2012, particularly after the Supreme Court's opinion in *[Shelby County v. Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelby_County_v._Holder "Shelby County v. Holder")* which struck down the preclearance formula defined in Section 4(b) of the VRA for states with historical discrimination concerns including Louisiana.

### Economy

The economy, including [gas prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline_and_diesel_usage_and_pricing "Gasoline and diesel usage and pricing"), [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021%E2%80%932023_inflation_surge "2021–2023 inflation surge"), [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_United_States "Healthcare in the United States"), [tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_second_Trump_administration "Tariffs in the second Trump administration"), and cost of living, is one the primary issues of the election. The second Trump administration has [imposed tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_second_Trump_administration "Tariffs in the second Trump administration") on much of the world with the intent to re-industrialize the United States. It was criticized by experts and global leaders for worsening inflation. Fuel costs were further increased by the [2026 Iran war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war "2026 Iran war"). The Trump administration announced in October 2026 that it would buy diesel from Russia and suspend sanctions on Russian diesel until April 2027. The move was intended to lower fuel prices before the November elections. A group of seven senators — four Republicans and three Democrats — criticized the decision. They said it violates the 2022 congressional ban on Russian energy imports. They also said the administration neither consulted Congress nor gave the required legal justification. At the same time, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent defended the agreement, saying the government was acting under existing legal authority and that the deal would reduce prices. Trade, including [tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariff "Tariff"), has been a leading issue of the campaign season.

Data released on October 14, 2026, by the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Labor_Statistics "Bureau of Labor Statistics") showed US annual inflation rising to 3.7 per cent in September. That marked an increase from 3.4 per cent in August and exceeded the 3.6 per cent forecast by economists in a [Bloomberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_News "Bloomberg News") poll. It was the first uptick since May. Core inflation, which leaves out food and energy, also surpassed [Wall Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal "The Wall Street Journal") expectations, climbing to 2.6 per cent from 2.4 per cent the month before.

### House of Representatives elections

All 435 voting seats in the [United States House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives "United States House of Representatives") will be up for election. Additionally, elections will be held to select the non-voting [delegate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia%27s_at-large_congressional_district "District of Columbia's at-large congressional district") for the [District of Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia "District of Columbia") and the non-voting [delegates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delegate_(United_States_Congress) "Delegate (United States Congress)") from 4 of the 5 [U.S. territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_insular_area "United States insular area"), excluding [Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico "Puerto Rico") due to its non-voting delegate serving a 4-year term. There are 16 Democratic incumbents in districts Donald Trump won, while only 8 Republican incumbents are in seats won by Harris.

The House map features a number of new congressional maps: [Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio "Ohio") and [Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah "Utah") will have new, court-mandated congressional districts this cycle; Missouri, North Carolina, and Texas redrew their maps mid-cycle; and the district map was changed in California following the passage of [Prop 50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_California_Proposition_50 "2025 California Proposition 50").

In Missouri, the legality of a Republican-backed congressional map remained disputed in September 2026. On September 8, [Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brett_Kavanaugh "Brett Kavanaugh") declined to intervene in an emergency appeal seeking to allow the new map to be used in the November election. Hours later, [U.S. District Judge Stephen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_R._Clark "Stephen R. Clark") issued a temporary order requiring Missouri to use the Republican-backed map, conflicting with Missouri Supreme Court ruling that had blocked its use and ordered a referendum on the map. The competing rulings left the state's congressional districts unresolved ahead of the general election. However, on September 10, the U.S. Supreme Court stayed Judge Clark's order whilst a federal lawsuit filed by Republican nominees [Bob Onder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Onder "Bob Onder") and [Rick Brattin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Brattin "Rick Brattin") for the 3rd and 5th districts respectively, played out. The [United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Eighth_Circuit "United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit") then fast-tracked the case for a hearing on September 17 and, ruling on the merits, affirmed the lower courts order on September 21, remanding the case back to the District Court for entry of a [permanent injunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injunction "Injunction") but stayed their ruling for a week pending an appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court. On September 25, the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously held that state law rules in the case and granted an emergency stay of the Eighth Circuit's order and Judge Clark's injunction, leaving the 2022 maps in place for the 2026 elections. The court also faulted the District Court and the Eighth Circuit for what it deemed as having "incorrectly applied" the [Purcell Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purcell_principle "Purcell principle") and further held that the Supreme Court of Missouri was not at fault for "upsetting the status quo".

## State elections

### Gubernatorial elections

36 states and three territories will be holding regularly scheduled gubernatorial elections. The governors of 15 states and two territories are term-limited. Prior to the 2026 gubernatorial elections, Republicans control 26 of the 50 state governorships, and each party holds 18 state governorships up for election. Republicans have held a majority of the state governorships since the [2010 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_elections "2010 United States elections").

### Lieutenant gubernatorial elections

Nine states will be holding lieutenant gubernatorial elections independently from their gubernatorial elections.

### Attorney general elections

30 states, two territories, and one federal district will be holding regularly scheduled attorney general elections.

### Secretary of state elections

26 states will be holding regularly scheduled secretary of state elections.

### Treasurer elections

27 states will be holding regularly scheduled treasurer elections.

### Auditor elections

23 states will be holding regularly scheduled auditor elections.

### Legislative elections

88 state legislative chambers and 5 territorial chambers will be holding regularly scheduled elections. Republicans have held a majority of the state legislative chambers and seats since the [2010 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_elections "2010 United States elections"). Prior to the regularly-scheduled 2026 state legislative elections, Republicans hold 4044 state legislative seats, while Democrats hold 3227 state legislative seats.

### State judicial elections

Elections are scheduled to be held in 2026, in various states across the country, including [supreme courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_court "Supreme court") and [appellate courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_court "Appellate court").

### Ballot measures

100 ballot measures in 37 states will be held in the November election.

## Local elections

[Local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_election "Local election") will be held in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") throughout 2026 to elect officers of municipal and county governments. At least 22 [mayorships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_the_50_largest_cities_in_the_United_States "List of mayors of the 50 largest cities in the United States") in the country's [top 100 most populous cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population "List of United States cities by population") will be up for election, and many more will hold elections for local ballot measures, city council, and numerous other municipal offices. Many of these races will coincide with the 2026 midterm elections.
