# Chemistry

**Chemistry** is the scientific study of the properties and behavior of [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter "Matter").[*[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*][*[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] It is a [physical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_science "Physical science") within the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science") that studies matter: composition, structure, properties, behavior and the changes they undergo during [reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") with other [substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance "Chemical substance").[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*] Chemistry also addresses the nature of [chemical bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond "Chemical bond") in [chemical compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound "Chemical compound").

In the scope of its subject, chemistry occupies an intermediate position between [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology"). It is sometimes called [the central science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_central_science "The central science") because it provides a foundation for understanding both [basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_research "Basic research") and [applied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science "Applied science") scientific disciplines at a fundamental level. For example, chemistry explains aspects of plant growth ([botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany "Botany")), the formation of igneous rocks ([geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology "Geology")), how atmospheric [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone "Ozone") is formed and how environmental [pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollutant "Pollutant") are degraded ([ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology "Ecology")), the properties of the soil on the Moon ([cosmochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmochemistry "Cosmochemistry")), how medications work ([pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology")), and how to collect [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") evidence at a crime scene ([forensics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forensic "Forensic")).[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

Chemistry has existed under various names since ancient times. It has evolved, and now chemistry encompasses various areas of specialisation, or subdisciplines, that continue to increase in number and interrelate to create further interdisciplinary fields of study. The applications of various fields of chemistry are used frequently for economic purposes in the [chemical industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_industry "Chemical industry").

## Etymology

The word *[chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry_(word) "Chemistry (word)")* comes from a modification during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") of the word *[alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy "Alchemy"),* which referred to an earlier set of practices that encompassed elements of chemistry, [metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy "Metallurgy"), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism"), and [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"). Alchemy is often associated with the quest to turn lead or other base metals into gold, though alchemists were also interested in many of the questions of modern chemistry.

The modern word *alchemy* in turn is derived from the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") word *al-kīmīā* (الكیمیاء). This may have [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_language "Egyptian language") origins since *al-kīmīā* is derived from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") χημία, which is in turn derived from the word *[Kemet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_of_Egypt "Name of Egypt")*, which is the ancient name of Egypt in the Egyptian language. Alternately, *al-kīmīā* may derive from χημεία 'cast together'.

## Modern principles

The current model of atomic structure is the [quantum mechanical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanical_model "Quantum mechanical model"). Traditional chemistry starts with the study of [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle "Elementary particle"), [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom"), [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule"), [substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance "Chemical substance"), [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal "Metal"), [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal "Crystal") and other aggregates of [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter "Matter"). Matter can be studied in solid, liquid, gas and plasma [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_matter "States of matter"), in isolation or in combination. The interactions, [reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") and transformations that are studied in chemistry are usually the result of interactions between atoms, leading to rearrangements of the [chemical bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond "Chemical bond") which hold atoms together. Such behaviors are studied in a chemistry [laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory "Laboratory").

The chemistry laboratory stereotypically uses various forms of [laboratory glassware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_glassware "Laboratory glassware"). However glassware is not central to chemistry, and a great deal of experimental (as well as applied/industrial) chemistry is done without it.

A [chemical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") is a transformation of some substances into one or more different substances. The basis of such a chemical transformation is the rearrangement of electrons in the chemical bonds between atoms. It can be symbolically depicted through a [chemical equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equation "Chemical equation"), which usually involves atoms as subjects. The number of atoms on the left and the right in the equation for a chemical transformation is equal. (When the number of atoms on either side is unequal, the transformation is referred to as a [nuclear reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reaction "Nuclear reaction") or [radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay "Radioactive decay").) The type of chemical reactions a substance may undergo and the energy changes that may accompany it are constrained by certain basic rules, known as [chemical laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_law "Chemical law").

[Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy "Energy") and [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy") considerations are invariably important in almost all chemical studies. Chemical substances are classified in terms of their [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure "Structure"), phase, as well as their [chemical compositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_composition "Chemical composition"). They can be analyzed using the tools of [chemical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_analysis "Chemical analysis"), e.g. [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy "Spectroscopy") and [chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography "Chromatography"). Scientists engaged in chemical research are known as [chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). Most chemists specialize in one or more sub-disciplines. Several [concepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept "Concept") are essential for the study of chemistry; some of them are:

### Matter

In chemistry, matter is defined as anything that has [rest mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_mass "Invariant mass") and [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume "Volume") (it takes up space) and is made up of [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle "Particle"). The particles that make up matter have rest mass as well – not all particles have rest mass, such as the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon "Photon"). Matter can be a pure [chemical substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance "Chemical substance") or a [mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture "Mixture") of substances.

#### Atom

The [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom") is the basic unit of chemistry. It consists of a dense core called the [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus") surrounded by a space occupied by an [electron cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_cloud "Electron cloud"). The nucleus is made up of positively charged [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") and uncharged [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") (together called [nucleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon "Nucleon")), while the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") cloud consists of negatively charged [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") which orbit the nucleus. In a neutral atom, the negatively charged electrons balance out the positive charge of the protons. The nucleus is dense; the mass of a nucleon is approximately 1,836 times that of an electron, yet the radius of an atom is about 10,000 times that of its nucleus.

The atom is also the smallest entity that can be envisaged to retain the [chemical properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_properties "Chemical properties") of the element, such as [electronegativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity "Electronegativity"), [ionization potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization_potential "Ionization potential"), preferred [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state "Oxidation state")(s), [coordination number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number "Coordination number"), and preferred types of bonds to form (e.g., [metallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal "Metal"), [ionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion "Ion"), [covalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent "Covalent")).

#### Element

A chemical element is a pure substance which is composed of a single type of atom, characterized by its particular number of [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") in the nuclei of its atoms, known as the [atomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number "Atomic number") and represented by the symbol *Z*. The [mass number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_number "Mass number") is the sum of the number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus. Although all the nuclei of all atoms belonging to one element will have the same atomic number, they may not necessarily have the same mass number; atoms of an element which have different mass numbers are known as [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope "Isotope"). For example, all atoms with 6 protons in their nuclei are atoms of the chemical element [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon"), but atoms of carbon may have mass numbers of 12 or 13.

The standard presentation of the chemical elements is in the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table"), which orders elements by atomic number. The periodic table is arranged in [groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table_group "Periodic table group"), or columns, and [periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_(periodic_table) "Period (periodic table)"), or rows. The periodic table is useful in identifying [periodic trends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_trends "Periodic trends").

#### Compound

A *compound* is a pure chemical substance composed of more than one element. The properties of a compound bear little similarity to those of its elements. The standard nomenclature of compounds is set by the [International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Pure_and_Applied_Chemistry "International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry") (IUPAC). [Organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound") are named according to the [organic nomenclature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_nomenclature "Organic nomenclature") system. The names for [inorganic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_compound "Inorganic compound") are created according to the [inorganic nomenclature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_nomenclature "Inorganic nomenclature") system. When a compound has more than one component, then they are divided into two classes, the electropositive and the electronegative components. In addition the [Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Abstracts_Service "Chemical Abstracts Service") has devised a method to index chemical substances. In this scheme each chemical substance is identifiable by a number known as its [CAS registry number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAS_registry_number "CAS registry number").

#### Molecule

A *molecule* is the smallest indivisible portion of a pure [chemical substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance "Chemical substance") that has its unique set of chemical properties, that is, its potential to undergo a certain set of chemical reactions with other substances. However, this definition only works well for substances that are composed of molecules, which is not true of many substances (see below). Molecules are typically a set of atoms bound together by [covalent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond "Covalent bond"), such that the structure is electrically neutral and all valence electrons are paired with other electrons either in bonds or in [lone pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_pair "Lone pair").

Thus, molecules exist as electrically neutral units, unlike ions. When this rule is broken, giving the "molecule" a charge, the result is sometimes named a [molecular ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_ion "Molecular ion") or a polyatomic ion. However, the discrete and separate nature of the molecular concept usually requires that molecular ions be present only in well-separated form, such as a directed beam in a vacuum in a [mass spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometer "Mass spectrometer"). Charged polyatomic collections residing in solids (for example, common [sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate "Sulfate") or [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate "Nitrate") ions) are generally not considered "molecules" in chemistry. Some molecules contain one or more unpaired electrons, creating [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_(chemistry) "Radical (chemistry)"). Most radicals are comparatively reactive, but some, such as [nitric oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_oxide "Nitric oxide") (NO) can be stable.

The "inert" or [noble gas elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas "Noble gas") ([helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium"), [neon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neon "Neon"), [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon "Argon"), [krypton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krypton "Krypton"), [xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon "Xenon") and [radon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon "Radon")) are composed of lone atoms as their smallest discrete unit, but the other isolated chemical elements consist of either molecules or networks of atoms bonded to each other in some way. Identifiable molecules compose familiar substances such as water, air, and many organic compounds like alcohol, sugar, gasoline, and the various [pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical "Pharmaceutical").

However, not all substances or chemical compounds consist of discrete molecules, and indeed most of the solid substances that make up the solid crust, mantle, and core of the Earth are chemical compounds without molecules. These other types of substances, such as [ionic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_compound "Ionic compound") and [network solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_covalent_bonding "Network covalent bonding"), are organized in such a way as to lack the existence of identifiable molecules *per se*. Instead, these substances are discussed in terms of [formula units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_unit "Formula unit") or [unit cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_cell "Unit cell") as the smallest repeating structure within the substance. Examples of such substances are mineral salts (such as [table salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_salt "Table salt")), solids like carbon and diamond, metals, and familiar [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica "Silica") and [silicate minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_mineral "Silicate mineral") such as quartz and granite.

One of the main characteristics of a molecule is its geometry often called its [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_structure "Molecular structure"). While the structure of [diatomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatomic "Diatomic"), [triatomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triatomic "Triatomic") or tetra-atomic molecules may be trivial, ([linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_molecular_geometry "Linear molecular geometry"), angular pyramidal etc.) the structure of polyatomic molecules, that are constituted of more than six atoms (of several elements) can be crucial for its chemical nature.

#### Substance and mixture

A chemical substance is a kind of matter with a definite [composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_composition "Chemical composition") and set of [properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_properties "Chemical properties"). A collection of substances is called a mixture. Examples of mixtures are [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_atmosphere "Earth's atmosphere") and [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy "Alloy").

#### Mole and amount of substance

The mole is a unit of measurement that denotes an [amount of substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amount_of_substance "Amount of substance") (also called chemical amount). One mole is defined to contain exactly 6.02214076×1023 particles (atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons), where the [number of particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_number "Particle number") per mole is known as the [Avogadro constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant "Avogadro constant"). [Molar concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration "Molar concentration") is the amount of a particular substance per volume of [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_(chemistry) "Solution (chemistry)"), and is commonly reported in mol/[dm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimetre "Decimetre")3.

### Phase

In addition to the specific chemical properties that distinguish different chemical classifications, chemicals can exist in several phases. For the most part, the chemical classifications are independent of these bulk phase classifications; however, some more exotic phases are incompatible with certain chemical properties. A *phase* is a set of states of a chemical system that have similar bulk structural properties, over a range of conditions, such as [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure "Pressure") or [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature "Temperature").

Physical properties, such as [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density "Density") and [refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index "Refractive index") tend to fall within values characteristic of the phase. The phase of matter is defined by the *[phase transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition")*, which is when energy put into or taken out of the system goes into rearranging the structure of the system, instead of changing the bulk conditions.

Sometimes the distinction between phases can be continuous instead of having a discrete boundary; in this case the matter is considered to be in a [supercritical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_fluid "Supercritical fluid") state. When three states meet based on the conditions, it is known as a [triple point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_point "Triple point") and since this is invariant, it is a convenient way to define a set of conditions.

The most familiar examples of phases are [solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid "Solid"), [liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid "Liquid"), and [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas "Gas"). Many substances exhibit multiple solid phases. For example, there are three phases of solid [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron "Iron") (alpha, gamma, and delta) that vary based on temperature and pressure. A principal difference between solid phases is the [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure "Crystal structure"), or arrangement, of the atoms. Another phase commonly encountered in the study of chemistry is the *aqueous* phase, which is the state of substances dissolved in [aqueous solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution "Aqueous solution") (that is, in water).

Less familiar phases include [plasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_physics "Plasma physics"), [Bose–Einstein condensates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose%E2%80%93Einstein_condensate "Bose–Einstein condensate") and [fermionic condensates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermionic_condensate "Fermionic condensate") and the [paramagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism "Paramagnetism") and [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism "Ferromagnetism") phases of [magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet "Magnet") materials. While most familiar phases deal with three-dimensional systems, it is also possible to define analogs in two-dimensional systems, which has received attention for its relevance to systems in [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology").

### Bonding

Atoms sticking together in molecules or crystals are said to be bonded with one another. A chemical bond may be visualized as the [multipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole "Multipole") balance between the positive charges in the nuclei and the negative charges oscillating about them. More than simple attraction and repulsion, the energies and distributions characterize the availability of an electron to bond to another atom.

The chemical bond can be a [covalent bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond "Covalent bond"), an [ionic bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_bond "Ionic bond"), a [hydrogen bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bond "Hydrogen bond") or just because of [Van der Waals force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waals_force "Van der Waals force"). Each of these kinds of bonds is ascribed to some potential. These potentials create the interactions which hold atoms together in [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") or [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal "Crystal"). In many simple compounds, [valence bond theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_bond_theory "Valence bond theory"), the Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion model ([VSEPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSEPR "VSEPR")), and the concept of [oxidation number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_number "Oxidation number") can be used to explain molecular structure and composition.

An ionic bond is formed when a metal loses one or more of its electrons, becoming a positively charged cation, and the electrons are then gained by the non-metal atom, becoming a negatively charged anion. The two oppositely charged ions attract one another, and the ionic bond is the electrostatic force of attraction between them. For example, [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium "Sodium") (Na), a metal, loses one electron to become an Na+ cation while [chlorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine "Chlorine") (Cl), a non-metal, gains this electron to become [Cl−](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride "Chloride"). The ions are held together due to electrostatic attraction, and that compound [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride "Sodium chloride") (NaCl), or common table salt, is formed.

In a covalent bond, one or more pairs of [valence electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_electron "Valence electron") are shared by two atoms: the resulting electrically neutral group of bonded atoms is termed a [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule"). Atoms will share valence electrons in such a way as to create a [noble gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas "Noble gas") electron configuration (eight electrons in their outermost shell) for each atom. Atoms that tend to combine in such a way that they each have eight electrons in their valence shell are said to follow the [octet rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octet_rule "Octet rule"). However, some elements like [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") and [lithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium "Lithium") need only two electrons in their outermost shell to attain this stable configuration; these atoms are said to follow the *duet rule*, and in this way they are reaching the electron configuration of the noble gas [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium"), which has two electrons in its outer shell.

Similarly, theories from [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics "Classical physics") can be used to predict many ionic structures. With more complicated compounds, such as [metal complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_(chemistry) "Complex (chemistry)"), valence bond theory is less applicable and alternative approaches, such as the [molecular orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital "Molecular orbital") theory, are generally used.

### Energy

In the context of chemistry, energy is an attribute of a substance as a consequence of its [atomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_structure "Atomic structure"), [molecular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_structure "Molecular structure") or aggregate [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_structure "Chemical structure"). Since a chemical transformation is accompanied by a change in one or more of these kinds of structures, it is invariably accompanied by an [increase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic_reaction "Endothermic reaction") or [decrease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic_reaction "Exothermic reaction") of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy "Energy") of the substances involved. Some energy is transferred between the surroundings and the reactants of the reaction in the form of heat or [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemistry "Photochemistry"); thus the products of a reaction may have more or less energy than the reactants.

A reaction is said to be [exergonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exergonic_reaction "Exergonic reaction") if the final state is lower on the energy scale than the initial state; in the case of [endergonic reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endergonic_reaction "Endergonic reaction") the situation is the reverse. A reaction is said to be [exothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic_reaction "Exothermic reaction") if the reaction releases heat to the surroundings; in the case of [endothermic reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic_reaction "Endothermic reaction"), the reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings.

Chemical reactions are invariably not possible unless the reactants surmount an energy barrier known as the [activation energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activation_energy "Activation energy"). ; if it is equal to zero the chemical reaction is said to be at [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equilibrium "Chemical equilibrium").

There exist only limited possible states of energy for electrons, atoms and molecules. These are determined by the rules of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), which require [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_(physics) "Quantization (physics)") of energy of a bound system. The atoms/molecules in a higher energy state are said to be excited. The molecules/atoms of substance in an excited energy state are often much more reactive; that is, more amenable to chemical reactions.

The phase of a substance is invariably determined by its energy and the energy of its surroundings. When the [intermolecular forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermolecular_force "Intermolecular force") of a substance are such that the energy of the surroundings is not sufficient to overcome them, it occurs in a more ordered phase like liquid or solid as is the case with water (H2O); a liquid at room temperature because its molecules are bound by [hydrogen bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bonds "Hydrogen bonds"). Whereas [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide "Hydrogen sulfide") (H2S) is a gas at room temperature and standard pressure, as its molecules are bound by weaker [dipole–dipole interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole%E2%80%93dipole_interaction "Dipole–dipole interaction").

The transfer of energy from one chemical substance to another depends on the *size* of energy [quanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum "Quantum") emitted from one substance. However, heat energy is often transferred more easily from almost any substance to another because the [phonons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonon "Phonon") responsible for vibrational and rotational energy levels in a substance have much less energy than [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon "Photon") invoked for the electronic energy transfer. Thus, because vibrational and rotational energy levels are more closely spaced than electronic energy levels, heat is more easily transferred between substances relative to light or other forms of electronic energy. For example, [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet") electromagnetic radiation is not transferred with as much efficacy from one substance to another as thermal or electrical energy.

The existence of characteristic energy levels for different [chemical substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance "Chemical substance") is useful for their identification by the analysis of [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_lines "Spectral lines"). Different kinds of spectra are often used in chemical [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy "Spectroscopy"), e.g. [IR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_spectroscopy "Infrared spectroscopy"), [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_spectroscopy "Microwave spectroscopy"), [NMR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance "Nuclear magnetic resonance"), [ESR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_spin_resonance "Electron spin resonance"), etc. Spectroscopy is also used to identify the composition of remote objects – like stars and distant galaxies – by analyzing their radiation spectra.

The term [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy#Chemical_energy "Energy") is often used to indicate the potential of a chemical substance to undergo a transformation through a [chemical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") or to transform other chemical substances.

### Reaction

When a chemical substance is transformed as a result of its interaction with another substance or with energy, a chemical reaction is said to have occurred. A *chemical reaction* is therefore a concept related to the "reaction" of a substance when it comes in close contact with another, whether as a mixture or a [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_(chemistry) "Solution (chemistry)"); exposure to some form of energy, or both. It results in some energy exchange between the constituents of the reaction as well as with the system environment, which may be designed vessels—often [laboratory glassware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_glassware "Laboratory glassware").

Chemical reactions can result in the formation or [dissociation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociation_(chemistry) "Dissociation (chemistry)") of molecules, that is, molecules breaking apart to form two or more molecules or rearrangement of atoms within or across molecules. Chemical reactions usually involve the making or breaking of chemical bonds. [Oxidation, reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox "Redox"), dissociation, acid–base [neutralization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutralization_(chemistry) "Neutralization (chemistry)") and molecular [rearrangement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rearrangement_reaction "Rearrangement reaction") are some examples of common chemical reactions.

A chemical reaction can be symbolically depicted through a [chemical equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equation "Chemical equation"). While in a non-nuclear chemical reaction the number and kind of atoms on both sides of the equation are equal, for a nuclear reaction this holds true only for the nuclear particles viz. protons and neutrons.

The sequence of steps in which the reorganization of chemical bonds may be taking place in the course of a chemical reaction is called its [mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_mechanism "Reaction mechanism"). A chemical reaction can be envisioned to take place in a number of steps, each of which may have a different speed. Many [reaction intermediates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_intermediates "Reaction intermediates") with variable stability can thus be envisaged during the course of a reaction. Reaction mechanisms are proposed to explain the [kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics "Chemical kinetics") and the relative product mix of a reaction. Many [physical chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemists "Chemists") specialize in exploring and proposing the mechanisms of various chemical reactions. Several empirical rules, like the [Woodward–Hoffmann rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodward%E2%80%93Hoffmann_rules "Woodward–Hoffmann rules") often come in handy while proposing a mechanism for a chemical reaction.

According to the [IUPAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUPAC "IUPAC") gold book, a chemical reaction is "a process that results in the interconversion of chemical species." Accordingly, a chemical reaction may be an [elementary reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_reaction "Elementary reaction") or a [stepwise reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepwise_reaction "Stepwise reaction"). An additional caveat is made, in that this definition includes cases where the [interconversion of conformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformational_isomerism "Conformational isomerism") is experimentally observable. Such detectable chemical reactions normally involve sets of molecular entities as indicated by this definition, but it is often conceptually convenient to use the term also for changes involving single molecular entities (i.e. 'microscopic chemical events').

### Ions and salts

An *ion* is a charged species, an atom or a molecule, that has lost or gained one or more electrons. When an atom loses an electron and thus has more protons than electrons, the atom is a positively charged ion or [cation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation "Cation"). When an atom gains an electron and thus has more electrons than protons, the atom is a negatively charged ion or [anion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anion "Anion"). Cations and anions can form a crystalline lattice of neutral [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_(chemistry) "Salt (chemistry)"), such as the Na+ and Cl− ions forming [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride "Sodium chloride"), or NaCl. Examples of [polyatomic ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyatomic_ion "Polyatomic ion") that do not split up during [acid–base reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid%E2%80%93base_reaction "Acid–base reaction") are [hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxide "Hydroxide") (OH−) and [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate "Phosphate") (PO43−).

[Plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_(physics) "Plasma (physics)") is composed of gaseous matter that has been completely ionized, usually through high temperature.

### Acidity and basicity

A substance can often be classified as an [acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid "Acid") or a [base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_(chemistry) "Base (chemistry)"). There are several different theories which explain acid–base behavior. The simplest is [Arrhenius theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrhenius_acid "Arrhenius acid"), which states that an acid is a substance that produces [hydronium ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydronium_ion "Hydronium ion") when it is dissolved in water, and a base is one that produces [hydroxide ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxide_ion "Hydroxide ion") when dissolved in water. According to [Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%B8nsted%E2%80%93Lowry_acid%E2%80%93base_theory "Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory"), acids are substances that donate a positive [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") [ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion "Ion") to another substance in a chemical reaction; by extension, a base is the substance which receives that hydrogen ion.

A third common theory is [Lewis acid–base theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acids_and_bases "Lewis acids and bases"), which is based on the formation of new chemical bonds. Lewis theory explains that an acid is a substance which is capable of accepting a pair of electrons from another substance during the process of bond formation, while a base is a substance which can provide a pair of electrons to form a new bond. There are several other ways in which a substance may be classified as an acid or a base, as is evident in the history of this concept.

Acid strength is commonly measured by two methods. One measurement, based on the Arrhenius definition of acidity, is [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH "PH"), which is a measurement of the hydronium ion concentration in a solution, as expressed on a negative [logarithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm "Logarithm") scale. Thus, solutions that have a low pH have a high hydronium ion concentration and can be said to be more acidic. The other measurement, based on the Brønsted–Lowry definition, is the [acid dissociation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_dissociation_constant "Acid dissociation constant") (Ka), which measures the relative ability of a substance to act as an acid under the Brønsted–Lowry definition of an acid. That is, substances with a higher Ka are more likely to donate hydrogen ions in chemical reactions than those with lower Ka values.

### Redox

Redox (*red*uction-*ox*idation) reactions include all [chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") in which atoms have their [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state "Oxidation state") changed by either gaining electrons (reduction) or losing electrons (oxidation). Substances that have the ability to oxidize other substances are said to be oxidative and are known as [oxidizing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizing_agents "Oxidizing agents"), oxidants or oxidizers. An oxidant removes electrons from another substance. Similarly, substances that have the ability to reduce other substances are said to be reductive and are known as [reducing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_agents "Reducing agents"), reductants, or reducers.

A reductant transfers electrons to another substance and is thus oxidized itself. And because it "donates" electrons it is also called an electron donor. Oxidation and reduction properly refer to a change in oxidation number—the actual transfer of electrons may never occur. Thus, oxidation is better defined as an increase in [oxidation number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_number "Oxidation number"), and reduction as a decrease in oxidation number.

### Equilibrium

Although the concept of [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equilibrium "Chemical equilibrium") is widely used across sciences, in the context of chemistry, it arises whenever a number of different states of the chemical composition are possible, as for example, in a mixture of several chemical compounds that can react with one another, or when a substance can be present in more than one kind of phase.

A system of chemical substances at equilibrium, even though having an unchanging composition, is most often not [static](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_equilibrium "Static equilibrium"); molecules of the substances continue to react with one another thus giving rise to a [dynamic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_equilibrium "Dynamic equilibrium"). Thus the concept describes the state in which the parameters such as chemical composition remain unchanged over time.

### Chemical laws

Chemical reactions are governed by certain laws, which have become fundamental concepts in chemistry. Some of them are:

* [Avogadro's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro%27s_law "Avogadro's law")
* [Beer–Lambert law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer%E2%80%93Lambert_law "Beer–Lambert law")
* [Boyle's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyle%27s_law "Boyle's law") (1662, relating pressure and volume)
* [Charles's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles%27s_law "Charles's law") (1787, relating volume and temperature)
* [Dalton's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalton%27s_law "Dalton's law") (1803, law of partial pressures)
* [Fick's laws of diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fick%27s_laws_of_diffusion "Fick's laws of diffusion")
* [Gay-Lussac's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_law "Pressure law") (1809, relating pressure and temperature)
* [Ideal gas law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_gas_law "Ideal gas law")
* [Le Chatelier's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Chatelier%27s_principle "Le Chatelier's principle")
* [Henry's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry%27s_law "Henry's law")
* [Hess's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hess%27s_law "Hess's law")
* [Law of conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy") leads to the important concepts of [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equilibrium "Chemical equilibrium"), [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics"), and [kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics "Chemical kinetics").
* [Law of conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_conservation_of_mass "Law of conservation of mass") continues to be conserved in [isolated systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_system "Isolated system"), even in modern physics. However, [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity") shows that due to [mass–energy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence "Mass–energy equivalence"), whenever non-material "energy" (heat, light, kinetic energy) is removed from a non-isolated system, some mass will be lost with it. High energy losses result in loss of weighable amounts of mass, an important topic in [nuclear chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chemistry "Nuclear chemistry").
* [Law of definite proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_definite_proportions "Law of definite proportions"), although in many systems (notably biomacromolecules and minerals) the ratios tend to require large numbers, and are frequently represented as a fraction.
* [Raoult's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoult%27s_law "Raoult's law")

## History

The [history of chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_chemistry "History of chemistry") spans a period from the ancient past to the present. Since several millennia BC, civilizations were using technologies that would eventually form the basis of the various branches of chemistry. Examples include extracting [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal "Metal") from [ores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore "Ore"), making pottery and glazes, fermenting beer and wine, extracting chemicals from plants for medicine and perfume, rendering fat into [soap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap "Soap"), making [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass "Glass"), and making [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy "Alloy") like [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze "Bronze").

Chemistry was preceded by its protoscience, [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy "Alchemy"), which operated a non-scientific approach to understanding the constituents of matter and their interactions. Despite being unsuccessful in explaining the nature of matter and its transformations, alchemists set the stage for modern chemistry by performing experiments and recording the results. [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle"), although skeptical of elements and convinced of alchemy, played a key part in elevating the "sacred art" as an independent, fundamental and philosophical discipline in his work *[The Sceptical Chymist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sceptical_Chymist "The Sceptical Chymist")* (1661).

While both alchemy and chemistry are concerned with matter and its transformations, the crucial difference was given by the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method") that [chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") employed in their work. Chemistry, as a body of knowledge distinct from alchemy, became an established science with the work of [Antoine Lavoisier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier "Antoine Lavoisier"), who developed a law of [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass "Conservation of mass") that demanded careful measurement and quantitative observations of chemical phenomena. The history of chemistry afterwards is intertwined with the [history of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_thermodynamics "History of thermodynamics"), especially through the work of [Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs "Willard Gibbs").

### Definition

The definition of chemistry has changed over time, as new discoveries and theories add to the functionality of the science. The term "chymistry", in the view of noted scientist [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle") in 1661, meant the subject of the material principles of mixed bodies. In 1663, the chemist [Christopher Glaser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Glaser "Christopher Glaser") described "chymistry" as a scientific art, by which one learns to dissolve bodies, and draw from them the different substances on their composition, and how to unite them again, and exalt them to a higher perfection.

The 1730 definition of the word "chemistry", as used by [Georg Ernst Stahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Ernst_Stahl "Georg Ernst Stahl"), meant the art of resolving mixed, compound, or aggregate bodies into their principles; and of composing such bodies from those principles. In 1837, [Jean-Baptiste Dumas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Dumas "Jean-Baptiste Dumas") considered the word "chemistry" to refer to the science concerned with the laws and effects of molecular forces. This definition further evolved until, in 1947, it came to mean the science of substances: their structure, their properties, and the reactions that change them into other substances—a characterization accepted by [Linus Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling "Linus Pauling"). More recently, in 1998, Professor [Raymond Chang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Chang_(chemist) "Raymond Chang (chemist)") broadened the definition of "chemistry" to mean the study of matter and the changes it undergoes.

### Background

Early civilizations, such as the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"), [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), and [Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_metallurgy_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "History of metallurgy in the Indian subcontinent"), amassed practical knowledge concerning the arts of metallurgy, pottery and dyes, but did not develop a systematic theory.

A basic chemical hypothesis first emerged in [Classical Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece "Classical Greece") with the theory of [four elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_elements "Four elements") as propounded definitively by [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") stating that [fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_(classical_element) "Fire (classical element)"), [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_(classical_element) "Air (classical element)"), [earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_(classical_element) "Earth (classical element)") and [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_(classical_element) "Water (classical element)") were the fundamental elements from which everything is formed as a combination. [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") [atomism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomism "Atomism") dates back to 440 BC, arising in works by philosophers such as [Democritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democritus "Democritus") and [Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus "Epicurus"). In 50 BCE, the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") philosopher [Lucretius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius "Lucretius") expanded upon the theory in his poem *[De rerum natura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_rerum_natura "De rerum natura")* (On The Nature of Things). Unlike modern concepts of science, Greek atomism was purely philosophical in nature, with little concern for empirical observations and no concern for chemical experiments.

An early form of the idea of [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass "Conservation of mass") is the notion that "[Nothing comes from nothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatio_ex_materia "Creatio ex materia")" in [Ancient Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy"), which can be found in [Empedocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empedocles "Empedocles") (approx. 4th century BC): "For it is impossible for anything to come to be from what is not, and it cannot be brought about or heard of that what is should be utterly destroyed." and [Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus "Epicurus") (3rd century BC), who, describing the nature of the Universe, wrote that "the totality of things was always such as it is now, and always will be".

In the [Hellenistic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_world "Hellenistic world") the art of alchemy first proliferated, mingling magic and occultism into the study of natural substances with the ultimate goal of transmuting elements into [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold "Gold") and discovering the elixir of eternal life. Work, particularly the development of [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation "Distillation"), continued in the early [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine "Byzantine") period with the most famous practitioner being the 4th century Greek-Egyptian [Zosimos of Panopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zosimos_of_Panopolis "Zosimos of Panopolis"). Alchemy continued to be developed and practised throughout the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world "Arab world") after the [Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests"), and from there, and from the Byzantine remnants, diffused into medieval and [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") Europe through Latin translations.

The Arabic works attributed to [Jabir ibn Hayyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabir_ibn_Hayyan "Jabir ibn Hayyan") introduced a systematic classification of chemical substances, and provided instructions for deriving an inorganic compound ([sal ammoniac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sal_ammoniac "Sal ammoniac") or [ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_chloride "Ammonium chloride")) from [organic substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound") (such as plants, blood, and hair) by chemical means. Some Arabic Jabirian works (e.g., the "Book of Mercy", and the "Book of Seventy") were later translated into Latin under the [Latinized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names "Latinisation of names") name "Geber", and in 13th-century Europe an anonymous writer, usually referred to as [pseudo-Geber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Geber "Pseudo-Geber"), started to produce alchemical and metallurgical writings under this name. Later influential Muslim philosophers, such as [Abū al-Rayhān al-Bīrūnī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ab%C5%AB_al-Rayh%C4%81n_al-B%C4%ABr%C5%ABn%C4%AB "Abū al-Rayhān al-Bīrūnī") and [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna") disputed the theories of alchemy, particularly the theory of the [transmutation of metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher%27s_stone "Philosopher's stone").

Improvements of the refining of ores and their extractions to smelt metals was widely used source of information for early chemists in the 16th century, among them [Georg Agricola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Agricola "Georg Agricola") (1494–1555), who published his major work *[De re metallica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_re_metallica "De re metallica")* in 1556. His work, describing highly developed and complex processes of mining metal ores and metal extraction, were the pinnacle of metallurgy during that time. His approach removed all mysticism associated with the subject, creating the practical base upon which others could and would build. The work describes the many kinds of furnaces used to smelt ore, and stimulated interest in minerals and their composition. Agricola has been described as the "father of metallurgy" and the founder of [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology "Geology") as a scientific discipline.

Under the influence of the [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution") and its new empirical methods propounded by [Sir Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Francis_Bacon "Sir Francis Bacon") and others, a group of chemists at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford "Oxford"), [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle"), [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke "Robert Hooke") and [John Mayow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mayow "John Mayow") began to reshape the old alchemical traditions into a scientific discipline. Boyle in particular questioned some commonly held chemical theories and argued for chemical practitioners to be more "philosophical" and less commercially focused in *[The Sceptical Chemyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sceptical_Chymist "The Sceptical Chymist")*. He formulated [Boyle's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyle%27s_law "Boyle's law"), rejected the classical "four elements" and proposed a mechanistic alternative of atoms and [chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") that could be subject to rigorous experiment.

In the following decades, many important discoveries were made, such as the nature of 'air' which was discovered to be composed of many different gases. The Scottish chemist [Joseph Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Black "Joseph Black") and the Flemish [Jan Baptist van Helmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont "Jan Baptist van Helmont") discovered [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide"), or what Black called 'fixed air' in 1754; [Henry Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Cavendish "Henry Cavendish") discovered [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") and elucidated its properties and [Joseph Priestley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Priestley "Joseph Priestley") and, independently, [Carl Wilhelm Scheele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Scheele "Carl Wilhelm Scheele") isolated pure [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen"). The theory of [phlogiston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlogiston "Phlogiston") (a substance at the root of all combustion) was propounded by the German [Georg Ernst Stahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Ernst_Stahl "Georg Ernst Stahl") in the early 18th century and was only overturned by the end of the century by the French chemist [Antoine Lavoisier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier "Antoine Lavoisier"), the chemical analogue of Newton in physics. Lavoisier did more than any other to establish the new science on proper theoretical footing, by elucidating the principle of [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass "Conservation of mass") and developing a new system of chemical nomenclature used to this day.

English scientist [John Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton "John Dalton") proposed the modern [theory of atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory "Atomic theory"); that all substances are composed of indivisible 'atoms' of matter and that different atoms have varying atomic weights.

The development of the electrochemical theory of chemical combinations occurred in the early 19th century as the result of the work of two scientists in particular, [Jöns Jacob Berzelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B6ns_Jacob_Berzelius "Jöns Jacob Berzelius") and [Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphry_Davy "Humphry Davy"), made possible by the prior invention of the [voltaic pile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaic_pile "Voltaic pile") by [Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta "Alessandro Volta"). Davy discovered nine new elements including the [alkali metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metals "Alkali metals") by extracting them from their [oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide "Oxide") with electric current.

British [William Prout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Prout "William Prout") first proposed ordering all the elements by their atomic weight as all atoms had a weight that was an exact multiple of the atomic weight of hydrogen. [J.A.R. Newlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.A.R._Newlands "J.A.R. Newlands") devised an early table of elements, which was then developed into the modern [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table") of elements in the 1860s by [Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev "Dmitri Mendeleev") and independently by several other scientists including [Julius Lothar Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Lothar_Meyer "Julius Lothar Meyer"). The inert gases, later called the [noble gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas "Noble gas") were discovered by [William Ramsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ramsay "William Ramsay") in collaboration with [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Rayleigh "Lord Rayleigh") at the end of the century, thereby filling in the basic structure of the table.

Organic chemistry was developed by [Justus von Liebig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justus_von_Liebig "Justus von Liebig") and others, following [Friedrich Wöhler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_W%C3%B6hler "Friedrich Wöhler")'s synthesis of [urea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urea "Urea"). Other crucial 19th century advances were; an understanding of valence bonding ([Edward Frankland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Frankland "Edward Frankland") in 1852) and the application of thermodynamics to chemistry ([J. W. Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._W._Gibbs "J. W. Gibbs") and [Svante Arrhenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svante_Arrhenius "Svante Arrhenius") in the 1870s).

At the turn of the twentieth century the theoretical underpinnings of chemistry were finally understood due to a series of remarkable discoveries that succeeded in probing and discovering the very nature of the internal structure of atoms. In 1897, [J.J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.J._Thomson "J.J. Thomson") of the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") discovered the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") and soon after the French scientist [Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Becquerel "Becquerel") as well as the couple [Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Curie "Pierre Curie") and [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie "Marie Curie") investigated the phenomenon of [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity "Radioactivity"). In a series of pioneering scattering experiments [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford") at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester") discovered the internal structure of the atom and the existence of the proton, classified and explained the different types of radioactivity and successfully [transmuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation "Nuclear transmutation") the first element by bombarding [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen") with [alpha particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle "Alpha particle").

His work on atomic structure was improved on by his students, the Danish physicist [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr"), the Englishman [Henry Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley "Henry Moseley") and the German [Otto Hahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Hahn "Otto Hahn"), who went on to father the emerging [nuclear chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chemistry "Nuclear chemistry") and discovered [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission"). The electronic theory of [chemical bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond "Chemical bond") and [molecular orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital "Molecular orbital") was developed by the American scientists [Linus Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling "Linus Pauling") and [Gilbert N. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis "Gilbert N. Lewis").

The year 2011 was declared by the United Nations as the [International Year of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Year_of_Chemistry "International Year of Chemistry"). As one of the United Nations' many International Year designations, it was an initiative to broaden outreach and public understanding in chemistry. The year was chosen as the 100th anniversary of [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie "Marie Curie")'s receipt of the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry"). Several years later, in 2019, the UN declared the [International Year of the Periodic Table](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=International_Year_of_the_Periodic_Table&action=edit&redlink=1 "International Year of the Periodic Table (page does not exist)").

## Practice

In the practice of chemistry, **pure chemistry** is the study of the fundamental principles of chemistry, while **applied chemistry** applies that knowledge to develop technology and solve real-world problems.

### Subdisciplines

Chemistry is typically divided into several major sub-disciplines. There are also several main cross-disciplinary and more specialized fields of chemistry.

* [Analytical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemistry "Analytical chemistry") is the analysis of material samples to gain an understanding of their [chemical composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_composition "Chemical composition") and [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_structure "Chemical structure"). Analytical chemistry incorporates standardized experimental methods in chemistry. These methods may be used in all subdisciplines of chemistry, excluding purely theoretical chemistry.

* [Biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry") is the study of the [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound "Chemical compound"), [chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") and interactions that occur in living [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism"). Biochemistry is highly interdisciplinary, covering [medicinal chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicinal_chemistry "Medicinal chemistry"), [neurochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurochemistry "Neurochemistry"), [molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology"), [forensics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forensic_science "Forensic science"), [plant science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_science "Plant science") and [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics"). [Neurochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurochemistry "Neurochemistry") is the study of [neurochemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurochemicals "Neurochemicals"); including transmitters, peptides, proteins, lipids, sugars, and nucleic acids; their interactions, and the roles they play in forming, maintaining, and modifying the nervous system.
* [Inorganic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_chemistry "Inorganic chemistry") traditionally was the study of metals and minerals, but is mainly concerned with the structure and behavior of compounds derived from metals and [main group elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_group_elements "Main group elements"). The distinction between organic and inorganic disciplines is not absolute and there is much overlap, most importantly in the sub-discipline of [organometallic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organometallic_chemistry "Organometallic chemistry").
* [Materials chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_Chemistry "Materials Chemistry") is the preparation, characterization, and understanding of solid state components or devices with a useful current or future function. The field is a new breadth of study in graduate programs, and it integrates elements from all classical areas of chemistry like [organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry"), [inorganic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_chemistry "Inorganic chemistry"), and [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography "Crystallography") with a focus on fundamental issues that are unique to [materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material "Material"). Primary systems of study include the chemistry of condensed phases (solids, liquids, [polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymers "Polymers")) and [interfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interface_(matter) "Interface (matter)") between different phases.
* [Organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry") is the study of the structure, properties, composition, mechanisms, and [reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction") of [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound"). An organic compound is defined as any compound based on a carbon skeleton. Organic compounds can be classified, organized and understood in reactions by their [functional groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_group "Functional group"), unit atoms or molecules that show characteristic chemical properties in a compound.

* [Physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry") is the study of the physical and fundamental basis of chemical systems and processes. In particular, the energetics and dynamics of such systems and processes are of interest to physical chemists. Important areas of study include [chemical thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_thermodynamics "Chemical thermodynamics"), [chemical kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics "Chemical kinetics"), [electrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry "Electrochemistry"), [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics"), [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy "Spectroscopy"), and more recently, [astrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrochemistry "Astrochemistry"). Physical chemistry has large overlap with [molecular physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_physics "Molecular physics"). Physical chemistry involves the use of [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") in deriving equations. It is usually associated with [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry "Quantum chemistry") and theoretical chemistry. Physical chemistry is a distinct discipline from [chemical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_physics "Chemical physics"), but again, there is very strong overlap.
* [Theoretical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_chemistry "Theoretical chemistry") is the study of chemistry via fundamental theoretical reasoning (usually within [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") or [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics")). In particular the application of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") to chemistry is called [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry "Quantum chemistry"). Since the end of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), the development of computers has allowed a systematic development of [computational chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_chemistry "Computational chemistry"), which is the art of developing and applying [computer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program "Computer program") for solving chemical problems. Theoretical chemistry has large overlap with (theoretical and experimental) [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics "Condensed matter physics") and [molecular physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_physics "Molecular physics").

Other subdivisions include [electrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry "Electrochemistry"), [femtochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femtochemistry "Femtochemistry"), [flavor chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavoring "Flavoring"), [flow chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_chemistry "Flow chemistry"), [immunohistochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunohistochemistry "Immunohistochemistry"), [hydrogenation chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenation "Hydrogenation"), [mathematical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_chemistry "Mathematical chemistry"), [molecular mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_mechanics "Molecular mechanics"), [natural product chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_product_chemistry "Natural product chemistry"), [organometallic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organometallic_chemistry "Organometallic chemistry"), [petrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrochemistry "Petrochemistry"), [photochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemistry "Photochemistry"), [physical organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_organic_chemistry "Physical organic chemistry"), [polymer chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_chemistry "Polymer chemistry"), [radiochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiochemistry "Radiochemistry"), [sonochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonochemistry "Sonochemistry"), [supramolecular chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supramolecular_chemistry "Supramolecular chemistry"), [synthetic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_chemistry "Synthetic chemistry"), and many others.
