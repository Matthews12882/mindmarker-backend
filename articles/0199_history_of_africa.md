# History of Africa

Archaic humans [emerged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out_of_Africa_1 "Out of Africa 1") out of [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") between 0.5 and 1.8 million years ago. This was followed by the [emergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recent_African_origin_of_modern_humans "Recent African origin of modern humans") of [modern humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomically_modern_humans "Anatomically modern humans") (*[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens")*) in [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa") around 300,000–250,000 years ago who spread across the world. The [oral word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoken_word "Spoken word") is revered in most African cultures, and history has predominantly been passed down through [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition"). In the 4th and 3rd millennia BCE [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Kerma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerma_culture "Kerma culture") arose in the northeast, while the [Tichitt Tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tichitt_Tradition "Tichitt Tradition") flourished in West Africa. Between around 3000 BCE and 500 CE, the [Bantu expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion "Bantu expansion") swept from north-western [Central Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Africa "Central Africa") (modern day [Cameroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameroon "Cameroon")) across much of Central, Eastern, and Southern Africa, displacing or absorbing groups such as the [Khoisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan "Khoisan") and [Pygmies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Pygmies "African Pygmies").

Some societies were [heterarchical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterarchy "Heterarchy") and [egalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarian "Egalitarian"), while others organised into [chiefdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiefdoms "Chiefdoms"). At its peak it is estimated that Africa had around 10,000 polities, with most following [traditional religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_traditional_religions "African traditional religions"). The continent has seen the rise and fall of many states, and there have long been extensive trading networks between its regions. Some [African empires and kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_empires "African empires") include:

From the 7th century CE, [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Africa "Islam in Africa") spread west amid the [Arab conquest of North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests"), and by [proselytization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_missionary_activity "Islamic missionary activity") to the Horn of Africa, bringing with it a new [social system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_system "Social system"). It later spread southwards to the [Swahili coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_coast "Swahili coast") assisted by Muslim dominance of the [Indian Ocean trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade "Indian Ocean trade"), and across the [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara") into the western [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") and [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sudanian_savanna "West Sudanian savanna"), catalysed by the [Fula jihads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fula_jihads "Fula jihads") of the 18th and 19th centuries. When the [trans-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_slave_trade "Trans-Saharan slave trade"), [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea_slave_trade "Red Sea slave trade"), [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_slave_trade "Indian Ocean slave trade") and [Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade "Atlantic slave trade") long-distance slave trades began, [local slave systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Africa "Slavery in Africa") started supplying captives for markets outside Africa. This reorientated many African economies, and created [various diasporas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora "African diaspora"), especially [in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora_in_the_Americas "African diaspora in the Americas").

Between 1870 and 1914, driven by the [Second Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution "Second Industrial Revolution") European [colonisation of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonisation_of_Africa "Colonisation of Africa") grew rapidly in the "[Scramble for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa "Scramble for Africa")", and saw the major European powers partition the continent at the 1884 [Berlin Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference "Berlin Conference"), resulting in territory under European imperial control increasing from one-tenth of the continent to over nine-tenths. European colonialism had [significant impacts on Africa's societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_Western_European_colonialism_and_colonization "Analysis of Western European colonialism and colonization"), and colonies were maintained for the purpose of economic exploitation of human and [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources_of_Africa "Natural resources of Africa") resources. Though Christianity has had a [long history in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Africa#History "Christianity in Africa"), widespread conversions occurred under European rule in southern West Africa, Central Africa, and Southern Africa due to successful [missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mission "Christian mission"), and the [syncretization of Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Africa#Africanizing_Christianity "Christianity in Africa") with local beliefs.

The [rise of nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_nationalism "African nationalism") gave birth to independence movements in many parts of the continent, and with a weakened Europe after the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War"), a wave of [decolonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonisation "Decolonisation") took place, culminating in the 1960 [Year of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_Africa "Year of Africa") and the establishment of the [Organisation of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity "Organisation of African Unity") in 1963 (the predecessor to the [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union "African Union")), with countries deciding to keep their colonial borders. [Traditional power structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_current_non-sovereign_African_monarchs "List of current non-sovereign African monarchs"), which had been incorporated into the colonial administration, remained partly in place in many parts of Africa, and their roles, powers, and influence vary greatly. Many countries have experienced the rise and fall of nationalism, and continue to face challenges such as internal conflict, [neocolonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neocolonialism "Neocolonialism"), and [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Africa "Climate change in Africa").

## History in Africa

The [oral word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoken_word "Spoken word") is revered in most African cultures, and history has predominantly been passed down through [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition"). In accordance with [African cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_cosmology "African cosmology"), African historical consciousness viewed historical change and continuity, order and purpose within the framework of a person and their environment, the gods, and their [ancestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestor_veneration "Ancestor veneration"), who believed themselves to be part of a [holistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holistic "Holistic") spiritual entity. In African societies, the historical process is largely a [communal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_communalism "African communalism") one, with eyewitness accounts, [hearsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearsay "Hearsay"), reminiscences, and occasionally [visions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vision_(spirituality) "Vision (spirituality)"), dreams, and hallucinations crafted into narrative oral traditions which are performed and transmitted through generations. In oral traditions time is sometimes [mythical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth "Myth") and social, and ancestors were considered historical actors. Mind and memory shapes traditions, as events are condensed over time and crystallise into [clichés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clich%C3%A9 "Cliché"). [Jan Vansina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Vansina "Jan Vansina") said that interpretation requires a proficient (or better yet native) understanding of the language and culture. Oral tradition can be [exoteric](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/exoteric "wiktionary:exoteric") or [esoteric](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/esoteric "wiktionary:esoteric"). Philosopher [Anselm Jimoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anselm_Jimoh&action=edit&redlink=1 "Anselm Jimoh (page does not exist)") wrote that in [African epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_epistemology "African epistemology"), the epistemic subject "experiences the epistemic object in a sensuous, emotive, intuitive, abstractive understanding, rather than through abstraction alone, as is the case in [Western epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology")" to arrive at a "complete knowledge", and as such oral traditions, [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Africa "Music of Africa"), [proverbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proverb "Proverb"), and the like were used in the preservation and transmission of knowledge.

## Prehistory

Africa is considered by most [paleoanthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoanthropologists "Paleoanthropologists") to be the [oldest inhabited territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_Humankind "Cradle of Humankind") on Earth, with the Human species originating from the continent. During the mid-20th century, [anthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology") discovered many [fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil "Fossil") and evidence of human occupation perhaps as early as seven million years ago ("[Before Present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present "Before Present")"; BP). Fossil remains of several species of early apelike humans thought to have [evolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved "Evolved") into modern humans, such as *[Australopithecus afarensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australopithecus_afarensis "Australopithecus afarensis")* [radiometrically dated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometrically_dated "Radiometrically dated") to approximately 3.9–3.0 million years BP, *[Paranthropus boisei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranthropus_boisei "Paranthropus boisei")* (c. 2.3–1.4 million years BP) and *[Homo ergaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_ergaster "Homo ergaster")* (c. 1.9 million–600,000 years BP) have been discovered.

After the evolution of *[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens")* approximately 350,000 to 260,000 years BP in Africa, the continent was mainly populated by groups of [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer"). These first modern humans left Africa and populated the rest of the [globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe "Globe") during the [Out of Africa II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out_of_Africa_II "Out of Africa II") migration dated to approximately 50,000 years BP, exiting the continent either across [Bab-el-Mandeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab-el-Mandeb "Bab-el-Mandeb") over the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea"), the [Strait of Gibraltar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Gibraltar "Strait of Gibraltar") in Morocco, or the [Isthmus of Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmus_of_Suez "Isthmus of Suez") in Egypt.

Other migrations of modern humans within the African continent have been dated to that time, with evidence of early human settlement found in Southern Africa, Southeast Africa, North Africa, and the [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara"). At the end of the [Ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age "Ice age"), estimated to have been around 10,500 BCE, the Sahara had again become a [green fertile valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Sahara "Green Sahara"), and its African populations returned from the interior and coastal highlands in Africa, with [rock art paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saharan_rock_art "Saharan rock art") depicting a fertile Sahara and large populations discovered in [Tassili n'Ajjer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tassili_n%27Ajjer "Tassili n'Ajjer") dating back perhaps 10 millennia. However, the warming and drying climate meant that by 5000 BCE, the Sahara region was becoming increasingly dry and hostile. Around 3500 BCE, due to a tilt in the Earth's [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit "Orbit"), the Sahara experienced a period of rapid desertification. The domestication of cattle in Africa preceded agriculture and seems to have existed alongside hunter-gatherer cultures. It is speculated that by 6000 BCE, cattle were domesticated in North Africa.

In West Africa, a wet phase ushered in an expanding [rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest "Rainforest") and wooded savanna from Senegal to Cameroon. Between 9000 and 5000 BCE, [Niger–Congo speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages "Niger–Congo languages") domesticated the [oil palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaeis_guineensis "Elaeis guineensis") and [raffia palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raffia_palm "Raffia palm"). [Black-eyed peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-eyed_pea "Black-eyed pea") and [voandzeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voandzeia "Voandzeia") (African groundnuts), were domesticated, followed by [okra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra "Okra") and [kola nuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_nut "Kola nut"). Since most of the plants grew in the forest, the Niger–Congo speakers invented polished stone axes for clearing forest. [Pygmies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Pygmies "African Pygmies") have inhabited Central Africa for many millennia, likely splitting into eastern and western groups around 5000 BP. Over 150,000 BP, there was an early dispersal of [anatomically modern humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomically_modern_humans "Anatomically modern humans") to Eastern and Southern Africa, equated with the modern-day [Khoekhoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoekhoe "Khoekhoe") and [San](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_peoples "San peoples"), the latter of whom have preserved their traditional hunter-gatherer way of life.

## c. 4000 BCE – c. 600 CE

### Northeast Africa

During the 4th millennium BCE, settled [agro-pastoralist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agro-pastoralist "Agro-pastoralist") populations along the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile") transitioned to [ranked societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_societies "Ranked societies"), legitimised by [local belief systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion "Ancient Egyptian religion"). From around 3500 BCE, [nomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nome_(Egypt) "Nome (Egypt)") (ruled by [nomarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomarch "Nomarch")) competed and expanded, with one centred in [Abydos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abydos,_Egypt "Abydos, Egypt") coming to rule [Upper Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Egypt "Upper Egypt"). Around 3100 BCE Upper Egypt conquered [Lower Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Egypt "Lower Egypt") to unify the region under the [1st dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt "First Dynasty of Egypt"), with the process of consolidation and assimilation completed by the time of the [3rd dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_dynasty "3rd dynasty") who formed the [Old Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Kingdom_of_Egypt "Old Kingdom of Egypt") in 2686 BCE. The [Kingdom of Kerma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kerma "Kingdom of Kerma") emerged around this time to become the dominant force in [Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia"), controlling territory as large as Egypt between the 1st and 4th [cataracts of the Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataracts_of_the_Nile "Cataracts of the Nile"). The height of the Old Kingdom saw the construction of many [great pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_pyramids "Egyptian pyramids"), though under the [6th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6th_dynasty "6th dynasty") power gradually decentralised to the nomarchs, culminating in the [disintegration of the kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt "First Intermediate Period of Egypt"), exacerbated by drought and famine. Around 2055 BCE, the [11th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11th_dynasty "11th dynasty"), based in [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt "Thebes, Egypt"), conquered the others to form the [Middle Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt "Middle Kingdom of Egypt"), and the [12th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12th_dynasty "12th dynasty") expanded into [Lower Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Nubia "Lower Nubia") at the expense of Kerma. Around 1700 BCE, the Middle Kingdom [fractured in two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt "Second Intermediate Period of Egypt"), and the [Hyksos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyksos "Hyksos") (a militaristic people from [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_(region) "Palestine (region)")) invaded and conquered Lower Egypt, while Kerma coordinated invasions deep into Egypt to reach its greatest extent. In 1550 BCE, the [18th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18th_dynasty "18th dynasty") expelled the Hyksos, and established the [New Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt "New Kingdom of Egypt"). The New Kingdom conquered the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") from the [Canaanites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaanites "Canaanites"), [Mittani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittani "Mittani"), [Amorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorites "Amorites"), and [Hittites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittites "Hittites"), and extinguished Kerma, incorporating Nubia into the empire and sending the Egyptian empire into its golden age. Internal struggles, drought, famine, and invasions by a [confederation of seafaring peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_peoples "Sea peoples") contributed to the New Kingdom's [collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt "Third Intermediate Period of Egypt") in 1069 BCE.

Egypt's collapse liberated the [Kingdom of Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush") in Nubia, which manoeuvred into power in Upper Egypt and conquered Lower Egypt in 754 BCE to form the Kushite Empire. The Kushites ruled for a century and oversaw a [revival in pyramid building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubian_pyramids "Nubian pyramids"), until they were [driven out of Egypt by the Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_conquest_of_Egypt "Assyrian conquest of Egypt") in 663 BCE. The Assyrians installed a [puppet dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/26th_Dynasty_of_Egypt "26th Dynasty of Egypt") that later gained independence and once more [unified Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Period_of_ancient_Egypt "Late Period of ancient Egypt"), until they were conquered by the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") in 525 BCE. Egypt briefly regained independence from the Achaemenids under the [28th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/28th_Dynasty_of_Egypt "28th Dynasty of Egypt") from 404 to 343 BCE. The conquest of [Achaemenid Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Egypt "Achaemenid Egypt") by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") in 332 BCE marked the beginning of [Hellenistic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period") and the installation of the [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians "Ancient Macedonians") [Ptolemaic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty "Ptolemaic dynasty") in Egypt. The Ptolemaics lost their holdings outside of Africa to the [Seleucids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucids "Seleucids") in the [Syrian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Wars "Syrian Wars"), expanded into [Cyrenaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica "Cyrenaica"), and briefly occupied [part of Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triakontaschoinos "Triakontaschoinos") in the 3rd century BCE. In the 1st century BCE, Ptolemaic Egypt became entangled in a [Roman civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar%27s_civil_war "Caesar's civil war"), leading to its conquest by the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") in 30 BCE. Kush persisted as a major regional power until, having been weakened from internal rebellion amid worsening climatic conditions, it disintegrated amid invasions by [Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum "Kingdom of Aksum") and the [Noba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noba "Noba") into [Makuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuria "Makuria"), [Alodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alodia "Alodia"), and [Nobatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobatia "Nobatia") around the 5th century CE.

### Horn of Africa

In the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa"), there was the [Land of Punt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_Punt "Land of Punt"), a kingdom thought to have been on the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea") which was a close trading partner of Ancient Egypt in the 3rd and 2nd millennia BCE. [Rodolfo Fattovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodolfo_Fattovich "Rodolfo Fattovich") equated it to the [Gash Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gash_Group "Gash Group") in the Sudanese-Eritrean lowlands, and some scholars have hypothesised modern-day [Somaliland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somaliland "Somaliland"), though [Kenneth Kitchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Kitchen "Kenneth Kitchen") and [Felix Chami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Chami "Felix Chami") locate it on [Zanzibar Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar_Island "Zanzibar Island"). In the [Eritrean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean_Highlands "Eritrean Highlands")-[Ethiopian Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Highlands "Ethiopian Highlands"), the kingdom of [dʿmt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%CA%BFmt "Dʿmt") rose c. 980 BCE as the region was incorporated into global trading networks, and it exhibited [Sabaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheba "Sheba") influences which most scholars attribute to a small migration of Sabaeans and their assimilation. Several scholars consider there to have been other contemporaneous states, and dʿmt's collapse in the mid-1st century BCE saw the region inhabited by small [polities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polities "Polities"). Modern-day Somalia was inhabited by [nomadic pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_pastoralists "Nomadic pastoralists"), and along the Horn's coast there were many [ancient Somali city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Somali_city-states "Ancient Somali city-states") that thrived off of the [wider Red Sea trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_history_of_Somalia "Maritime history of Somalia"), and enjoyed a lucrative [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly "Monopoly") on cinnamon from [Ancient India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_India "Ancient India") due to their freedom from Roman interference. In the 1st century CE, the [Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum "Kingdom of Aksum") rose from a city-state to rule much of the northern Ethiopian-Eritrean Highlands and the Red Sea port of [Adulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adulis "Adulis"). Aksum was described as one of the four [great powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_powers "Great powers") by Persian prophet [Mani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mani_(prophet) "Mani (prophet)") in the 3rd century. [Aksum's king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezana_of_Axum "Ezana of Axum") converted from [traditional religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum#Religion "Kingdom of Aksum") to [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") in the 4th century, gradually followed by the population. In the 6th century, Aksum [conquered South Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite_invasion_of_Himyar "Aksumite invasion of Himyar"), though struggled to maintain control over it, and began to gradually lose its dominance over Red Sea trade to Persians and Arabians.

### Northwest Africa

Northwest Africa (the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb "Maghreb")) was inhabited by [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers "Berbers") semi-[nomadic pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_pastoralists "Nomadic pastoralists"). In the 1st millennium BCE, [Phoenician migration and settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_settlement_of_North_Africa "Phoenician settlement of North Africa") came in search for precious metals in the [Gulf of Tunis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tunis "Gulf of Tunis"). This grew into [Ancient Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage "Ancient Carthage") after gaining independence from [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") in the 6th century BCE, and they built an [extensive trading empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage#Independence,_expansion_and_hegemony "Ancient Carthage") with a strict [mercantile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercantile "Mercantile") network. Carthage's collapse and conquest by Rome in the [Punic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punic_Wars "Punic Wars") (3rd and 2nd centuries BCE) saw [Numidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numidia "Numidia") and [Mauretania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauretania "Mauretania") become major powers in the Maghreb. Towards the end of the 2nd century BCE Mauretania fought alongside Numidia's [Jugurtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jugurtha "Jugurtha") against the Romans in the [Jugurthine War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jugurthine_War "Jugurthine War") after he had usurped the Numidian throne from a Roman ally. Together they inflicted heavy casualties, with the war only ending inconclusively when Mauretania's [Bocchus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bocchus_I "Bocchus I") sold out Jugurtha to the Romans. Around the turn of the millennium, both came under direct Roman rule. While [traditional religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Berber_religion "Traditional Berber religion") predominated among Berbers, some people converted to Christianity. In the 5th century CE the [Vandals conquered Roman Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandal_conquest_of_Roman_Africa "Vandal conquest of Roman Africa") followed by the [fall of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Rome "Fall of Rome"), though the province was [reconquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandalic_War "Vandalic War") by the [Byzantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") a century later. Large swathes of indigenous peoples regained self-governance in [Masuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauro-Roman_Kingdom "Mauro-Roman Kingdom") and its [numerous successor polities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romano-Berber_kingdoms "Romano-Berber kingdoms") in the Maghreb, including the kingdoms of [Ouarsenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ouarsenis "Kingdom of Ouarsenis"), [Aurès](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_Aur%C3%A8s "Kingdom of the Aurès"), and [Altava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Altava "Kingdom of Altava").

### West Africa

In the western [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") the rise of settled communities occurred largely as a result of the domestication of [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet "Millet") and of [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum"), and cattle [pastoralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism "Pastoralism") began c. 2500 BCE. Extensive east-west belts of [deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel"), [grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sudanian_savanna "West Sudanian savanna"), and [forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinean_forest%E2%80%93savanna_mosaic "Guinean forest–savanna mosaic") from north to south were adapted to by their respective societies and meant that [symbiotic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/symbiotic "wiktionary:symbiotic") trade relations developed in response to the differing environments. Beginning around 4000 BCE, the [Tichitt culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tichitt_culture "Tichitt culture") in modern-day Mauritania and Mali is the oldest known [complexly organised society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_society "Complex society") in West Africa, while others included the [Kintampo culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kintampo_culture "Kintampo culture") in modern-day Ghana, the [Nok culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nok_culture "Nok culture") in modern-day Nigeria, and the [Daima culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daima "Daima") around [Lake Chad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Chad "Lake Chad"). Towards the end of the 3rd century CE, a [wet period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsoon#Africa_(West_African_and_Southeast_African) "Monsoon") in the Sahel created areas for human habitation and exploitation that had not been habitable for the best part of a millennium. The [Ghana Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire "Ghana Empire") (also called *Wagadu*) rose out of the Tichitt culture. It grew wealthy following the introduction of the [camel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dromedary "Dromedary") to the western Sahel, which revolutionised the [trans-Saharan trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade "Trans-Saharan trade") that linked their capital and [Aoudaghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aoudaghost "Aoudaghost") with [Tahert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahert "Tahert") and [Sijilmasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sijilmasa "Sijilmasa") in North Africa. [Soninke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soninke_people "Soninke people") tradition holds that the final founding of Wagadu occurred after [Dinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinga_(ruler) "Dinga (ruler)") did a deal with [Bida](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bida_(mythology)&action=edit&redlink=1 "Bida (mythology) (page does not exist)") [[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bida_(mythologie) "fr:Bida (mythologie)")] (a serpent deity who was guarding a well) to sacrifice one maiden annually in exchange for assurance regarding plenty of rainfall and gold supply. Based on [large tumuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumulus#Africa "Tumulus") scattered across West Africa dating to this period, several scholars have speculated that there were further simultaneous and preceding states relative to Wagadu.

### Central, Eastern, and Southern Africa

In the [Grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_High_Plateau "Western High Plateau") of northwestern Cameroon, [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages "Bantu languages")-speaking agriculturalists started to gradually [migrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion "Bantu expansion") south sometime between 5000 and 3000 BCE. Despite intensive research, the cause of the migrations, and that of the directions taken, is still unclear, however there is consensus that there were multiple dispersal events. Around 1500 BCE, Bantu speakers reached central Cameroon. The 'Western Stream' likely followed the coast and the major rivers of the [Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_River "Congo River") system southwards to reach the southern fringe of the [Congolian Rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congolian_rainforests "Congolian rainforests") around 500 BCE (some may have used the sea to circumvent the rainforest). Their arrival coincided with the spread of [iron metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_metallurgy_in_Africa "Iron metallurgy in Africa") through Central Africa. Meanwhile, the 'Eastern Stream' travelled either the northern fringe of the rainforest or the [Ubangi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubangi_River "Ubangi River") eastwards, and reached just west of [Lake Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Victoria "Lake Victoria") around 500 BCE. While there, Bantu speakers adopted iron metallurgy from [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages "Cushitic languages") speakers (who had settled East Africa in the 3rd millennium BCE), and coexisted with them. Dispersal from the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Great_Lakes "African Great Lakes") region occurred in two more streams. One went west to meet the Western Stream in DR Congo and Angola, while the other went south and spread across Eastern and Southern Africa. Around the turn of the millennium, Bantu speakers reached central modern-day Tanzania and the east coast, before rapidly moving southwards along the coast to reach modern-day [Kwazulu Natal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwazulu_Natal "Kwazulu Natal") in South Africa around the 3rd century CE. Throughout this, Bantu speakers displaced, replaced, or intermarried with and absorbed [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer"), pastoralist, and agriculturalist groups.

## c. 600 – c. 1800

### North Africa

In the 7th century, as part of the [Arab conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_conquests "Arab conquests") which sought to [spread Islamic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam "Spread of Islam"), the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate "Rashidun Caliphate") scored crucial military victories against the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") and expanded rapidly, conquering Egypt and the [Exarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exarchate_of_Africa "Exarchate of Africa") from them in the 640s. After a [brief civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Fitna "First Fitna"), the Rashiduns were supplanted by the [Umayyads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate "Umayyad Caliphate"), who, after stiff resistance from [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers "Berbers") leaders such as [Kusaila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kusaila "Kusaila") and [Kahina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahina "Kahina"), had conquered the Maghreb by the early-8th century. Large numbers of Berber and [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_people "Coptic people") people willingly converted to Islam, and followers of [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") ('[People of the Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_the_Book "People of the Book")') were protected, though followers of [traditional Berber religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Berber_religion "Traditional Berber religion") were violently oppressed and often given the ultimatum to convert to Islam or face captivity or enslavement. In the 8th century the [Berber Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_Revolt "Berber Revolt") rocked the Caliphate and Berber dynasties took control over the Maghreb, while the [Bashmurian revolts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashmurian_revolts "Bashmurian revolts") ensued in Egypt.

The [Tulunids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulunids "Tulunids") briefly ruled an independent Egypt in the 9th century. In the 10th century, the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimids "Fatimids") rose to power in modern-day Tunisia, established a rival caliphate, and [conquered Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_conquest_of_Egypt "Fatimid conquest of Egypt"), before expanding into the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"). In the 11th century, the Fatimids started to collapse amidst [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire "Seljuk Empire") and [Crusader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade "First Crusade") expansion; [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin "Saladin"), a Fatimid [vizier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vizier "Vizier"), usurped power in the 12th century and established the [Ayyubid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty "Ayyubid dynasty") which restored Egyptian prestige. Meanwhile the zealous [Almoravids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almoravids "Almoravids") conquered the Maghreb and intervened in the Christian [reconquest of Iberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista"). The [Almohad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almohads "Almohads") revolution deposed the Almoravids to the east. After Christian gains in Iberia in the 13th century, the Almohads disintegrated into the [Marinids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marinids "Marinids"), [Zayyanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zayyanids "Zayyanids"), and [Hafsids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafsids "Hafsids") in modern-day Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia respectively. In the face of [Mongol expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasion_of_Syria "Mongol invasion of Syria"), [Mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk "Mamluk") generals [seized power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate "Mamluk Sultanate") in Egypt and expanded into the Middle East. In the early-16th century, the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") rapidly [conquered North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_wars_in_Africa "Ottoman wars in Africa") (save for Morocco, now under [Saadi rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadi_Sultanate "Saadi Sultanate")) to counter [Spanish expansionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars#Clash_of_interests_in_the_Mediterranean_and_Europe "Spanish–Ottoman wars"). In the following centuries, over a million Europeans are estimated to have been captured and [enslaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_slave_trade "Barbary slave trade") by [Barbary corsairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_corsairs "Barbary corsairs"). The [Ottoman regencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Eyalets_of_the_Ottoman_Empire_in_Africa "Category:Eyalets of the Ottoman Empire in Africa") became more independent during the 18th century.

### Western Sahel and Sudan

[Wagadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagadu "Wagadu") was the most powerful of a constellation of states stretching from [Takrur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takrur "Takrur") on the [Senegal River Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegal_River_Valley "Senegal River Valley") to [Mema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mema "Mema") in the [Niger Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger_River "Niger River"), all of whom were subservient to Wagadu at least some of the time. The [Gao Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao_Empire "Gao Empire") was situated to the east of Wagadu, and controlled the salt trade. Islam had reached Gao and Takrur by the 11th century, during which the Almoravids (before their conquests north) captured Wagadu's royal seat of [Aoudaghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aoudaghost "Aoudaghost"), gaining the king's conversion, and several vassals broke free. Despite Wagadu regaining full independence and power throughout the 12th century, this could not counteract the worsening climate and shifts in trade south and east, leading to its conquest by its former vassal [Sosso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sosso_Empire "Sosso Empire") c. 1200, propelling Soninke migration. In the face of Sosso aggression, [Sundiata Keita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundiata_Keita "Sundiata Keita") united the [Mandinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandinka_people "Mandinka people") clans and conquered Sosso and Gao, expanding across the western [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") and [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sudanian_savanna "West Sudanian savanna") to found the [Mali Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire "Mali Empire"). Mali grew fabulously rich through controlling the [trans-Saharan trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade "Trans-Saharan trade"). During the 14th and 15th centuries Mali experienced political instability and lost control over the [Jolof Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolof_Empire "Jolof Empire") in [Senegambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegambia "Senegambia"), and Sahelian trading centres to the nomadic [Tuareg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_people "Tuareg people"). Under [Sonni Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonni_Ali "Sonni Ali"), Gao swept through the crumbling empire, fending off the [Mossi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people "Mossi people") kingdom of [Yatenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yatenga_Kingdom "Yatenga Kingdom") and forming the [Songhai Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_Empire "Songhai Empire"). Weakened by internal conflict, in 1591 [Gao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao "Gao") was [conquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_invasion_of_the_Songhai_Empire "Moroccan invasion of the Songhai Empire") by [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadi_Sultanate "Saadi Sultanate"), and the [political vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_vacuum "Political vacuum") came to be filled by the [Segou Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9gou_Empire "Ségou Empire") and several other states. The once lucrative trans-Saharan trade gradually lost its prominence to the coastal Atlantic trade with Europeans, and the following centuries saw many droughts, famines, and epidemics.

### Central and Eastern Sahel and Sudan

Located around [Lake Chad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Chad "Lake Chad"), the founding of the [Kanem Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanem_Empire "Kanem Empire") is typically dated to c. 700, and it greatly benefited from serving as a crossroad for east–west and north-south Saharan trade, growing to encompass several peoples. Facilitated by contact with Muslim traders or nomadic [Tubu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toubou_people "Toubou people"), in the 11th century the [Duguwa dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duguwa_dynasty "Duguwa dynasty") converted to [Ibadi Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibadi_Islam "Ibadi Islam"), before they were usurped by the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni "Sunni") and [Kanembu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanembu_people "Kanembu people") [Sayfawa dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayfawa_dynasty "Sayfawa dynasty"). Under the Sayfawa, [Kanem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanem_(historical_region) "Kanem (historical region)") became a centre of [Islamic scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Science in the medieval Islamic world"), and extended its rule to the [Kaouar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaouar "Kaouar") and [Fezzan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fezzan_Basin "Fezzan Basin") oases, gaining even greater control over trans-Saharan trade. In Nubia, having converted to Christianity, [Makuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuria "Makuria") annexed [Nobatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobatia "Nobatia"), and [halted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_battle_of_Dongola "Second battle of Dongola") Muslim expansion during the 7th century, with whom it signed a [peace treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baqt "Baqt"). Over the following centuries Makuria defended the [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church "Coptic Orthodox Church") [Patriarch of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Alexandria "Patriarch of Alexandria") in Muslim-controlled Egypt, and entered into a [dynastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynastic_union "Dynastic union") or [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_union "Political union") union with [Alodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alodia "Alodia") during the 10th century. A warm period in the 14th century caused Lake Chad to dry up, and Kanem became engrossed in conflict with the Tubu and [Bilala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilala_dynasty "Bilala dynasty"); Kanem's [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Njimi "Njimi") was captured by the Bilala, forcing the leadership and other Kanembu to move to [Bornu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornu_(historical_region) "Bornu (historical region)"). Meanwhile, Islam spread through Hausaland; in the 15th century a [deposed Bornu ruler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman_III_Kalinumuwa "Uthman III Kalinumuwa") fled to [Kano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kano "Kingdom of Kano"), leading Bornu to vassalise several [Hausa kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_kingdoms "Hausa kingdoms"). Bornu also conquered [Kotoko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotoko_kingdom "Kotoko kingdom") (which had grown out of the [Sao civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sao_civilisation "Sao civilisation")), reaching the height of its power. In Hausaland the 16th century saw the rise of [Kebbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kebbi "Kingdom of Kebbi") which countered Bornu influence, while [Amina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_of_Zazzau "Amina of Zazzau")'s [Zazzau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Zazzau "Kingdom of Zazzau") expanded south. In Nubia, following Mamluk aggression, the [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague "Bubonic plague"), and the [migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization_of_the_Sudan_region "Islamization of the Sudan region") of [Bedouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedouin "Bedouin") tribes to the region, Makuria and Alodia gradually collapsed and disintegrated; the [Funj Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funj_Sultanate "Funj Sultanate") came to rule the Sudan, and blocked [Turco-Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turco-Egyptian "Turco-Egyptian") expansion south. To the west in modern-day [Darfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darfur "Darfur") and eastern Chad, after the successive [Daju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daju_kingdom "Daju kingdom") and [Tunjur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunjur_kingdom "Tunjur kingdom") kingdoms, the 17th century saw the rise of the [Bagirmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Bagirmi "Sultanate of Bagirmi"), [Wadai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadai_Sultanate "Wadai Sultanate"), and [Darfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Darfur "Sultanate of Darfur") sultanates, and frequent [slave-raiding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_raiding "Slave raiding") of populations to their south. Bornu entered into decline as it experienced droughts and lost control of trans-Saharan trade to the Tuareg and Ottomans.

### Horn of Africa

Following the [birth of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Islam#Inception "History of Islam") in the early-7th century, nomadic [Somalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalis "Somalis") began to convert to Islam, and it spread through the eastern Horn via *[daʿwah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da%CA%BFwah "Daʿwah")*. Though Muslim-Aksumite relations were [initially friendly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_to_Abyssinia "Migration to Abyssinia"), they soon soured. Aksum lost control of the Red Sea, and its isolation from [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") caused Ethiopian society to introspect and take inspiration from the [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament"). Islamised [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_kingdoms "Beja kingdoms") invaded the Ethiopian Highlands, forcing Aksum to migrate south, where, according to tradition, Aksum's expansion was met by a [Jewish or traditionalist queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudit "Gudit") who destroyed the state c. 960. From the 11th century, the growing Somalian population expanded across the eastern Horn, with Islam key to the assimilation of other groups. During the 13th century, [hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemony "Hegemony") in the Horn was contested by the Christian [Zagwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagwe_dynasty "Zagwe dynasty"), the Muslims of [Shewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Shewa "Sultanate of Shewa"), and the traditionalist [Damot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Damot "Kingdom of Damot"), while the [Ajuran Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajuran_Sultanate "Ajuran Sultanate") ruled the southern [Somali clans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_clans "Somali clans") on the coast. In the late-13th century, the [Solomonic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonic_dynasty "Solomonic dynasty") overthrew the Zagwe to found the [Ethiopian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Empire "Ethiopian Empire"), while Shewa was replaced by [Ifat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Ifat "Sultanate of Ifat"). Soon after, Ethiopia [conquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_Ethiopia#Early_Solomonic_period_to_Zemene_Mesafint "Territorial evolution of Ethiopia") Damot and the Muslim states, entering into a '[golden age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_age "Golden age")'. In the 16th century Muslims united behind [Adal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adal_Sultanate "Adal Sultanate") in *[jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihad "Jihad")* to reconquer the Muslim lands and occupy Ethiopia, until, with [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire "Portuguese Empire") assistance, Ethiopia [counter-attacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wayna_Daga "Battle of Wayna Daga") and its sovereignty was restored in a reduced state. The 16th and 17th centuries also saw [Oromo invasions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_invasions "Oromo invasions") into the Horn, weakening Ethiopia further and causing the collapse of Adal, while Ajuran also disintegrated. Ethiopia briefly consolidated its authority in the late-17th century, though the turn of the century saw [fragmentation and gradually-increasing anarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zemene_Mesafint "Zemene Mesafint"), which continued throughout the 18th century.

### West African forest regions

In the centuries following the turn of the first millennium, sophisticated states and urban centres emerged in the dense tropical rainforest belt of [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"), stretching from the [Senegambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegambia "Senegambia") and [Sierra Leone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Leone "Sierra Leone") eastward through [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia "Liberia"), [Côte d’Ivoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast "Ivory Coast"), [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana "Ghana"), [Togo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Togo "Togo"), [Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benin "Benin") and southern [Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigeria "Nigeria") to the [Cameroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameroon "Cameroon") border. Although these polities never approached the territorial size of the great [Sahelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") empires to the north, gold, kola nuts and later captives from their forests became crucial commodities in the commercial networks that linked the forest to the [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna "Savanna"), the [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara") and, from the late fifteenth century, the Atlantic coast.

In the forest zone of southwestern Nigeria, [Ile-Ife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ife_Empire "Ife Empire") developed from the late first millennium CE into the earliest major urban centre and the first empire in [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people "Yoruba people") history. By the eleventh and twelfth centuries it was a densely populated city enclosed by concentric walls, with extensive potsherd pavements and specialised craft production. Between the twelfth and fourteenth centuries [Ile-Ife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If%E1%BA%B9 "Ifẹ") stood at the centre of a wide political, economic and ritual network. Its economy was based on an indigenous primary glass industry (high-lime high-alumina glass), the only known industrial centre of its kind in [sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa"); glass beads functioned as a key currency of power and wealth. Naturalistic terracotta and copper-alloy sculpture of high quality was produced, and the city standardised the ideology of divine kingship and the [Òrìṣà](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orisha "Orisha") religious system. Its influence extended across much of [Yorubaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorubaland "Yorubaland") as far as the [River Niger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger_River "Niger River") and the Atlantic coast, encompassing both [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people "Yoruba people") and non-Yoruba peoples. The [Ife Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ife_Empire "Ife Empire") declined around 1420 amid prolonged drought, regional political disruption and internal crisis; successor [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people "Yoruba people") states continued many of its institutional forms while Ile-Ife retained lasting religious primacy.

Among the [Edo people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_people "Edo people") further east, the [Kingdom of Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Benin "Kingdom of Benin") coalesced by the thirteenth century into a centralised monarchy under the [Oba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oba_of_Benin "Oba of Benin"). The capital, [Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benin_City "Benin City") City, was surrounded by one of the largest systems of earthworks in pre-colonial [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"). From the mid-fifteenth century the kingdom expanded through military campaigns and diplomacy. From the late fifteenth century it traded with Portuguese and later other European merchants, exchanging ivory, pepper and limited numbers of captives for European goods. Royal workshops produced the celebrated brass and bronze plaques, sculptures and ivory carvings associated with the court. The kingdom remained a major regional power until its conquest by a British expedition in 1897.

In the western [Niger Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger_Delta "Niger Delta") the [Kingdom of Warri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Warri "Kingdom of Warri") (Iwere) was founded around 1480 by Prince [Ginuwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginuwa_I "Ginuwa I"), a son of the [Oba of Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oba_of_Benin "Oba of Benin"), who established the Olu dynasty over existing riverine communities. The kingdom developed as an independent coastal polity and from the sixteenth century maintained close relations with Portuguese traders and missionaries; several Olus adopted Christianity and one was educated in Portugal. Warri acted as a middleman in the Atlantic trade while remaining politically distinct from [Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Benin "Kingdom of Benin"). After a long succession crisis beginning in 1848 and the rise of powerful merchant princes, British intervention led to its incorporation into the [Southern Nigeria Protectorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Nigeria_Protectorate "Southern Nigeria Protectorate") by 1900.

Further north in the [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people "Yoruba people") forest–savanna fringe the [Oyo Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyo_Empire "Oyo Empire") rose in the early seventeenth century. Relying on cavalry obtained through northern trade, Oyo expanded across large parts of [Yorubaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorubaland "Yorubaland") and beyond, making [Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey "Dahomey") tributary for much of the eighteenth century. The [Alafin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rulers_of_the_Yoruba_state_of_Oyo "List of rulers of the Yoruba state of Oyo") ruled with a council of title-holders. The empire reached its greatest extent in the mid-eighteenth century before collapsing in the early nineteenth century amid internal conflict and external pressures.

In the forest zone of present-day [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana "Ghana"), [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people "Akan people")-speaking peoples developed a series of states from the late first millennium. Early centres such as [Bono Manso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bono_Manso "Bono Manso") participated in gold and kola trade with the Middle Niger. In the seventeenth century larger states, including [Denkyira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Denkyira "Kingdom of Denkyira") and [Akwamu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akwamu_Empire "Akwamu Empire"), dominated the gold-producing districts. Around 1701 the [Asante Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asante_Empire "Asante Empire") was founded under [Osei Tutu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osei_Kofi_Tutu_I "Osei Kofi Tutu I"); from its capital at [Kumasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumasi "Kumasi") it expanded to control most of the forest interior, monopolising gold production and inland routes to the coast. [Asante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asante_people "Asante people") developed a complex hierarchy of chiefs and a strong military organisation and remained a major power until British conquest in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

In the Dahomey Gap the [Kingdom of Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey "Dahomey") emerged in the early seventeenth century on the [Abomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abomey "Abomey") plateau. Under King [Agaja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agaja "Agaja") it conquered [Allada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allada "Allada") and Whydah in the 1720s, gaining direct access to the Atlantic coast. The kingdom developed a highly centralised military monarchy and participated extensively in the slave trade. For much of the eighteenth century it paid tribute to [Oyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyo_Empire "Oyo Empire"); after Oyo’s decline it asserted greater independence before falling to French conquest in the 1890s.

In southeastern [Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigeria "Nigeria") most [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people "Igbo people") communities remained organised in village and village-group democracies. A theocratic polity centred on [Nri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Nri "Kingdom of Nri") exerted ritual and moral authority over a wide area from the ninth or tenth century onward. The nearby site of [Igbo-Ukwu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo-Ukwu "Igbo-Ukwu") has yielded sophisticated bronze castings and other prestige goods dated to the same period.

Throughout the forest belt numerous smaller principalities and acephalous societies coexisted with these larger states. From the late fifteenth century European coastal trade intensified demand for gold, ivory and captives, reshaping political economies and accelerating the consolidation of several forest kingdoms. Most of these states retained their independence until the European partition of [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") in the late nineteenth century.

### Western Congo Basin

In the western [Congo Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Basin "Congo Basin"), a period of state and class formation began circa 700 with three centres: one in the west around [Pool Malebo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pool_Malebo "Pool Malebo"), one south around the [highlands of Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Angola "Geography of Angola"), and a third north-central around [Lake Mai-Ndombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Mai-Ndombe "Lake Mai-Ndombe"). By the 13th century there were three main federations of states in the western Congo Basin around Pool Malebo: the [Seven Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Kingdoms_of_Kongo_dia_Nlaza "Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza"), [Mpemba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mpemba "Mpemba"), and one led by [Vungu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vungu "Vungu"). [Kongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongo_language "Kongo language") traditions detail the founding and rapid expansion of the [Kingdom of Kongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kongo "Kingdom of Kongo") in the late-14th and early-15th centuries by [Lukeni lua Nimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lukeni_lua_Nimi "Lukeni lua Nimi"), though it was checked by the [Tio Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tio_Kingdom "Tio Kingdom") to its north (which saw [Mwene Muji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mwene_Muji "Mwene Muji") around Lake Mai-Ndombe to its northeast). In the late-15th century the Portuguese established relations with Kongo, whose ruler converted to [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Kongo "Catholic Church in Kongo") (eventually syncretising [traditional beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongo_religion "Kongo religion")). Atlantic trade began, with slaves being the most lucrative export. In the 16th century, [Afonso I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afonso_I_of_Kongo "Afonso I of Kongo")'s conquests produced many captives for Kongo's [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly "Monopoly"), and the slave trade rapidly grew to meet demand in the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"). By the mid-16th century, the [Kingdom of Loango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Loango "Kingdom of Loango") had formed north of the Congo River, and to the south [Ndongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ndongo "Ndongo") had broken free from Kongo and entered commercial relations with the Portuguese. In the late-16th century, as a concession for helping to expel [Yaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaka_people "Yaka people") invasions (from Mwene Muji), Kongo allowed the Portuguese to establish a colony at [Luanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luanda "Luanda"), which expanded and became [Portuguese Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Angola "Portuguese Angola"). Over the course of the late-16th and 17th centuries, the Portuguese sought to conquer Ndongo (allied to [Matamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matamba "Matamba")) in the [Angolan Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angolan_Wars "Angolan Wars"); despite fierce resistance from Queen [Nzinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba "Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba"), and brief [occupation of Luanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Loango-Angola "Dutch Loango-Angola") by the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_colonial_empire "Dutch colonial empire") (allied to Kongo), Portugal controlled most of Ndongo by the 1670s. Kongo had endured numerous civil wars throughout its history, though the rise of its vassal [Soyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyo "Soyo") prevented consolidation, and the late-17th century saw Kongo [fragment between royal houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongo_Civil_War "Kongo Civil War") as several vassals broke away; by the time of Kongo's restoration in 1709, it had ceased being the dominant regional power. Also during the 17th century, in the [textile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuba_textiles "Kuba textiles")-producing northeast, the [Boma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boma_Kingdom "Boma Kingdom"), [Yaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yaka_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Yaka Kingdom (page does not exist)"), and possibly the [Kuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuba_Kingdom "Kuba Kingdom") and [Pende](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pende_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Pende Kingdom (page does not exist)") kingdoms broke away from Mwene Muji.

### Great Lakes

During the 1st millennium CE the [Great Lakes region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Great_Lakes "African Great Lakes") saw several internal migrations of Bantu-speaking people out of the [Urewe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urewe "Urewe") culture west of [Lake Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Victoria "Lake Victoria"). Simultaneously, [symbiotic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/symbiotic "wiktionary:symbiotic") relations grew between [pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralists "Pastoralists") and agriculturalists, though there was likely also some competition over land use. [Banana cultivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana_cultivation "Banana cultivation") (derived from [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia")) had spread widely by the end of the millennium. Periods of drought made farmers dependent on cattle-keepers, while periods of heavy rainfall produced rich harvests, shifting power relations. Polities are thought to have formed around the earthwork sites of [Ntusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ntusi "Ntusi"), [Bigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bigo_bya_Mugenyi "Bigo bya Mugenyi"), and [Munsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munsa "Munsa") among others. [Nyoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyoro_language "Nyoro language") traditions detail the history of a '[Kitara Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitara_Empire "Kitara Empire")', ruled successively by the [Tembuzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tembuzi "Tembuzi") and [Chwezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chwezi "Chwezi") dynasties. Most scholars consider the Tembuzi to be [mythical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythical "Mythical"), and the Chwezi to have been historical figures, usually dated to the 14th and 15th centuries. Though earlier scholars often interpreted Kitara as an expansive empire, most recent scholars are sceptical, and Peter Robertshaw considered the Chwezi to have been religious leaders who utilised networks of hill-top shrines to project authority. Around 1500, severe droughts and famine destabilised the region, occurring again during the early-17th and 18th centuries. [Luo-speaking peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luo_peoples "Luo peoples") migrated from the Upper Nile basin into modern-day Uganda, and a [Biito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biito "Biito") dynasty replaced the Chwezi in [Bunyoro-Kitara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunyoro "Bunyoro"). The [Siita](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Siita&action=edit&redlink=1 "Siita (page does not exist)") dynasties of [Nkore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nkore "Nkore"), [Karagwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Karagwe "Kingdom of Karagwe"), and [Haya states](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Haya_kingdoms&action=edit&redlink=1 "Haya kingdoms (page does not exist)") were replaced by [Hinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinda_clan "Hinda clan") dynasties, who trace their origin to the last Chwezi king and his cattle-keeper [Ruhinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhinda "Ruhinda"). To the east, [Buganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buganda "Buganda") was founded sometime in the first half of the second millennium, traditionally by [Kintu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kintu "Kintu"). To the south, small states associated with clans saw increasing numbers of pastoralists who grew dominant and amalgamated them into the kingdom of [Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Rwanda "Kingdom of Rwanda") towards the end of the 15th century. Throughout the 16th and 17th centuries, Bunyoro raided states to the south such as Nkore and Rwanda. [Mpororo](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mpororo&action=edit&redlink=1 "Mpororo (page does not exist)") rose and fragmented during the 18th century, while Buganda rapidly expanded at the expense of Bunyoro to become the major power in the region.

### Eastern Congo Basin

In the central Congo Basin, farmers, fishermen, and hunters (often Pygmies) built symbiotic relations, and the [Balega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balega "Balega") organised into *[bwami](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bwami&action=edit&redlink=1 "Bwami (page does not exist)")* (socio-political collective 'grades' within a [hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchy "Hierarchy")). [Mongo peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongo_peoples "Mongo peoples") migrated throughout the [northeastern Congolian Rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_Congolian_lowland_forests "Northeastern Congolian lowland forests"), eventually organising into [polities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polities "Polities") called *[nkumu](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Nkumu&action=edit&redlink=1 "Nkumu (page does not exist)")*. Some groups highly valued [socio-political equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_equality "Social equality") and disapproved of leaving it. In the [Upemba Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upemba_Depression "Upemba Depression"), people traded with the [Copperbelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copperbelt "Copperbelt"), and graves dated to the 8th century contained objects associated with authority, such as anvils and [ceremonial axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_axe "Ceremonial axe"). [Jan Vansina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Vansina "Jan Vansina") wrote that in the [southern savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Congolian_forest%E2%80%93savanna_mosaic "Southern Congolian forest–savanna mosaic") 'lords of the land' held priestly roles due to their special relationship with the spirits of the land, and held sway over multiple villages, effectively ruling embryonic kingdoms. Accordingly, as lineages grew, authority was opportunistically absorbed or incorporated by force. The [centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabongo,_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo "Kabongo, Democratic Republic of the Congo") of the [Luba Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luba_Empire "Luba Empire") is yet to undergo archaeological research, meaning knowledge about its rise is limited; its foundation is typically dated to the 14th/15th centuries, though scholarly estimates range from the 8th to 17th centuries. [Luba-Katanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luba-Katanga_language "Luba-Katanga language") traditions (likely telescoped) detail the arrival of [sacred kingship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_kingship "Sacred kingship") (*bulopwe*) in the narrative about tyrannical-conqueror [Nkongolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nkongolo "Nkongolo") and the prodigy [Kalala Ilunga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalala_Ilunga "Kalala Ilunga"), where it is brought by the noble hunter [Mbidi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbidi "Mbidi"). Meanwhile, a [Lunda state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunda_Empire "Lunda Empire") formed in the [Nkalany Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nkalany_River "Nkalany River") (estimates range from c. 1450 to c. 1700), and [Lunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunda_people "Lunda people") traditions say that queen [Lueji](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lueji&action=edit&redlink=1 "Lueji (page does not exist)") married Mbidi's grandson and hunter [Chibinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibinda_Ilunga "Chibinda Ilunga"), whose son was the first [Mwata Yamvo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mwata_Yamvo "Mwata Yamvo"). In the 18th century, Lunda rapidly expanded into an extensive [commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth "Commonwealth") across the southern savanna, using the institutions of [perpetual kinship](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Perpetual_kinship&action=edit&redlink=1 "Perpetual kinship (page does not exist)") and [positional succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Positional_succession&action=edit&redlink=1 "Positional succession (page does not exist)") to incorporate peoples. Lunda lost several rulers in a long conflict (eventually won) against [Kanyok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanyok_Kingdom "Kanyok Kingdom") to the north, and conquered the [Pende](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pende_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Pende Kingdom (page does not exist)") and [Yaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yaka_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Yaka Kingdom (page does not exist)") kingdoms in the west. The most important constituents in the east and south were [Kazembe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazembe "Kazembe"), [Luvale](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Luvale_Kingdom&action=edit&redlink=1 "Luvale Kingdom (page does not exist)"), and [Kanongesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Kanongesha "Chief Kanongesha"). Meanwhile, Luba expanded its number of [tributaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary_state "Tributary state"), in some cases using its widely acknowledged prestige to intervene in succession disputes and distribute [ritually-powerful](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ritual_power&action=edit&redlink=1 "Ritual power (page does not exist)") [royal regalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_regalia "Royal regalia") related to *bulopwe*, in other cases using conquest.

### The East African coast and interior, and Madagascar

The [East African coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_coast "Swahili coast") and coastal archipelagos were dotted with proto-[Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili "Swahili") settlements, each with productive locally-developed industries. They participated in [Indian Ocean trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade "Indian Ocean trade"), and converted to Islam from c. 800, enabling them to participate in Muslim trading networks. Trade boomed from the 10th century, and major [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_city-states "Swahili city-states") included [Manda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manda_Island "Manda Island"), [Shanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanga,_Pate_Island "Shanga, Pate Island"), [Mombasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mombasa_Sultanate "Mombasa Sultanate"), and [Kilwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilwa_Sultanate "Kilwa Sultanate"), the latter of which seized control of the [Sofala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofala "Sofala") gold trade from [Mogadishu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Mogadishu "Sultanate of Mogadishu") (according to the *[Kilwa Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilwa_Chronicle "Kilwa Chronicle")*) and expanded its control along the coast. The interior was inhabited by [pastoralist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism "Pastoralism") [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages "Cushitic languages") and [Nilotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilotic_languages "Nilotic languages") speakers, along with Bantu speakers who continued to expand and adapt to their lands while absorbing the former two; the 17th century saw the establishment of [tributary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribute "Tribute") systems that stabilised the region. [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Madagascar "Geography of Madagascar") had been settled by [Austronesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesians "Austronesians") who had traversed the Indian Ocean in [outrigger canoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outrigger_boat "Outrigger boat"), likely sometime between the 5th and 7th centuries. The [Antalaotra](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Antalaotra&action=edit&redlink=1 "Antalaotra (page does not exist)") controlled trade on the northwest coast, while Malagasy societies organised and competed with one another over the [island's estuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rivers_of_Madagascar "List of rivers of Madagascar") and [bridgeheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgehead "Bridgehead"). In the 15th century, trade routes for Zimbabwean gold shifted north, weakening Kilwa. The Portuguese arrived at the coast soon after, and exploited divisions between coastal city-states to gain control over them while utilising a powerful navy. Meanwhile, southern Madagascar was integrated into global trade; the [Sakalava Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakalava_Empire "Sakalava Empire") rose to dominate west-coastal trade, accompanied by numerous other states, and the island saw several failed European colonial ventures. In the 17th and 18th centuries, [Lamu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamu_Archipelago "Lamu Archipelago")-Swahili cities rebelled against the Portuguese and allied the [Omani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omani_Empire "Omani Empire") which supplanted Portuguese rule.

### Zambezia and the Zimbabwean Plateau

Just south of the [Limpopo River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limpopo_River "Limpopo River"), from c. 1000 growing wealth and inequality at [Bambandyanalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambandyanalo "Bambandyanalo") led the population to move to the foot of [Mapungubwe Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapungubwe_Hill "Mapungubwe Hill") c. 1220, while the ruler settled its flat summit; the [Mapungubwe state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Mapungubwe "Kingdom of Mapungubwe") grew to cover 30,000 km2 (12,000 sq mi). Around 1300, amid unknown events and shifting trade routes north, the state collapsed, and [Great Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Zimbabwe "Great Zimbabwe"), with its monumental [dry-stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry-stone "Dry-stone") walls, rose to become the region's main political and economic centre. Its *[mambo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mambo_(title) "Mambo (title)")* (king) controlled the gold trade exporting to the east coast, and [the state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Zimbabwe "Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe") likely covered 50,000 km2 (19,000 sq mi). In the 15th century a new trade route was founded along the [Zambezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambezi "Zambezi") directly to the goldfields, contributing to the decline of Great Zimbabwe and the rise of [Butua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butua "Butua") near the goldfields to its west and the [Mutapa Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutapa_Empire "Mutapa Empire") on the northern [Zimbabwean Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwean_Plateau "Zimbabwean Plateau"). After having left [Luba territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katanga_Province "Katanga Province") in the 11th century, the [Maravi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maravi "Maravi") arrived near [Lake Malawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Malawi "Lake Malawi") around the 15th century and incorporated the pre-existing [Chewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chewa_people "Chewa people") politico-religious structure into their state. During the early-16th century, the Portuguese gained greater control over Zambezian trade. In the 17th century [Muzura](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Muzura&action=edit&redlink=1 "Muzura (page does not exist)") rapidly expanded the Maravi empire but failed to expel the Portuguese from Zambezia. In 1629 the Portuguese vassalised Mutapa by intervening in a succession conflict; the influx of *[prazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prazo "Prazo")*-holders (settlers) in Mutapa caused widespread lawlessness and insecurity, which exacerbated the devastation caused by drought, famine, and disease in the mid- to late-17th century. The 1680s and '90s saw the rise of [Changamire Dombo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Changamire_Dombo "Changamire Dombo") who conquered Butua to found the [Rozvi Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rozvi_Empire "Rozvi Empire") and expelled all Portuguese from the Plateau. Around the same time, some members of the Rozvi dynasty migrated south and conquered the [Venda groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venda_people "Venda people") in the [Soutpansberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soutpansberg "Soutpansberg") to found the [Singo state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singo_state "Singo state"); the kingdom fragmented around 1780 following shifting trade routes south and a succession conflict. The Maravi state collapsed amid succession crises in the 18th century, which also saw many migrations and the resettlement of much of the southern and central Plateau, replacing local dynasties and gradually weakening Rozvi power.

### Southern Africa

By the late-1st millennium CE, Bantu-speaking farmers populated most of [Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa "Southern Africa")'s cultivable [savannas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannas "Savannas"), reaching southwards to the [Kei River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kei_River "Kei River"); they traded goods such as livestock, grain, and metal tools between communities and with hunter-gatherers. Around the 11th century, more agriculturists—who likely brought with them ancestral [Nguni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguni_languages "Nguni languages") and [Sotho-Tswana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sotho%E2%80%93Tswana_languages "Sotho–Tswana languages") languages—migrated across the [Limpopo River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limpopo_River "Limpopo River") and came to dominate the earlier farmers both politically and culturally, organising into loosely-defined [chiefdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiefdoms "Chiefdoms") that held dynamic alliance networks. Further south, the Dutch began colonizing the [western Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Cape "Western Cape") in the 17th century, and Britain seized control of [the colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Cape_Colony "Dutch Cape Colony") around the turn of the 19th century. In the late-18th century, the interplay of pre-existing trends of political centralization with the effects of international trade, environmental instability, and European colonisation set the *[Mfecane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mfecane "Mfecane")* ("Crushing") in motion, as various African states were founded.

## c. 1800 – c. 1935

By the turn of the 19th century, many of Africa's economic and political structures were unstable and brittle as a result of long-term effects of the slave trade. In Egypt [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt "Muhammad Ali of Egypt") led a national movement to seize power from Ottoman [suzerainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerainty "Suzerainty"), instituting sweeping reforms and greatly strengthening Egyptian power by militaristic expansion. Meanwhile, in West Africa the [Fula *jihad*s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fula_jihads "Fula jihads"), which had been gaining momentum throughout the 18th century, culminated in the establishment of the expansive [Sokoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate "Sokoto Caliphate") and [Hamdullahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate_of_Hamdullahi "Caliphate of Hamdullahi") caliphates. In the Horn, [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Empire "Ethiopian Empire") underwent reforms, seeking to revive the imperial state, something that was later achieved by [Menelik II's conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II%27s_conquests "Menelik II's conquests"). In Southern Africa the *[Mfecane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mfecane "Mfecane")* propelled large long-distance migrations that caused widespread disruption across the vast region, while several [Boer republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boer_republics "Boer republics") were founded.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] European campaigns to abolish the [Atlantic slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade "Atlantic slave trade") led to supply refocusing on internal markets and saw the rapid growth of the [Indian Ocean slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_slave_trade "Indian Ocean slave trade"). Across the continent many rulers attempted reforms and indigenous forms of '[modernisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernisation "Modernisation")', however these efforts were consistently undermined by European traders and missionaries as the tide of European encroachment rose.

For 400 years, European nations had mainly limited their involvement to trading stations on the African coast, with few daring to venture inland. The [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution "Second Industrial Revolution") in Europe produced several technological innovations which assisted them in overcoming this 400-year pattern. One was the development of [repeating rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeating_rifle "Repeating rifle"), which were easier and quicker to load than [muskets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musket "Musket"). [Artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery "Artillery") was being used increasingly. In 1885, [Hiram S. Maxim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiram_S._Maxim "Hiram S. Maxim") developed the [maxim gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun "Maxim gun"), the model of the modern-day [machine gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_gun "Machine gun"). European states kept these weapons largely among themselves by refusing to sell these weapons to African leaders. African germs took numerous European lives and deterred [permanent settlements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler_colonialism "Settler colonialism"). Diseases such as [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever "Yellow fever"), [sleeping sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis "African trypanosomiasis"), [yaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaws "Yaws"), and [leprosy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprosy "Leprosy") made Africa a very inhospitable place for Europeans. The deadliest disease was [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria"), endemic throughout [Tropical Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_Africa "Tropical Africa"). In 1854, the discovery of [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine") and other medical innovations helped to make conquest and colonisation in Africa possible.

There were strong motives for conquest of Africa. [Raw materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources_of_Africa "Natural resources of Africa") were needed for European factories, and [free trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade "Free trade") was championed to prevent Africans from regulating their economies. Prestige and imperial rivalries were at play. Acquiring African colonies would show rivals that a nation was powerful and significant. These contextual factors forged the [Scramble for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa "Scramble for Africa"); during the late-19th and early-20th centuries the European powers [partitioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference "Berlin Conference") and conquered almost all of Africa despite fierce resistance (only Ethiopia and [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia "Liberia") remained independent). Traditional leaders were incorporated into the colonial regimes as a form of [indirect rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_rule "Indirect rule") to extract human and natural resources and curb organized resistance. Colonial borders were drawn unilaterally by the Europeans, often cutting across bonds of kinship, language, culture, and established routes, and sometimes incorporating groups who previously had little in common. The threat to trade routes was mitigated by poor policing and African entrepreneurs (viewed as smugglers) who exploited the differing tax and legal schemes. The first few decades of European colonialism saw intense violence, disorder, and atrocities, such as that of the [Congo Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State "Atrocities in the Congo Free State"), [German South West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide "Herero and Nama genocide"), and [French Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_genocide "Algerian genocide"), with the late colonial period relatively peaceful in comparison. Colonial rule had [wide-reaching effects on African societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_European_colonialism_and_colonization "Analysis of European colonialism and colonization"), such as the [spread of Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_colonialism "Christianity and colonialism") as part of the '[civilising mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilising_mission "Civilising mission")'. [African culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_culture "African culture") was strongly denigrated and persecuted, as pervasive racial discrimination deepened the scars of humiliation.

## c. 1935 – present

[Imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism "Imperialism") ruled until after World War II when forces of [African nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_nationalism "African nationalism") grew stronger. In the 1950s and 1960s the colonial holdings became independent states. The process was usually peaceful but there were several long bitter bloody civil wars, as in Algeria, Kenya, and elsewhere. Across Africa the powerful new force of [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism") drew upon the [advanced militaristic skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_warfare "Modern warfare") that natives learned during the world wars serving in the British, French, and other armies. It led to organizations that were not controlled by or endorsed by either the colonial powers nor the [traditional local power structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_current_non-sovereign_African_monarchs "List of current non-sovereign African monarchs") who were viewed as collaborators. Nationalistic organizations began to challenge both the traditional and the new colonial structures, and finally displaced them. Leaders of nationalist movements took control when the European authorities evacuated; many ruled for decades or until they died. In recent decades, many African countries have undergone the triumph and defeat of nationalistic fervour, changing in the process the loci of the centralizing state power and patrimonial state.

The wave of [decolonization of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonization_of_Africa "Decolonization of Africa") started with [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libya "Libya") in 1951, although [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia "Liberia"), [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa "South Africa"), [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") and [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia "Ethiopia") were already independent. Many countries followed in the 1950s and 1960s, with a peak in 1960 with the [Year of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_Africa "Year of Africa"), which saw 17 African nations declare independence, including a large part of [French West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa "French West Africa"). Most of the remaining countries gained independence throughout the 1960s, although some colonizers (Portugal in particular) were reluctant to relinquish sovereignty, resulting in bitter wars of independence which lasted for a decade or more.

The last African countries to gain formal independence were [Guinea-Bissau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea-Bissau "Guinea-Bissau") (1974), [Mozambique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique "Mozambique") (1975) and [Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angola "Angola") (1975) from Portugal; [Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti "Djibouti") from France in 1977; Zimbabwe from the United Kingdom in 1980; and [Namibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibia "Namibia") from South Africa in 1990. Eritrea later split off from Ethiopia in 1993. The nascent countries, despite some prior talk of redrawing borders, decided to keep their colonial borders in the [Organisation of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity "Organisation of African Unity") (OAU) conference of 1964 due to fears of civil wars and regional instability, and placed emphasis on [Pan-Africanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Africanism "Pan-Africanism"), with the OAU later developing into the [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union "African Union"). During the 1990s and early 2000s there were the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War "First Congo War") and [Second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Congo_War "Second Congo War") Congo Wars, often termed the African World Wars.
