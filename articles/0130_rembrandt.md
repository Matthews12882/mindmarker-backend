# Rembrandt

**Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn** (15 July 1606 – 4 October 1669), known mononymously as **Rembrandt**, was a [Dutch Golden Age painter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Golden_Age_painter "Dutch Golden Age painter"), [printmaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printmaker "Printmaker"), and [draughtsman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing "Drawing"). He is generally considered one of the greatest [visual artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_artists "Visual artists") in the history of [Western art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Europe "Art of Europe"). It is estimated that Rembrandt's surviving works amount to about three hundred paintings, three hundred etchings, and several hundred drawings.

Unlike most Dutch painters of the 17th century, Rembrandt's works depict a wide range of styles and subject matter, from [portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait_painting "Portrait painting") and self-portraits to landscapes, [genre scenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genre_scenes "Genre scenes"), allegorical and historical scenes, biblical and mythological subjects and animal studies. His contributions to art came in a period that historians call the [Dutch Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Golden_Age "Dutch Golden Age").

Rembrandt never went abroad, but was considerably influenced by the work of the Italian [Old Masters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Masters "Old Masters") and [Dutch and Flemish artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bentvueghels "Bentvueghels") who had studied in Italy. After he achieved youthful success as a portrait painter, Rembrandt's later years were marked by personal tragedy and financial hardships. Still, his etchings and paintings were popular throughout his lifetime; his reputation as an artist remained high, and for twenty years he taught many important Dutch painters. Rembrandt's portraits of his contemporaries, self-portraits and illustrations of scenes from the Bible are regarded as his greatest creative triumphs. His approximately 100 [self-portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-portraits_by_Rembrandt "Self-portraits by Rembrandt"), over 40 of which are paintings, form an intimate autobiography.

## Early life and education

Rembrandt Harmenszoon (abbr. Harmensz.) van Rijn was born on 15 July 1606 in Leiden, in the [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic "Dutch Republic"), now the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands "Netherlands"). He was the ninth child born to Harmen Gerritszoon van Rijn and Neeltgen Willemsdochter van Zuijtbrouck. His family was quite well-to-do; his father was a [miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller "Miller") and his mother was a baker's daughter. His mother was [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic "Catholic"), and his father belonged to the [Dutch Reformed Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Reformed_Church "Dutch Reformed Church"). Religion is a central theme in Rembrandt's works and the religiously fraught period in which he lived makes his faith a matter of interest.

As a boy, he attended a [Latin school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stedelijk_Gymnasium_Leiden "Stedelijk Gymnasium Leiden"). In 1620, he was enrolled at [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University "Leiden University"), although he had a greater inclination towards painting and was soon apprenticed to [Jacob van Swanenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_van_Swanenburg "Jacob van Swanenburg"), with whom he spent three years. After a brief but important apprenticeship of six months with the [history painter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_painter "History painter") [Pieter Lastman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Lastman "Pieter Lastman") in Amsterdam, Rembrandt stayed a few months with [Jacob Pynas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Pynas "Jacob Pynas") in 1625, though [Simon van Leeuwen](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_van_Leeuwen "nl:Simon van Leeuwen") claimed that Rembrandt was taught by [Joris van Schooten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joris_van_Schooten "Joris van Schooten") and then started his own workshop.

## Career

In 1625, Rembrandt opened a studio in Leiden, which he shared with friend and colleague [Jan Lievens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Lievens "Jan Lievens"). In 1627, Rembrandt began to accept students, among them [Gerrit Dou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrit_Dou "Gerrit Dou") and [Isaac de Jouderville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_de_Jouderville "Isaac de Jouderville"). [Joan Huydecoper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Huydecoper_van_Maarsseveen_(1599%E2%80%931661) "Joan Huydecoper van Maarsseveen (1599–1661)") is mentioned as the first buyer of a Rembrandt painting in 1628. In 1629, Rembrandt was discovered by the statesman [Constantijn Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantijn_Huygens "Constantijn Huygens") who procured for Rembrandt important commissions from the court of The Hague. As a result of this connection, Prince [Frederik Hendrik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederik_Hendrik "Frederik Hendrik") continued to purchase paintings from Rembrandt.

At the end of 1631, Rembrandt moved to Amsterdam, a city rapidly expanding as the business and trade capital. He began to practice as a professional portraitist for the first time, with great success. He initially stayed with an art dealer, [Hendrick van Uylenburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrick_van_Uylenburgh "Hendrick van Uylenburgh"), and in 1634, married Hendrick's cousin, [Saskia van Uylenburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskia_van_Uylenburgh "Saskia van Uylenburgh"). Saskia came from a respected family: her father [Rombertus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rombertus_van_Uylenburgh "Rombertus van Uylenburgh") was a lawyer and had been *[burgomaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgomaster "Burgomaster")* (mayor) of Leeuwarden. The couple married in the local church of [St. Annaparochie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Annaparochie "St. Annaparochie") without the presence of Rembrandt's relatives. In the same year, Rembrandt became a [citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poorter "Poorter") of Amsterdam and a member of the local [guild of painters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_of_painters "Guild of painters"). He also acquired a number of students, among them [Ferdinand Bol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Bol "Ferdinand Bol") and [Govert Flinck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Govert_Flinck "Govert Flinck").

In 1635, Rembrandt and Saskia rented a fashionable lodging with a view of the river Amstel. In 1637, Rembrandt moved upriver to [Vlooienburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vlooienburg "Vlooienburg"), in a building on the previous site of the current [Stopera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stopera "Stopera"). In May 1639 they moved to a recently modernised house in the upscale ['Breestraat'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodenbreestraat "Jodenbreestraat") with artists and art dealers; [Nicolaes Pickenoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaes_Pickenoy "Nicolaes Pickenoy"), a portrait painter, was his neighbour. The [mortgage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortgage_loan "Mortgage loan") to finance the 13,000 [guilder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilder "Guilder") purchase would be a cause for later financial difficulties. The neighborhood sheltered many immigrants and was becoming the Jewish quarter. It was there that Rembrandt frequently sought his [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") neighbours to model for his [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") scenes.
One of the great patrons at the early stages of his career was Amsterdam statesman [Andries de Graeff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andries_de_Graeff "Andries de Graeff").

Although they were by now affluent, the couple suffered several personal setbacks; three children died within weeks of their births. Only their fourth child, [Titus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titus_van_Rijn "Titus van Rijn"), who was born in 1641, survived into adulthood. Saskia died in 1642, probably from [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis"). Rembrandt's drawings of her on her sick and death bed are among his most moving works. After Saskia's illness, the widow [Geertje Dircx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geertje_Dircx "Geertje Dircx") was hired as Titus's caretaker and dry nurse; at some time, she also became Rembrandt's lover. In May 1649 she left and charged Rembrandt with [breach of promise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breach_of_promise "Breach of promise") and asked to be awarded [alimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alimony "Alimony"). Rembrandt tried to settle the matter amicably, but to pay her lawyer she pawned the diamond ring he had given her that once belonged to Saskia. On 14 October they came to an agreement; the court particularly stated that Rembrandt had to pay a yearly maintenance allowance, provided that Titus remained her only heir and she sold none of Rembrandt's possessions. As Dircx broke her promise, Rembrandt and members of Dircx's own family had her committed to a women's house of correction at [Gouda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gouda,_South_Holland "Gouda, South Holland") in August 1650. Rembrandt also took measures to ensure she stayed in the house of correction for as long as possible. Rembrandt paid for the costs.

In early 1649, Rembrandt began a relationship with the 23-year-old [Hendrickje Stoffels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrickje_Stoffels "Hendrickje Stoffels"), who had initially been his maid. She may have been the cause of Geertje's leaving. In that year he made no (dated) paintings or etchings at all. In 1654 Rembrandt painted a nude [Bathsheba at Her Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathsheba_at_Her_Bath_(Rembrandt) "Bathsheba at Her Bath (Rembrandt)"). In June Hendrickje received three summonses from the Reformed Church to answer the charge "that she had committed the acts of a whore with Rembrandt the painter". In July she admitted her guilt and was banned from receiving [communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist "Eucharist"). Rembrandt was not summoned to appear for the Church council. In October they had a daughter, Cornelia. Had he remarried he would have lost access to a trust set up for Titus in Saskia's will.

### Insolvency

Rembrandt, despite his artistic success, found himself in financial turmoil. His penchant for acquiring art, prints, and rare items led him to live beyond his means. In January 1653 the sale of the property formally was finalised but Rembrandt still had to cover half of the remaining mortgage. Creditors began pressing for [instalments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hire_purchase "Hire purchase") but Rembrandt, facing financial strain, sought a postponement. The house required repairs, prompting Rembrandt to borrow money from friends, among them [Jan Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Six "Jan Six").

In November 1655, amid a year overshadowed by [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_(disease) "Plague (disease)") and the drafting of wills, Rembrandt's 14-year-old son Titus took a significant step by drafting a will that designated his father as the sole heir, effectively sidelining his mother's family. In December Rembrandt orchestrated a sale of his paintings, yet the earnings failed to meet expectations. This tumultuous period deeply impacted the art industry, prompting Rembrandt to seek a high court arrangement known as *[cessio bonorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cessio_bonorum "Cessio bonorum")*. Despite his financial difficulties, Rembrandt's bankruptcy was not forced. In July 1656, he declared his [insolvency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insolvency "Insolvency"), taking stock and willingly surrendered his assets. Notably, he had already transferred the house to his son. Both the authorities and his creditors showed leniency, granting him ample time to settle his debts. [Jacob J. Hinlopen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_J._Hinlopen#Jacob_J._Hinlopen,_son "Jacob J. Hinlopen") allegedly played a role.

In November 1657 another auction was held to sell his paintings, as well as a substantial number of etching plates and drawings, some of the latter by famous artists, including [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael "Raphael"), [Mantegna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Mantegna "Andrea Mantegna") and [Giorgione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgione "Giorgione"). Remarkably, Rembrandt was permitted to retain his tools as a means of generating income. Rembrandt lost the guardianship of his son and thus control over his actions. A new guardian, Louis Crayers, claimed the house in settlement of Titus's debt.

The sale list comprising 363 items offers insight into Rembrandt's diverse collections, which encompassed [Old Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Master "Old Master") paintings, drawings, busts of [Roman emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperors "Roman emperors"), statues of [Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophers "Greek philosophers"), books (including a bible), two [globes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe "Globe"), bonnets, [armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armour "Armour"), and various objects from Asia ([porcelain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porcelain "Porcelain")), as well as a collections of [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history "Natural history") specimens (two lion skins, a [bird-of-paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird-of-paradise "Bird-of-paradise"), [corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corals "Corals") and minerals). However, the sale prices were disappointing.

By February 1658, Rembrandt' house had been sold at a [foreclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreclosure "Foreclosure") auction, and the family moved to more modest lodgings at [Rozengracht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rozengracht "Rozengracht"). In 1660, he finished *[Ahasuerus and Haman at the feast of Esther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahasuerus_and_Haman_at_the_feast_of_Esther "Ahasuerus and Haman at the feast of Esther")*, which he sold to [Jan J. Hinlopen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_J._Hinlopen "Jan J. Hinlopen"). In early December 1660, the sale of the house was finalised but the proceeds went directly to Titus's guardian.

Two weeks later, Hendrickje and Titus established a [dummy corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dummy_corporation "Dummy corporation") as art dealers, allowing Rembrandt, who had [board and lodging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_and_board "Room and board"), to continue his artistic pursuits. In 1661, they secured a contract for a major project at the newly completed [town hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Palace_(Amsterdam) "Royal Palace (Amsterdam)"). The resulting work, *[The Conspiracy of Claudius Civilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Conspiracy_of_Claudius_Civilis "The Conspiracy of Claudius Civilis")*, was rejected by [the mayors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Amsterdam#17th_century "List of mayors of Amsterdam") and returned to the painter within a few weeks; the surviving fragment (in Stockholm) is only a quarter of the original.

Despite these setbacks, Rembrandt continued to receive significant portrait commissions and completed notable works, such as the *[Sampling Officials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_Officials "Sampling Officials")* in 1662. It remains a challenge to gauge Rembrandt's wealth accurately as he may have overestimated the value of his art collection. Nonetheless, half of his assets were earmarked for Titus's inheritance.

In March 1663, with Hendrickje's illness, Titus assumed a more prominent role. Isaac van Hertsbeeck, Rembrandt's primary creditor, went to the [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hof_van_Holland "Hof van Holland") and contested Titus's priority for payment, leading to legal battles that Titus ultimately won in 1665 when he came of age. During this time, Rembrandt worked on notable pieces like *[The Jewish Bride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jewish_Bride "The Jewish Bride")* and his final self-portraits, but struggled with rent arrears. Notably, [Cosimo III de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosimo_III_de%27_Medici,_Grand_Duke_of_Tuscany "Cosimo III de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany"), visited Rembrandt twice, and returned to Florence with one of the self-portraits.

Rembrandt outlived both Hendrickje and Titus; he died on Friday, 4 October 1669, and was buried four days later in a rented grave in the [Westerkerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerkerk#Rembrandt "Westerkerk"). His [illegitimate child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illegitimate_child "Illegitimate child"), Cornelia (1654–1684), eventually moved to [Batavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavia,_Dutch_East_Indies "Batavia, Dutch East Indies") in 1670, accompanied by an obscure painter and her mother's inheritance. Titus's considerable inheritance passed to his only child, Titia (1669-1715) who married her cousin and lived at [Blauwburgwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blauwburgwal "Blauwburgwal"). Rembrandt's life was marked by more than just artistic achievements; he navigated numerous legal and financial challenges, leaving a complex legacy.

## Works

### Overview

In a letter to Huygens, Rembrandt offered the only surviving explanation of what he sought to achieve through his art, writing that, "the greatest and most natural movement", translated from *de meeste en de natuurlijkste beweegelijkheid*. The word "*beweegelijkheid*" translates to "emotion" or "motive". Whether this refers to objectives, material, or something else, is not known but critics have drawn particular attention to the way Rembrandt seamlessly melded the earthly and spiritual.

As connoisseurship developed in the 19th and 20th centuries, Rembrandt's oeuvre increased in size. By the early 20th century, connoisseurs thought Rembrandt had produced well over 600 paintings, nearly 400 etchings and 2,000 drawings. More recent scholarship, from the 1960s to the present day (led by the [Rembrandt Research Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembrandt_Research_Project "Rembrandt Research Project")), often controversially, has winnowed his oeuvre to nearer 300 paintings. His [prints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_master_print "Old master print"), traditionally all called etchings, although many are produced in whole or part by [engraving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraving "Engraving") and sometimes [drypoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drypoint "Drypoint"), have a much more stable total of slightly under 300. It is likely Rembrandt made many more drawings in his lifetime than 2,000 but those extant are rarer than presumed. Two experts claim that the number of drawings whose autograph status can be regarded as effectively "certain" is no higher than about 75, although this is disputed. The list was to be unveiled at a scholarly meeting in February 2010.

At one time, approximately 90 paintings were counted as [Rembrandt self-portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-portraits_by_Rembrandt "Self-portraits by Rembrandt") but it is now known that he had his students copy his own self-portraits as part of their training. Modern scholarship has reduced the autograph count to over forty paintings, as well as a few drawings and thirty-one etchings, which include many of the "most remarkable" images of the group. Some show him posing in quasi-historical fancy dress, or pulling faces at himself. His oil paintings trace the progress from an uncertain young man, through the dapper and very successful portrait-painter of the 1630s, to the troubled but massively powerful portraits of his old age. Together they are believed to give a remarkably clear picture of the man, his appearance and his psychological make-up, as revealed by his richly weathered face.

In his portraits and self-portraits, he angles the sitter's face in such a way that the ridge of the nose nearly always forms the line of demarcation between brightly illuminated and shadowy areas. A Rembrandt face is a face partially eclipsed; and the nose, bright and obvious, thrusting into the riddle of halftones, serves to focus the viewer's attention upon, and to dramatise, the division between a flood of light—an overwhelming clarity—and a brooding duskiness.

In some of his biblical works, including *The Raising of the Cross*, *Joseph Telling His Dreams*, and *[The Stoning of Saint Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stoning_of_Saint_Stephen "The Stoning of Saint Stephen")*, Rembrandt painted himself as a character in the crowd. Durham suggests that this was because the Bible was for Rembrandt "a kind of diary, an account of moments in his own life".

Among the more prominent characteristics of Rembrandt's work are his use of [chiaroscuro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiaroscuro "Chiaroscuro"), the theatrical employment of light and shadow derived from [Caravaggio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravaggio "Caravaggio"), or, more likely, from the [Dutch Caravaggisti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utrecht_Caravaggism "Utrecht Caravaggism") but adapted for very personal means. Also notable are his dramatic and lively presentation of subjects, devoid of the rigid formality that his contemporaries often displayed, and a deeply felt compassion for mankind, irrespective of wealth and age. His immediate family—his wife Saskia, his son Titus and his common-law wife Hendrickje—often figured prominently in his paintings, many of which had [mythical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythology "Mythology"), biblical or historical subjects.

### Periods, subjects and styles

Throughout his career, Rembrandt took as his most common subjects portraits, narrative or "[history paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_painting "History painting")", mostly biblical, and [landscapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_painting "Landscape painting"). He was especially praised by his contemporaries for his biblical subjects, for his skill in representing emotions, and attention to detail. Stylistically, his paintings progressed from the early "smooth" manner, characterised by fine technique in the portrayal of illusionistic form, to the late "rough" treatment of richly variegated paint surfaces, which allowed for an illusionism of form suggested by the tactile quality of the paint itself. Rembrandt must have realised that if he kept the paint deliberately loose and "paint-like" on some parts of the canvas, the perception of space became much greater.

A parallel development may be seen in Rembrandt's skill as a printmaker. In the etchings of his maturity, particularly from the late 1640s onward, the freedom and breadth of his drawings and paintings found expression in the print medium as well. The works encompass a wide range of subject matter and technique, sometimes leaving large areas of white paper to suggest space, at other times employing complex webs of line to produce rich dark tones.

Lastman's influence on Rembrandt was most prominent during his period in Leiden from 1625 to 1631. Paintings were rather small but rich in details (for example, in costumes and jewelry). Religious and [allegorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory "Allegory") subjects were favoured, as were [tronies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tronie "Tronie"). In 1626 Rembrandt produced his first etchings, the wide dissemination of which would largely account for his international fame. In 1629, he completed *[Judas Repentant, Returning the Pieces of Silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judas_Repentant,_Returning_the_Pieces_of_Silver "Judas Repentant, Returning the Pieces of Silver")* and *[The Artist in His Studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artist_in_his_studio "Artist in his studio")*, works that evidence his interest in the handling of light and variety of paint application and constitute the first major progress in his development as a painter.

During his early years in Amsterdam (1632–1636), Rembrandt began to paint dramatic biblical and mythological scenes in high contrast and of large format (*[The Blinding of Samson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blinding_of_Samson "The Blinding of Samson")*, 1636, *[Belshazzar's Feast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belshazzar%27s_Feast_(Rembrandt) "Belshazzar's Feast (Rembrandt)")*, c. 1635 *[Danaë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana%C3%AB_(Rembrandt) "Danaë (Rembrandt)")*, 1636 but reworked later), seeking to emulate the baroque style of [Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubens "Rubens"). With the occasional help of assistants in Uylenburgh's workshop, he painted numerous portrait commissions both small (*[Jacob de Gheyn III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_de_Gheyn_III_(painting) "Jacob de Gheyn III (painting)")*) and large (*Portrait of the Shipbuilder Jan Rijcksen and his Wife*, 1633, *[Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy_Lesson_of_Dr._Nicolaes_Tulp "Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp")*, 1632).

By the late 1630s, Rembrandt had produced a few paintings and many etchings of [landscapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_painting "Landscape painting"). Often these landscapes highlighted natural drama, featuring uprooted trees and ominous skies (*Cottages before a Stormy Sky*, c. 1641; *[The Three Trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Trees "The Three Trees")*, 1643). From 1640 his work became less exuberant and more sober in tone, possibly reflecting personal tragedy. Biblical scenes were now derived more often from [Christianity’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament") than [Judaism’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism") [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible"), as had been the case before. In 1642 he painted *[The Night Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Night_Watch "The Night Watch")*, the most substantial of the important group portrait commissions which he received in this period, and through which he sought to find solutions to compositional and narrative problems that had been attempted in previous works.

In the decade following *[The Night Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Night_Watch "The Night Watch")*, Rembrandt's paintings varied greatly in size, subject, and style. The previous tendency to create dramatic effects primarily by strong contrasts of light and shadow gave way to the use of frontal lighting and larger and more saturated areas of colour. Simultaneously, figures came to be placed parallel to the picture plane. These changes can be seen as a move toward a classical mode of composition and, considering the more expressive use of brushwork as well, may indicate a familiarity with Venetian art (*[Susanna and the Elders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susanna_and_the_Elders_(Rembrandt) "Susanna and the Elders (Rembrandt)")*, 1637–47). At the same time, there was a marked decrease in painted works in favour of etchings and drawings of landscapes.

In the 1650s, Rembrandt's style changed again. Colours became richer and brush strokes more pronounced. With these changes, Rembrandt distanced himself from earlier work and current fashion, which increasingly inclined toward fine, detailed works. His use of light becomes more jagged and harsh, and shine becomes almost nonexistent. His singular approach to paint application may have been suggested in part by familiarity with the work of [Titian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titian "Titian"), and could be seen in the context of the then current discussion of 'finish' and surface quality of paintings. Contemporary accounts sometimes remark disapprovingly of the coarseness of Rembrandt's brushwork, and the artist himself was said to have dissuaded visitors from looking too closely at his paintings. The tactile manipulation of paint may hearken to medieval procedures, when mimetic effects of rendering informed a painting's surface. The result is a richly varied handling of paint, deeply layered and often apparently haphazard, which suggests form and space in both an illusory and highly individual manner.

In later years, [biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible") subjects were often depicted but emphasis shifted from dramatic group scenes to intimate portrait-like figures (*James the Apostle*, 1661). In his last years, Rembrandt painted his most deeply reflective self-portraits (from 1652 to 1669 he painted fifteen), and several moving images of both men and women (*[The Jewish Bride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jewish_Bride "The Jewish Bride")*, c. 1666)—in love, in life, and before God.

### Graphic works

Rembrandt produced [etchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etching "Etching") for most of his career, from 1626 to 1660, when he was forced to sell his printing-press and practically abandoned etching. Only the troubled year of 1649 produced no dated work. He took easily to etching and, though he learned to use a [burin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burin_(engraving) "Burin (engraving)") and partly [engraved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraving "Engraving") many plates, the freedom of etching technique was fundamental to his work. He was very closely involved in the whole process of printmaking, and must have printed at least early examples of his etchings himself. At first he used a style based on drawing but soon moved to one based on painting, using a mass of lines and numerous bitings with the acid to achieve different strengths of line. Towards the end of the 1630s, he reacted against this manner and moved to a simpler style, with fewer bitings.

He worked on the so-called *[Hundred Guilder Print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Guilder_Print "Hundred Guilder Print")* in stages throughout the 1640s, and it was the "critical work in the middle of his career", from which his final etching style began to emerge. Although the print only survives in two [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(printmaking) "State (printmaking)"), the first very rare, evidence of much reworking can be seen underneath the final print and many drawings survive for elements of it.

In the mature works of the 1650s, Rembrandt was more ready to improvise on the plate and large prints typically survive in several states, up to eleven, often radically changed. He now used [hatching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatching "Hatching") to create his dark areas, which often take up much of the plate. He also experimented with the effects of printing on different kinds of paper, including [Japanese paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_paper "Japanese paper"), which he used frequently, and on [vellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vellum "Vellum"). He began to use "[surface tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tone "Surface tone")", leaving a thin film of ink on parts of the plate instead of wiping it completely clean to print each impression. He made more use of [drypoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drypoint "Drypoint"), exploiting, especially in landscapes, the rich fuzzy burr that this technique gives to the first few impressions.

His prints have similar subjects to his paintings, although the 27 self-portraits are relatively more common, and portraits of other people less so. The landscapes, mostly small, largely set the course for the graphic treatment of landscape until the end of the 19th century. Of the many hundreds of drawings Rembrandt made, only about two hundred have a landscape motif as their subject, and of the approximately three hundred etchings, about thirty show a landscape. As for his painted landscapes, one does not even get beyond eight works. One third of his etchings are of religious subjects, many treated with a homely simplicity, whilst others are his most monumental prints. A few erotic, or just obscene, compositions have no equivalent in his paintings. Rembrandt owned, until forced to sell it, a magnificent collection of works by other artists. He was influenced by artists including [Caravaggio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravaggio "Caravaggio") with his chiaroscuro lighting. Borrowings and influences in his work can be traced to artists as diverse as [Andrea Mantegna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Mantegna "Andrea Mantegna") (with his *Entombment*), [Anthony van Dyck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_van_Dyck "Anthony van Dyck"), [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael "Raphael"), [Titian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titian "Titian"), [Peter Paul Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens "Peter Paul Rubens"), [Hercules Seghers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hercules_Seghers "Hercules Seghers"), and [Giovanni Benedetto Castiglione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Benedetto_Castiglione "Giovanni Benedetto Castiglione").

Drawings by Rembrandt and [his pupils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Rembrandt_pupils "List of Rembrandt pupils") have been extensively studied by many artists and scholars through the centuries.

### Asian inspiration

Rembrandt was interested in [Mughal miniatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_painting "Mughal painting"), especially around the 1650s. He drew versions of some 23 Mughal paintings and may have owned an album of them. These miniatures include paintings of [Shah Jahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Jahan "Shah Jahan"), [Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar "Akbar"), [Jahangir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahangir "Jahangir") and [Dara Shikoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dara_Shikoh "Dara Shikoh") and may have influenced the costumes and other aspects of his works.

### *The Night Watch*

Rembrandt painted *The Militia Company of Captain [Frans Banninck Cocq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frans_Banninck_Cocq "Frans Banninck Cocq")*, known as *[The Night Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Night_Watch "The Night Watch")*, between 1640 and 1642, and it became his most famous work. The piece was commissioned for the new hall of the *[Kloveniersdoelen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kloveniersdoelen,_Amsterdam "Kloveniersdoelen, Amsterdam")*, the musketeer branch of the [civic militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutterij "Schutterij"). Rembrandt departed from convention on both narrative painting and portraits, which ordered that such genre pieces should be stately and formal. Instead, he created a complex layering of figures in a dramatic depiction of an action, the firing of a musket, affecting some of the characters but not others. The painting is not set at night, its darkness being caused by ageing; and it is not of a watch or patrol, but a ceremony.

The painting has received many interpretations; if as Joseph Manca suggests it was meant to function at multiple levels, many of the interpretations may be correct. Thus, unlike in a conventional narrative painting, the people depicted are represented in lifelike individual portraits. The style seems to show a real event in a real place, but its complex structure appears contrived or theatrical, while the street setting is invented. It can be seen as a picture of a militia charged with keeping order, but it equally looks like a disorderly scene. It alludes to serious works like *[The School of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_School_of_Athens "The School of Athens")* by [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael "Raphael"), and has been seen as humorous or parodic. Manca suggests that the calmness of the two officers in the foreground, continuing to carry out their duty despite the disturbance behind them, indicates their "moral excellence"; certainly, their status is clearly indicated, even flattered.

## Expert assessments

In 1968, the Rembrandt Research Project began under the sponsorship of the Netherlands Organisation for the Advancement of Scientific Research; it was initially expected to last a highly optimistic ten years. Art historians teamed up with experts from other fields to reassess the authenticity of works attributed to Rembrandt, using all methods available, including state-of-the-art technical diagnostics, and to compile a complete new [catalogue raisonné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalogue_raisonn%C3%A9 "Catalogue raisonné") of his paintings. As a result of their findings, many paintings that were previously attributed to Rembrandt have been removed from their list, although others have been added back.

One example of activity is *[The Polish Rider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Polish_Rider "The Polish Rider")*, now in the [Frick Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frick_Collection "Frick Collection") in [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). Rembrandt's authorship had been questioned by at least one scholar, Alfred von Wurzbach, at the beginning of the twentieth century but for many decades later most scholars, including the foremost authority writing in English, [Julius S. Held](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_S._Held "Julius S. Held"), agreed that it was indeed by the master. In the 1980s, however, Dr Josua Bruyn of the Foundation Rembrandt Research Project cautiously and tentatively attributed the painting to one of Rembrandt's closest and most talented pupils, [Willem Drost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_Drost "Willem Drost"), about whom little is known. But Bruyn's remained a minority opinion, the suggestion of Drost's authorship is now generally rejected, and the Frick itself never changed its own attribution, the label still reading "Rembrandt" and not "attributed to" or "school of". More recent opinion has shifted even more decisively in favour of the Frick; In his 1999 book *Rembrandt's Eyes*, [Simon Schama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Schama "Simon Schama") and the Rembrandt Project scholar [Ernst van de Wetering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_van_de_Wetering "Ernst van de Wetering") (Melbourne Symposium, 1997) both argued for attribution to the master. Those few scholars who still question Rembrandt's authorship feel that the execution is uneven and favour different attributions for different parts of the work.

A similar issue was raised by Schama concerning the verification of titles associated with the subject matter depicted in Rembrandt's works. For example, the exact subject being portrayed in *[Aristotle with a Bust of Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle_with_a_Bust_of_Homer "Aristotle with a Bust of Homer")*, recently retitled by curators at the Metropolitan Museum, has been directly challenged by Schama applying the scholarship of Paul Crenshaw. Schama presents a substantial argument that it was the famous ancient Greek painter [Apelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apelles "Apelles") who is depicted in contemplation by Rembrandt and not Aristotle.

Another painting, *Pilate Washing His Hands*, is also of questionable attribution. Critical opinion of this picture has varied since 1905, when [Wilhelm von Bode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_von_Bode "Wilhelm von Bode") described it as "a somewhat abnormal work" by Rembrandt. "Most scholars since the 1940s have dated the painting to the 1660s and assigned it to an anonymous pupil," possibly Aert de Gelder. The composition bears superficial resemblance to mature works by Rembrandt but lacks the master's command of illumination and modelling.

The attribution and re-attribution work is ongoing. In 2005 four oil paintings previously attributed to Rembrandt's students were reclassified as the work of Rembrandt himself: *Study of an Old Man in Profile* and *Study of an Old Man with a Beard* from a US private collection, *[Study of a Weeping Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Study_of_a_Weeping_Woman "Study of a Weeping Woman")*, owned by the [Detroit Institute of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Institute_of_Arts "Detroit Institute of Arts"), and *Portrait of an Elderly Woman in a White Bonnet*, painted in 1640. The *Old Man Sitting in a Chair* is a further example: in 2014, Professor Ernst van de Wetering offered his view to *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")* that the demotion of the 1652 painting *Old Man Sitting in a Chair* "was a vast mistake...it is a most important painting. The painting needs to be seen in terms of Rembrandt's experimentation". This was highlighted much earlier by [Nigel Konstam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Konstam "Nigel Konstam") who studied Rembrandt throughout his career.

Rembrandt's own studio practice is a major factor in the difficulty of attribution, since, like many masters before him, he encouraged his students to copy his paintings, sometimes finishing or retouching them to be sold as originals, and sometimes selling them as authorised copies. Additionally, his style proved easy enough for his most talented students to emulate. Further complicating matters is the uneven quality of some of Rembrandt's own work, and his frequent stylistic evolutions and experiments. As well, there were later imitations of his work, and restorations which so seriously damaged the original works that they are no longer recognisable.

## Painting materials

Technical investigation of Rembrandt's paintings in the possession of the [Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gem%C3%A4ldegalerie_Alte_Meister "Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister") and in the [Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister (Kassel)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gem%C3%A4ldegalerie_Alte_Meister_(Kassel) "Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister (Kassel)") was conducted by Hermann Kühn in 1977. The pigment analyses of some thirty paintings have shown that Rembrandt's palette consisted of the following pigments: [lead white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_white "Lead white"), various [ochres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochres "Ochres"), [van Dyke brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Dyke_brown "Van Dyke brown"), bone black, [charcoal black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal_black "Charcoal black"), [lamp black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamp_black "Lamp black"), [vermilion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermilion "Vermilion"), [madder lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madder_lake "Madder lake"), [azurite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azurite "Azurite"), [ultramarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultramarine "Ultramarine"), yellow lake and [lead-tin-yellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead-tin-yellow "Lead-tin-yellow"). Synthetic [orpiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orpiment "Orpiment") was shown in the shadows of the sleeve of the jewish groom. This toxic arsenic yellow was rarely used in oil painting. One painting (Saskia van Uylenburgh as Flora) reportedly contains [gamboge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamboge "Gamboge"). Rembrandt very rarely used pure blue or green colours, the most pronounced exception being [*Belshazzar's Feast*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belshazzar%27s_Feast_(Rembrandt) "Belshazzar's Feast (Rembrandt)") in the [National Gallery in London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_in_London "National Gallery in London").

The book by Bomford describes more recent technical investigations and pigment analyses of Rembrandt's paintings predominantly in the National Gallery in London. The entire array of pigments employed by Rembrandt can be found at ColourLex. The best source for technical information on Rembrandt's paintings on the web is the Rembrandt Database containing all works of Rembrandt with detailed investigative reports, infrared and radiography images and other scientific details.

## Name and signature

"Rembrandt" is a modification of the spelling of the artist's first name that he introduced in 1633. The [patronymic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patronymic "Patronymic") "Harmenszoon" indicates that his father's name is Harmen. "van Rijn" indicates that his family lived near the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine "Rhine").

Rembrandt's earliest signatures (c. 1625) consisted of an initial "R", or the monogram "RH" (for Rembrant Harmenszoon), and starting in 1629, "RHL" (the "L" stood, presumably, for Leiden). In 1632, he used this monogram early in the year, then added his family name to it, "RHL-van Rijn" but replaced this form in that same year and began using his first name alone with its original spelling, "Rembrant". In 1633 he added a "d", and maintained this form consistently from then on, proving that this minor change had a meaning for him (whatever it might have been). This change is purely visual; it does not change the way his name is pronounced. Despite the large number of paintings and etchings signed with this modified first name, most of the documents that mentioned him during his lifetime retained the original "Rembrant" spelling. (Note: the rough chronology of signature forms above applies to the paintings, and to a lesser degree to the etchings; from 1632, presumably, there is only one etching signed "RHL-v. Rijn", the large-format "Raising of Lazarus", B 73).

His practice of signing his work with his first name, later followed by [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh "Vincent van Gogh"), was probably inspired by [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael "Raphael"), [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci "Leonardo da Vinci") and [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo "Michelangelo") who, then as now, were referred to by their first names alone.

## Workshop

Rembrandt ran a large workshop and had many pupils. The [list of Rembrandt pupils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Rembrandt_pupils "List of Rembrandt pupils") from his period in Leiden as well as his time in Amsterdam is quite long, mostly because his influence on painters around him was so great that it is difficult to tell whether someone worked for him in his studio or just copied his style for patrons eager to acquire a Rembrandt. A partial list should include [Ferdinand Bol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Bol "Ferdinand Bol"), [Adriaen Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriaen_Brouwer "Adriaen Brouwer"), [Gerrit Dou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrit_Dou "Gerrit Dou"), [Willem Drost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_Drost "Willem Drost"), [Heiman Dullaart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heiman_Dullaart "Heiman Dullaart"), [Gerbrand van den Eeckhout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerbrand_van_den_Eeckhout "Gerbrand van den Eeckhout"), [Carel Fabritius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carel_Fabritius "Carel Fabritius"), [Govert Flinck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Govert_Flinck "Govert Flinck"), [Hendrick Fromantiou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrick_Fromantiou "Hendrick Fromantiou"), [Aert de Gelder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aert_de_Gelder "Aert de Gelder"), [Samuel Dirksz van Hoogstraten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Dirksz_van_Hoogstraten "Samuel Dirksz van Hoogstraten"), [Abraham Janssens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Janssens "Abraham Janssens"), [Godfrey Kneller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_Kneller "Godfrey Kneller"), [Philip de Koninck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_de_Koninck "Philip de Koninck"), [Jacob Levecq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Levecq "Jacob Levecq"), [Nicolaes Maes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaes_Maes "Nicolaes Maes"), [Jürgen Ovens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Ovens "Jürgen Ovens"), [Christopher Paudiß](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Paudi%C3%9F "Christopher Paudiß"), [Willem de Poorter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_de_Poorter "Willem de Poorter"), [Jan Victors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Victors "Jan Victors"), and [Willem van der Vliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_van_der_Vliet "Willem van der Vliet").

## Museum collections

The United States has the largest number of Rembrandt's paintings, spread over several museums including the [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art "Metropolitan Museum of Art") (mostly portraits) and the [Frick Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frick_Collection "Frick Collection") in New York City, the [National Gallery of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Art "National Gallery of Art") in Washington, D.C., [Museum of Fine Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Fine_Arts,_Boston "Museum of Fine Arts, Boston") in Boston, and [J. Paul Getty Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Paul_Getty_Museum "J. Paul Getty Museum") in Los Angeles, in total 86 paintings. Other large groups are in Germany, with 69 paintings, at the [Gemäldegalerie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gem%C3%A4ldegalerie,_Berlin "Gemäldegalerie, Berlin") in Berlin, [Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gem%C3%A4ldegalerie_Alte_Meister "Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister") in Dresden, and [Schloss Wilhelmshöhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Wilhelmsh%C3%B6he "Schloss Wilhelmshöhe") in Kassel, and elsewhere. The UK has a total of 51, especially in the [National Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery "National Gallery") and [Royal Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Collection "Royal Collection"). There are 49 in the Netherlands, many in the [Rijksmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijksmuseum "Rijksmuseum"), which has *[The Night Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Night_Watch "The Night Watch")* and *[The Jewish Bride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jewish_Bride "The Jewish Bride")*, and the [Mauritshuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritshuis "Mauritshuis") in The Hague. Others can be found in [The Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Louvre "The Louvre"), the [Hermitage Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitage_Museum "Hermitage Museum"), and [Nationalmuseum, Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalmuseum,_Stockholm "Nationalmuseum, Stockholm"). The [Royal Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Castle,_Warsaw "Royal Castle, Warsaw") in Warsaw has two paintings by Rembrandt.

Large collections of Rembrandt's drawings are held in the [Rijksmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijksmuseum "Rijksmuseum"), [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre "Louvre"), and [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum "British Museum"). The [Rembrandt House Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembrandt_House_Museum "Rembrandt House Museum") holds many of his drawings and "almost all" the etchings, a selection of which are on rotating display in the house.

Apart from a few very rare prints, mostly less important early studies, or "the informal printed scribbles from the artist's early years", most of his prints are not very rare by museum standards, and major [print rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_room "Print room") have good collections. Both the Rijksmuseum and the British Museum, who claim to have the best collections, have over 1,000 impressions of the 300-odd prints; most of these can be viewed in great detail online. The degree to which these collections are displayed to the public or can easily be viewed by them in the print room, varies greatly. [The Morgan Library & Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Morgan_Library_%26_Museum "The Morgan Library & Museum") in New York claims to have the best collection in the United States, with "impressions of most of the three hundred or so known etchings by Rembrandt, as well as multiple, often exceedingly rare impressions of various states"; it has "almost 500" images online.

Impressions often continued to be printed by others until at least the 19th-century, with many of the plates reworked as they became worn. In 1986, 79 of Rembrandt's [original copper plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembrandt%27s_prints#Copper_plates "Rembrandt's prints") still existed.
