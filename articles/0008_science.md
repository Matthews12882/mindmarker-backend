# Science

**Science** is a systematic discipline that builds and organises [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge "Knowledge") in the form of testable explanations about [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science") and [society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science"). It is driven by the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method"): an empirical cycle that typically involves making observations, producing [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotheses "Hypotheses"), testing them with [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence "Empirical evidence"), and drawing conclusions. Science encompasses this process and the body of knowledge it produces, which the [scientific community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_community "Scientific community") continuously challenges, validates, and organises.

Modern science is typically divided into two major branches: the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science"), which study the physical world, and the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences "Social sciences"), which study individuals and societies. While referred to as the [formal sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_science "Formal science"), [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), mathematics, and [theoretical computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_computer_science "Theoretical computer science") are typically regarded as separate because they rely on [deductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_reasoning "Deductive reasoning") instead of the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method") as their main methodology. Meanwhile, [applied sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science "Applied science") are disciplines that use scientific knowledge for practical purposes, such as engineering and medicine.

The [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science "History of science") spans the majority of the historical record, with the earliest identifiable predecessors to modern science dating to the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") in [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") (c. 3000–1200 BCE). Their contributions to mathematics, [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), and medicine entered and shaped the Greek [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") and later [medieval scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_renaissances "Medieval renaissances"), whereby formal attempts were made to provide explanations of events in the physical world based on natural causes; further advances, including the introduction of the [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system "Hindu–Arabic numeral system"), were made during the [Golden Age of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age_of_India#Gupta_Empire "Golden Age of India") and the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age").

The recovery and assimilation of [Greek works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_literature "Ancient Greek literature") and [Islamic inquiries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Science in the medieval Islamic world") into Western Europe during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") revived natural philosophy, which was later transformed by the [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution") that began in the 16th century as new ideas and discoveries departed from previous Greek conceptions and traditions. The scientific method soon played a greater role in the acquisition of knowledge, and in the [19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th_century_in_science "19th century in science"), many of the [institutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutionalisation "Institutionalisation") and [professional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professionalization "Professionalization") features of science began to take shape, along with the changing of "natural philosophy" to "natural science".

New knowledge in science is advanced by research from scientists who are motivated by curiosity about the world and a desire to solve problems. Contemporary scientific research is often highly collaborative and is frequently carried out by teams in [academic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy "Academy") and [research institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_institution "Research institution"), government agencies, and companies. At the same time, many major advances—particularly in [fundamental science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_science "Fundamental science")—have come from individual researchers and are widely recognised through major international awards such as the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize"). The practical results of scientific work have led to the emergence of [science policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_policy "Science policy") that seek to prioritise the [responsible development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsible_Research_and_Innovation "Responsible Research and Innovation") of commercial products, [health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care "Health care"), public infrastructure, [environmental protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_protection "Environmental protection"), and defense capabilities.

## Etymology

The word *science* has been used in English since the 14th century ([Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English "Middle English")) in the sense of "the state of knowing". The word was borrowed from the [Anglo-Norman language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_language "Anglo-Norman language") as the suffix *-cience*, which was borrowed from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") word *[scientia](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/scientia "wikt:scientia")*, meaning 'knowledge, awareness, understanding', a [noun derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_derivation "Morphological derivation") of *[sciens](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sciens "wikt:sciens")* meaning 'knowing', itself the present active participle of *[sciō](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/scio#Latin "wikt:scio")* 'to know'.

There are many hypotheses for *science*'s ultimate word origin. According to [Michiel de Vaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michiel_de_Vaan "Michiel de Vaan"), Dutch linguist and [Indo-Europeanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Europeanist "Indo-Europeanist"), *sciō* may have its origin in the [Proto-Italic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Italic_language "Proto-Italic language") as \**skije-* or \**skijo-* meaning 'to know', which may originate from [Proto-Indo-European language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language "Proto-Indo-European language") as \**skh1-ie*, \**skh1-io* meaning 'to incise'. The *[Lexikon der indogermanischen Verben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexikon_der_indogermanischen_Verben "Lexikon der indogermanischen Verben")* proposed *sciō* is a [back-formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-formation "Back-formation") of *[nescīre](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/nescire "wikt:nescire")*, meaning 'to not know, be unfamiliar with', which may derive from Proto-Indo-European *\**[sekH-](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/sek- "wikt:Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/sek-")** in Latin *[secāre](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/secare "wikt:secare")*, or \**skh2-* from \**sḱʰeh2(i)-* meaning 'to cut'.

In the past, science was a synonym for "knowledge" or "study", in keeping with its Latin origin. A person who conducted scientific research was called a "natural philosopher" or "man of science". In 1834, [William Whewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Whewell "William Whewell") introduced the term *scientist* in a review of [Mary Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Somerville "Mary Somerville")'s book *[On the Connexion of the Physical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Connexion_of_the_Physical_Sciences "On the Connexion of the Physical Sciences")*, crediting it to "some ingenious gentleman" (possibly himself).

## History

### Early history

Science has no single origin. Rather, scientific thinking emerged gradually over the course of tens of thousands of years, taking different forms around the world, and few details are known about the very earliest developments. [Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_science "Women in science") likely played a central role in prehistoric science, as did [religious rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual#Religious_perspectives "Ritual"). Some scholars use the term "[protoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoscience "Protoscience")" to label activities in the past that resemble modern science in some but not all features; however, this label has also been criticised as denigrating, or too suggestive of [presentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presentism_(historical_analysis) "Presentism (historical analysis)"), thinking about those activities only in relation to modern categories.

Direct evidence for scientific processes becomes clearer with the advent of [writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_systems "Writing systems") in the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") civilisations of [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") (c. 3000–1200 BCE), creating the earliest written records in the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science "History of science"). Although the words and concepts of "science" and "nature" were not part of the conceptual landscape at the time, the ancient Egyptians and Mesopotamians made contributions that would later find a place in Greek and medieval science: mathematics, astronomy, and medicine.

From the 3rd millennium BCE, the ancient Egyptians developed a non-positional [decimal numbering system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal#Origin "Decimal"), solved practical problems using [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), and developed a [calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_calendar "Egyptian calendar"). Their healing therapies involved drug treatments and the supernatural, such as prayers, [incantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incantation "Incantation"), and rituals. Ancient [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubians "Nubians") pioneered early [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotics "Antibiotics") and established a system of geometric principles that served as the basis for initial [sunclocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundials "Sundials"). Nubians also exercised a [trigonometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry") methodology comparable to their Egyptian counterparts.

The ancient [Mesopotamians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") used knowledge about the properties of various natural chemicals for manufacturing [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery "Pottery"), [faience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faience "Faience"), glass, soap, metals, [lime plaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_plaster "Lime plaster"), and waterproofing. They studied [animal physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_physiology "Animal physiology"), [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy "Anatomy"), [behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_behavior "Animal behavior"), and [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology") for [divinatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinatory "Divinatory") purposes. The Mesopotamians had an [intense interest in medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_medicine "Babylonian medicine"), and the earliest [medical prescriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_prescription "Medical prescription") appeared in [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_language "Sumerian language") during the [Third Dynasty of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Dynasty_of_Ur "Third Dynasty of Ur"). They seem to have studied scientific subjects which had practical or religious applications and had little interest in satisfying curiosity.

### Classical antiquity

In [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), there was no real analogue of a modern scientist. Instead, well-educated, usually upper-class, and almost universally male individuals performed various investigations into nature whenever they could afford the time. Before the invention or discovery of the [concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept "Concept") of *[phusis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phusis "Phusis")* or nature by the [pre-Socratic philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratic_philosopher "Pre-Socratic philosopher"), the same words tend to be used to describe the natural "way" in which a plant grows, and the "way" in which, for example, one tribe worships a particular god. For this reason, it is claimed that these men were the first philosophers in the strict sense and the first to clearly distinguish "nature" and "convention".

The early [Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophers "Greek philosophers") of the Milesian school, which was founded by [Thales of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus "Thales of Miletus") and later continued by his successors [Anaximander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaximander "Anaximander") and [Anaximenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaximenes_of_Miletus "Anaximenes of Miletus"), were the first to attempt to explain [natural phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_phenomena "Natural phenomena") without relying on the [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural "Supernatural"). The [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans "Pythagoreans") developed a [complex number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number") philosophy and contributed significantly to the development of mathematical science. The [theory of atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomism "Atomism") was developed by the Greek philosopher [Leucippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leucippus "Leucippus") and his student [Democritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democritus "Democritus"). Later, [Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus "Epicurus") would develop a full natural cosmology based on atomism, and would adopt a "canon" (ruler, standard) which established physical criteria or standards of scientific truth. The Greek doctor [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates "Hippocrates") established the tradition of systematic medical science and is known as "[The Father of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_persons_considered_father_or_mother_of_a_scientific_field#Medicine_and_physiology "List of persons considered father or mother of a scientific field")".

A turning point in the history of early philosophical science was [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates")' example of applying philosophy to the study of human matters, including human nature, the nature of political communities, and human knowledge itself. The [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method "Socratic method") as documented by [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato")'s dialogues is a [dialectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic "Dialectic") method of hypothesis elimination: better hypotheses are found by steadily identifying and eliminating those that lead to contradictions. The Socratic method searches for general commonly held truths that shape beliefs and scrutinises them for consistency. Socrates criticised the older type of study of physics as too purely speculative and lacking in [self-criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-criticism "Self-criticism").

In the 4th century BCE, [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") created a systematic programme of [teleological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleological "Teleological") philosophy. In the 3rd century BCE, Greek astronomer [Aristarchus of Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchus_of_Samos "Aristarchus of Samos") was the first to propose a [heliocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_model "Heliocentric model") of the universe, with the Sun at the centre and all the planets orbiting it. Aristarchus's model was widely rejected because it was believed to violate the laws of physics, while Ptolemy's *[Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest "Almagest")*, which contains a geocentric description of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System"), was accepted through the early Renaissance instead. The inventor and mathematician [Archimedes of Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes_of_Syracuse "Archimedes of Syracuse") made major contributions to the beginnings of [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus"). [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder "Pliny the Elder") was a Roman writer and [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath") who wrote the seminal encyclopaedia *[Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_(Pliny) "Natural History (Pliny)")*.

[Positional notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positional_notation "Positional notation") for representing numbers likely emerged between the 3rd and 5th centuries CE along Indian trade routes. This numeral system made efficient [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic") operations more accessible and would eventually become standard for mathematics worldwide.

### Middle Ages

Due to the [collapse of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Collapse of the Western Roman Empire"), the 5th century saw an intellectual decline, with knowledge of classical Greek conceptions of the world deteriorating in Western Europe. Latin encyclopaedists of the period such as [Isidore of Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Seville "Isidore of Seville") preserved the majority of general ancient knowledge. In contrast, because the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") resisted attacks from invaders, they were able to preserve and improve prior learning. [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus"), a Byzantine scholar in the 6th century, started to question Aristotle's teaching of physics, introducing the [theory of impetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_impetus "Theory of impetus"). His criticism served as an inspiration to medieval scholars and Galileo Galilei, who extensively cited his works ten centuries later.

During [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity") and the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages "Early Middle Ages"), natural phenomena were mainly examined via the Aristotelian approach. The approach includes Aristotle's [four causes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_causes "Four causes"): material, formal, efficient, and final causes. Many Greek classical texts were preserved by the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"), and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") translations were made by Christians, mainly [Nestorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorian_schism "Nestorian schism") and [Miaphysites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysites "Miaphysites"). Under the Abbasids, these Arabic translations were later improved and developed by Arabic scientists. By the 6th and 7th centuries, the neighbouring [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire") established the medical [Academy of Gondishapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Gondishapur "Academy of Gondishapur"), which was considered by Greek, Syriac, and Persian physicians as the most important medical hub of the ancient world.

Islamic study of [Aristotelianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism#Islamic_world "Aristotelianism") flourished in the [House of Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Wisdom "House of Wisdom") established in the Abbasid capital of [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad"), Iraq and it flourished until the [Mongol invasions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions "Mongol invasions") in the 13th century. [Ibn al-Haytham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham"), better known as Alhazen, used controlled experiments in his optical study. [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna")'s compilation of *[The Canon of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canon_of_Medicine "The Canon of Medicine")*, a medical encyclopaedia, is considered to be one of the most important publications in medicine and was used until the 18th century.

By the 11th century most of Europe had become Christian, and in 1088, the [University of Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bologna "University of Bologna") emerged as the first university in Europe. As such, demand for Latin translation of ancient and scientific texts grew, a major contributor to the [Renaissance of the 12th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_of_the_12th_century "Renaissance of the 12th century"). Renaissance [scholasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism") in Western Europe flourished, with experiments done by observing, describing, and classifying subjects in nature. In the 13th century, medical teachers and students at Bologna began opening human bodies, leading to the first anatomy textbook based on human dissection by [Mondino de Luzzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondino_de_Luzzi "Mondino de Luzzi").

### Renaissance

From a single print shop in [Mainz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainz "Mainz"), Germany around 1440, the [movable-type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type "Movable type") [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press "Printing press") had [spread to no less than around 270 cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press#Mass_production_and_spread_in_Europe "Printing press") in Central, Western and Eastern Europe and had already produced more than 20 million volumes by the end of the 15th century. Printing made scholarly books more widely accessible, allowing researchers to consult ancient texts freely and to compare their own observations with those of fellow scholars. Printing ended the [manuscript culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript_culture "Manuscript culture") of the Middle Ages, where [facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact "Fact") were few and far between, and replaced it with a [printing culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_culture "Printing culture") where reliable and documented facts rapidly proliferated and became the secure foundation for scientific knowledge.

In the 16th century, [Nicolaus Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus "Nicolaus Copernicus") formulated a [heliocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_heliocentrism "Copernican heliocentrism") of the Solar System, with the Sun positioned near the centre of the universe, motionless, with Earth and the other planets orbiting around it in circular motions, modified by [epicycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicycle "Epicycle"), and at uniform speeds. The Copernican model challenged the dominant [geocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism#Ptolemaic_model "Geocentrism") of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy"), which had placed Earth at the centre of the universe. 16th-century astronomers believed that Copernicus' elimination of the [equant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equant "Equant") was his chief achievement, but his model never displaced Ptolemy's, which only fell out of favour 70 years later after [Galileo's telescopic observations of 1610](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei#Phases_of_Venus "Galileo Galilei").

### Scientific Revolution

[Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_Brahe "Tycho Brahe")'s unprecedentedly accurate [astronomical observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_observation "Astronomical observation") in the late 16th century and [Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei")’s [early 17th-century telescopic observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei#Phases_of_Venus "Galileo Galilei") combined to turn astronomy into the first modern science. Galileo's observations ended a millennium of premodern astronomical orthodoxy, while [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler "Johannes Kepler") used Brahe's data to discover that planets have [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_laws_of_planetary_motion#First_law "Kepler's laws of planetary motion"), not circular, orbits and develop the [laws of planetary motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_laws_of_planetary_motion "Kepler's laws of planetary motion"). Because of Kepler, astronomical phenomena came to be seen as being governed by physical laws. The "New Science" that ultimately emerged by the end of the 17th century broke sharply with the natural philosophy that had preceded it, departed from previous Greek conceptions and traditions, was more mechanistic in its worldview and more integrated with mathematics, and was obsessed with the acquisition and interpretation of new evidence.

### Age of Enlightenment

At the start of the [Age of Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment"), [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") formed the foundation of [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics") with his *[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica "Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica")*, greatly influencing future physicists. [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz") incorporated terms from [Aristotelian physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_physics "Aristotelian physics"), now used in a new non-[teleological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleological "Teleological") way. This implied a shift in the view of objects: objects were now considered as having no innate goals. Leibniz assumed that different types of things all work according to the same general laws of nature, with no special formal or final causes.

During this time, the declared purpose and value of science became producing wealth and inventions that would improve human lives, in the [materialistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_materialism "Economic materialism") sense of having more food, clothing, and other things. In [Bacon's words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novum_Organum "Novum Organum"), "the real and legitimate goal of sciences *is the endowment of human life with new inventions and riches*", and he discouraged scientists from pursuing intangible philosophical or spiritual ideas, which he believed contributed little to human happiness beyond "the fume of subtle, sublime or pleasing [speculation]".

Science during the Enlightenment was dominated by [scientific societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_societies "Scientific societies") and academies, which had largely replaced universities as centres of scientific research and development. Societies and academies were the backbones of the maturation of the scientific profession. Another important development was the [popularisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_culture "Popular culture") of science among an increasingly literate population. Enlightenment philosophers turned to a few of their scientific predecessors – [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo "Galileo"), [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler "Kepler"), [Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle"), and Newton principally – as the guides to every physical and social field of the day.

The 18th century saw significant advancements in the practice of medicine and physics; the development of biological [taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy_(biology) "Taxonomy (biology)") by [Carl Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus "Carl Linnaeus"); a new understanding of [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism "Magnetism") and electricity; and the maturation of [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") as a discipline. Ideas on human nature, society, and economics evolved during the Enlightenment. [Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume "David Hume") and other Scottish Enlightenment thinkers developed *[A Treatise of Human Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_of_Human_Nature "A Treatise of Human Nature")*, which was expressed historically in works by authors including [James Burnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Burnett,_Lord_Monboddo "James Burnett, Lord Monboddo"), [Adam Ferguson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Ferguson "Adam Ferguson"), [John Millar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Millar_(philosopher) "John Millar (philosopher)") and [William Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Robertson_(historian) "William Robertson (historian)"), all of whom merged a scientific study of how humans behaved in ancient and primitive cultures with a strong awareness of the determining forces of [modernity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernity "Modernity"). Modern sociology largely originated from this movement. In 1776, [Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith "Adam Smith") published *[The Wealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations "The Wealth of Nations")*, which is often considered the first work on modern economics.

### 19th century

During the 19th century, many distinguishing characteristics of contemporary modern science began to take shape. These included the transformation of the life and physical sciences; the frequent use of precision instruments; the emergence of terms such as "biologist", "physicist", and "scientist"; an increased professionalisation of those studying nature; scientists gaining cultural authority over many dimensions of society; the industrialisation of numerous countries; the thriving of popular science writings; and the emergence of science journals. During the late 19th century, psychology emerged as a separate discipline from philosophy when [Wilhelm Wundt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Wundt "Wilhelm Wundt") founded the first laboratory for psychological research in 1879.

During the mid-19th century, [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin "Charles Darwin") and [Alfred Russel Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Russel_Wallace "Alfred Russel Wallace") independently proposed the theory of evolution by [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection") in 1858, which explained how different plants and animals originated and evolved. Their theory was set out in detail in Darwin's book *[On the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species "On the Origin of Species")*, published in 1859. Separately, [Gregor Mendel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel "Gregor Mendel") presented his paper "[Experiments on Plant Hybridisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experiments_on_Plant_Hybridisation "Experiments on Plant Hybridisation")" in 1865, which outlined the principles of biological inheritance, serving as the basis for modern genetics.

Early in the 19th century, [John Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton "John Dalton") suggested the modern [atomic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory "Atomic theory"), based on Democritus's original idea of indivisible particles called *atoms*. The laws of [conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy"), [conservation of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_momentum "Conservation of momentum"), and [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass "Conservation of mass") suggested a highly stable universe where there could be little loss of resources. However, with the advent of the [steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine "Steam engine") and the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"), there was an increased understanding that not all forms of energy have the same [energy qualities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_quality "Energy quality"), the ease of conversion to useful [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_(thermodynamics) "Work (thermodynamics)") or to another form of energy. This realisation led to the development of the laws of [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics"), in which the free energy of the universe is seen as constantly declining: the [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy") of a closed universe increases over time.

The [electromagnetic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_theory "Electromagnetic theory") was established in the 19th century by the works of [Hans Christian Ørsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted "Hans Christian Ørsted"), [André-Marie Ampère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9-Marie_Amp%C3%A8re "André-Marie Ampère"), [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday "Michael Faraday"), [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell"), [Oliver Heaviside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Heaviside "Oliver Heaviside"), and [Heinrich Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Hertz "Heinrich Hertz"). The new theory raised questions that could not easily be answered using Newton's framework. The discovery of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") inspired the discovery of [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity "Radioactivity") by [Henri Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Becquerel "Henri Becquerel") and [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie "Marie Curie") in 1896; Marie Curie then became the first person to win two Nobel Prizes. In the next year came the discovery of the first subatomic particle, the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron").

### 20th century

In the first half of the century, the development of [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotics "Antibiotics") and [artificial fertilisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_fertiliser "Artificial fertiliser") improved human living standards globally. Harmful [environmental issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues "Environmental issues") such as [ozone depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion "Ozone depletion"), [ocean acidification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_acidification "Ocean acidification"), [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication "Eutrophication"), and [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") came to the public's attention and caused the onset of [environmental studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_studies "Environmental studies").

During this period, scientific experimentation became increasingly [large in scale and funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_science "Big science"). The extensive technological innovation stimulated by [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), and the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") led to competitions between [global powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_power "Great power"), such as the [Space Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Race "Space Race") and [nuclear arms race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_arms_race "Nuclear arms race"). Substantial international collaborations were also made, despite armed conflicts.

In the late 20th century, active recruitment of women and elimination of [sex discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_discrimination "Sex discrimination") greatly increased the number of women scientists, but large gender disparities remained in some fields. The discovery of the [cosmic microwave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background "Cosmic microwave background") in 1964 led to a rejection of the [steady-state model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_model "Steady-state model") of the universe in favour of the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang") theory of [Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lema%C3%AEtre "Georges Lemaître").

The century saw fundamental changes within scientific disciplines. Evolution became a unified theory in the early 20th century when the [modern synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_synthesis_(20th_century) "Modern synthesis (20th century)") reconciled Darwinian evolution with [classical genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_genetics "Classical genetics"). [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s [theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity "Theory of relativity") and the development of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") complement classical mechanics to describe physics in extreme [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length "Length"), time, and [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity"). Widespread use of [integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit "Integrated circuit") in the last quarter of the 20th century combined with [communications satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_satellite "Communications satellite") led to a revolution in information technology and the rise of the global internet and [mobile computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_computing "Mobile computing"), including [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphone "Smartphone"). The need for mass systematisation of long, intertwined causal chains and large amounts of data led to the rise of the fields of [systems theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_theory "Systems theory") and computer-assisted [scientific modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_modelling "Scientific modelling").

### 21st century

The [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project "Human Genome Project") was completed in 2003 by identifying and mapping all of the genes of the [human genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_genome "Human genome"). The first [induced pluripotent human stem cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_pluripotent_stem_cell "Induced pluripotent stem cell") were made in 2006, allowing adult cells to be transformed into [stem cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stem_cell "Stem cell") and turn into any cell type found in the body. With the affirmation of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson "Higgs boson") discovery in 2013, the last particle predicted by the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") of particle physics was found. In 2015, [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave"), predicted by [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") a century before, were [first observed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves "First observation of gravitational waves"). In 2019, the international collaboration [Event Horizon Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_Horizon_Telescope "Event Horizon Telescope") presented the first direct image of a [black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole "Black hole")'s [accretion disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accretion_disc "Accretion disc").

## Branches

Modern science is commonly divided into three major [branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branches_of_science "Branches of science"): [natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science"), [social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science"), and [formal science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_science "Formal science"). Each of these branches comprises various specialised yet overlapping scientific disciplines that often possess their own [nomenclature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomenclature "Nomenclature") and expertise. Both natural and social sciences are [empirical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_science "Empirical science"), as their knowledge is based on [empirical observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence "Empirical evidence") and is capable of being tested for its validity by other researchers working under the same conditions.

### Natural

[Natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science") is the study of the physical world. It can be divided into two main branches: [life science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_science "Life science") and [physical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_science "Physical science"). These two branches may be further divided into more specialised disciplines. For example, physical science can be subdivided into physics, [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), and [earth science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_science "Earth science"). Modern natural science is the successor to the [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") that began in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"). [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo "Galileo"), [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes "Descartes"), [Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon "Francis Bacon"), and [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") debated the benefits of using approaches that were more [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics") and more experimental in a methodical way. Still, philosophical perspectives, [conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture "Conjecture"), and [presuppositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presupposition "Presupposition"), often overlooked, remain necessary in natural science. Systematic data collection, including [discovery science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_science "Discovery science"), succeeded [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history "Natural history"), which emerged in the 16th century by describing and classifying plants, animals, minerals, and other biotic beings. Today, "natural history" suggests observational descriptions aimed at popular audiences.

### Social

[Social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science") is the study of human behaviour and the functioning of societies. It has many disciplines that include, but are not limited to, [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology"), economics, history, [human geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_geography "Human geography"), [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science "Political science"), psychology, and sociology. In the social sciences, there are many competing theoretical perspectives, many of which are extended through competing [research programmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_programme "Research programme") such as the [functionalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionalists "Functionalists"), [conflict theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_theories "Conflict theories"), and [interactionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactionism "Interactionism") in sociology. Due to the limitations of conducting controlled experiments involving large groups of individuals or complex situations, social scientists may adopt other research methods such as the [historical method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_method "Historical method"), [case studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_studies "Case studies"), and [cross-cultural studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-cultural_studies "Cross-cultural studies"). Moreover, if quantitative information is available, social scientists may rely on statistical approaches to better understand social relationships and processes.

### Formal

[Formal science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_science "Formal science") is an area of study that generates knowledge using [formal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_system "Formal system"). A formal system is an [abstract structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_structure "Abstract structure") used for inferring [theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem "Theorem") from [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom "Axiom") according to a set of rules. It includes mathematics, [systems theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_theory "Systems theory"), and [theoretical computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_computer_science "Theoretical computer science"). The formal sciences share similarities with the other two branches by relying on objective, careful, and systematic study of an area of knowledge. They are, however, different from the empirical sciences as they rely exclusively on deductive reasoning, without the need for empirical evidence, to verify their abstract concepts. The formal sciences are therefore *[a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori "A priori")* disciplines, and because of this, there is disagreement on whether they constitute a science. Nevertheless, the formal sciences play an important role in the empirical sciences. [Calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus"), for example, was initially invented to understand [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion "Motion") in physics. Natural and social sciences that rely heavily on mathematical applications include [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"), [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_chemistry "Mathematical chemistry"), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_biology "Mathematical biology"), [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_finance "Mathematical finance"), and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_economics "Mathematical economics").

### Applied

[Applied science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science "Applied science") is the use of the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method") and knowledge to attain practical goals and includes a broad range of disciplines such as engineering and medicine. Engineering is the use of scientific principles to invent, design, and build machines, structures, and technologies. Science may contribute to the development of new technologies. Medicine is the practice of caring for patients by maintaining and restoring health through the [prevention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevention_(medical) "Prevention (medical)"), [diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diagnosis "Medical diagnosis"), and [treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapy "Therapy") of injury or disease.

### Basic

The applied sciences are often contrasted with the [basic sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_science "Basic science"), which are focused on advancing scientific theories and laws that explain and predict events in the natural world.

### Blue skies

[Blue skies research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_skies_research "Blue skies research"), also called blue sky science, is scientific research in domains where "real-world" applications are not immediately apparent. It has been defined as "research without a clear goal" and "curiosity-driven science". Proponents of this mode of science argue that unanticipated scientific breakthroughs are sometimes more valuable than the outcomes of agenda-driven research, heralding advances in [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") and [stem cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stem_cell "Stem cell") biology as examples of unforeseen benefits of research that was originally seen as purely theoretical in scope. Because of the inherently uncertain [return on investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_on_investment "Return on investment"), blue-sky projects are sometimes politically and commercially unpopular and tend to lose funding to research perceived as being more reliably profitable or practical.

### Computational

[Computational science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_science "Computational science") applies [computer simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_simulations "Computer simulations") to science, enabling a better understanding of scientific problems than formal mathematics alone can achieve. The use of [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning") and [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") is becoming a central feature of computational contributions to science, for example in [agent-based computational economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent-based_computational_economics "Agent-based computational economics"), [random forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_forest "Random forest"), [topic modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_model "Topic model") and various forms of prediction. However, machines alone rarely advance knowledge as they require human guidance and capacity to reason; and they can introduce bias against certain social groups or sometimes underperform against humans.

### Interdisciplinary

[Interdisciplinary science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary_science "Interdisciplinary science") involves the combination of two or more disciplines into one, such as [bioinformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioinformatics "Bioinformatics") (a combination of biology and computer science) and [cognitive sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science "Cognitive science"). The concept has existed since the ancient Greek period, and it became popular again in the 20th century.

## Research

Scientific research can be labelled as either basic or applied research. [Basic research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_research "Basic research") is the search for knowledge, and [applied research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_research "Applied research") is the search for solutions to practical problems using this knowledge. Most understanding comes from basic research, though sometimes applied research targets specific practical problems. This leads to technological advances that were not previously imaginable.

### Scientific method

Scientific research involves using the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method"), which seeks to [objectively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objectivity_(science) "Objectivity (science)") explain the events of [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature "Nature") in a [reproducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducible "Reproducible") way. Scientists usually take for granted a set of basic assumptions that are needed to justify the scientific method: there is an [objective reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_reality "Objective reality") shared by all rational observers; this objective reality is governed by [natural laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law "Natural law"); these laws were discovered by means of systematic [observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation "Observation") and experimentation. Mathematics is essential in the formation of [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotheses "Hypotheses"), [theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theories "Theories"), and laws, because it is used extensively in quantitative modelling, observing, and collecting [measurements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurements "Measurements"). Statistics is used to summarise and analyse data, which allows scientists to assess the reliability of experimental results.

In the scientific method, an explanatory [thought experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_experiment "Thought experiment") or hypothesis is put forward as an explanation using [parsimony principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor "Occam's razor") and is expected to seek [consilience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consilience "Consilience") – fitting with other accepted facts related to an observation or scientific question. This tentative explanation is used to make [falsifiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiable "Falsifiable") predictions, which are typically posted before being tested by experimentation. Disproof of a prediction is evidence of progress. Experimentation is especially important in science to help establish [causal relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_relationships "Causal relationships") to avoid the [correlation fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_fallacy "Correlation fallacy"), though in some sciences such as astronomy or geology, a predicted observation might be more appropriate.

When a hypothesis proves unsatisfactory, it is modified or discarded. If the hypothesis survives testing, it may become adopted into the framework of a [scientific theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory "Scientific theory"), a [validly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_logic "Deductive logic") [reasoned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason"), self-consistent model or framework for describing the behaviour of certain natural events. A theory typically describes the behaviour of much broader sets of observations than a hypothesis; commonly, a large number of hypotheses can be logically bound together by a single theory. Thus, a theory is a hypothesis explaining various other hypotheses. In that vein, theories are formulated according to most of the same scientific principles as hypotheses. Scientists may generate a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_modelling "Scientific modelling"), an attempt to describe or depict an observation in terms of a logical, physical, or mathematical representation, and to generate new hypotheses that can be tested by experimentation.

While performing experiments to test hypotheses, scientists may have a preference for one outcome over another. Eliminating the bias can be achieved through transparency, careful [experimental design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_design "Experimental design"), and a thorough [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review "Peer review") process of the experimental results and conclusions. After the results of an experiment are announced or published, it is normal practice for independent researchers to double-check how the research was performed, and to follow up by performing similar experiments to determine how dependable the results might be. Taken in its entirety, the scientific method allows for highly creative [problem solving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_solving "Problem solving") while minimising the effects of subjective and [confirmation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias "Confirmation bias"). [Intersubjective verifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersubjective_verifiability "Intersubjective verifiability"), the ability to reach a consensus and reproduce results, is fundamental to the creation of all scientific knowledge.

### Literature

Scientific research is published in a range of literature. [Scientific journals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_journal "Scientific journal") communicate and document the results of research carried out in universities and various other research institutions, serving as an archival record of science. The first scientific journals, *[Journal des sçavans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_des_s%C3%A7avans "Journal des sçavans")* followed by *[Philosophical Transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions "Philosophical Transactions")*, began publication in 1665. Since that time, the total number of active periodicals has steadily increased. In 1981, one estimate for the number of scientific and technical journals in publication was 11,500.

Most scientific journals cover a single scientific field and publish the research within that field; the research is normally expressed in the form of a [scientific article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_article "Scientific article"). Science has become so pervasive in modern societies that it is considered necessary to communicate the achievements, news, and ambitions of scientists to a wider population.

## Philosophy

There are different schools of thought in the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science"). The most popular position is [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism "Empiricism"), which holds that knowledge is created by a process involving observation; scientific theories generalise observations. Empiricism generally encompasses [inductivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductivism "Inductivism"), a position that explains how general theories can be made from the finite amount of empirical evidence available. Many versions of empiricism exist, with the predominant ones being [Bayesianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesianism "Bayesianism") and the [hypothetico-deductive method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothetico-deductive_method "Hypothetico-deductive method").

Empiricism has stood in contrast to [rationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism "Rationalism"), the position originally associated with [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes "Descartes"), which holds that knowledge is created by the human intellect, not by observation.
[Critical rationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_rationalism "Critical rationalism") is a contrasting 20th-century approach to science, first defined by Austrian-British philosopher [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper "Karl Popper"). Popper rejected the way that empiricism describes the connection between theory and observation. He claimed that theories are not generated by observation, but that observation is made in the light of theories, and that the only way theory A can be affected by observation is *after* theory A were to conflict with observation, but theory B were to survive the observation.
Popper proposed replacing verifiability with [falsifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability "Falsifiability") as the landmark of scientific theories, replacing induction with falsification as the empirical method. Popper further claimed that there is actually only one universal method, not specific to science: the negative method of criticism, [trial and error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_and_error "Trial and error"), covering all products of the human mind, including science, mathematics, philosophy, and art.

Another approach, [instrumentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumentalism "Instrumentalism"), emphasises the utility of theories as instruments for explaining and predicting phenomena. It views scientific theories as black boxes, with only their input (initial conditions) and output (predictions) being relevant. Consequences, theoretical entities, and logical structure are claimed to be things that should be ignored. Close to instrumentalism is [constructive empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_empiricism "Constructive empiricism"), according to which the main criterion for the success of a scientific theory is whether what it says about observable entities is true.

[Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn "Thomas Kuhn") argued that the process of observation and evaluation takes place within a paradigm, a [logically consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_consistent "Logically consistent") "portrait" of the world that is consistent with observations made from its framing. He characterised *normal science* as the process of observation and "puzzle solving", which takes place within a paradigm, whereas *revolutionary science* occurs when one paradigm overtakes another in a [paradigm shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm_shift "Paradigm shift"). Each paradigm has its own distinct questions, aims, and interpretations. The choice between paradigms involves setting two or more "portraits" against the world and deciding which likeness is most promising. A paradigm shift occurs when a significant number of observational anomalies arise in the old paradigm, and a new paradigm makes sense of them. That is, the choice of a new paradigm is based on observations, even though those observations are made against the background of the old paradigm. For Kuhn, acceptance or rejection of a paradigm is a social process as much as a logical process. Kuhn's position, however, is not one of [relativism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativism "Relativism").

Another approach often cited in debates of [scientific scepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_scepticism "Scientific scepticism") against controversial movements like "[creation science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_science "Creation science")" is [methodological naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodological_naturalism "Methodological naturalism"). Naturalists maintain that a difference should be made between natural and supernatural, and science should be restricted to natural explanations. Methodological naturalism maintains that science requires strict adherence to [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical "Empirical") study and independent verification.

One question for philosophy of science is how scientific evidence and theories can lead to decisions. As the [is-ought problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is%E2%80%93ought_problem "Is–ought problem") highlights, facts alone cannot determine what ought to be. This connects to the key concept of *value-ladenness*: how choices made on the basis of scientific findings depend on values.

## Community

The [scientific community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_community "Scientific community") is a network of interacting scientists who conduct scientific research. The community consists of smaller groups working in scientific fields. By having [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review "Peer review"), through discussion and debate within journals and conferences, scientists maintain the quality of research methodology and objectivity when interpreting results.

### Scientists

Scientists are individuals who conduct scientific research to advance knowledge in an area of interest. Scientists may exhibit a strong curiosity about reality and a desire to apply scientific knowledge for the benefit of public health, nations, the environment, or industries; other motivations include recognition by peers and prestige. In modern times, many scientists study within specific areas of science in [academic institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_institution "Academic institution"), often obtaining [advanced degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_degree "Terminal degree") in the process. Many scientists pursue careers in various [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectors_of_the_economy "Sectors of the economy") such as academia, [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_(economics) "Industry (economics)"), [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administration_(government) "Administration (government)"), and nonprofit organisations.

Science has historically been a male-dominated field, with notable exceptions. Women have historically faced considerable discrimination in science. For example, women were frequently passed over for job opportunities and denied credit for their work. The achievements of [women in science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_science "Women in science") have been attributed to the defiance of their traditional role as labourers within the [domestic sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_sphere "Domestic sphere"). Recently [discrimination against men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_against_men "Discrimination against men") has been observed in parts of the scientific community.

### Learned societies

[Learned societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learned_societies "Learned societies") for the communication and promotion of scientific thought and experimentation have existed since the Renaissance. Many scientists belong to a learned society that promotes their respective scientific discipline, [profession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profession "Profession"), or group of related disciplines. Membership may either be open to all, require possession of scientific credentials, or be conferred by election. Most scientific societies are nonprofit organisations, and many are [professional associations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_association "Professional association"). Their activities typically include holding regular [conferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_conference "Academic conference") for the presentation and discussion of new research results and publishing or sponsoring [academic journals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_journal "Academic journal") in their discipline. Some societies act as [professional bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_bodies "Professional bodies"), regulating the activities of their members in the public interest, or the collective interest of the membership.

The professionalisation of science, begun in the 19th century, was partly enabled by the creation of national distinguished [academies of sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_sciences "Academy of sciences") such as the Italian *[Accademia dei Lincei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_dei_Lincei "Accademia dei Lincei")* in 1603, the British [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in 1660, the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") in 1666, the American [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1863, the German [Kaiser Wilhelm Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_Society "Kaiser Wilhelm Society") in 1911, and the [Chinese Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Sciences "Chinese Academy of Sciences") in 1949. International scientific organisations, such as the [International Science Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Science_Council "International Science Council"), are devoted to [international cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_cooperation "International cooperation") for scientific advancement.

### Awards

[Science awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lists_of_science_and_technology_awards "Lists of science and technology awards") are usually given to individuals or organisations that have made significant contributions to a discipline. They are often given by prestigious institutions; thus, it is considered a great honour for a scientist receiving them. Since the early Renaissance, scientists have often been awarded medals, money, and titles. The Nobel Prize, a widely regarded prestigious award, is awarded annually to those who have achieved scientific advances in the fields of medicine, physics, and [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry").

## Society

### Funding and policies

[Funding of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funding_of_science "Funding of science") is often through a competitive process in which potential research projects are evaluated and only the most promising receive funding. Such processes, which are run by government, corporations, or foundations, allocate scarce funds. Total research funding in most [developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_countries "Developed countries") is between 1.5% and 3% of GDP. In the [OECD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD "OECD"), around two-thirds of [research and development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_development "Research and development") in scientific and technical fields is carried out by industry, and 20% and 10%, respectively, by universities and government. The government funding proportion in certain fields is higher, and it dominates research in social science and the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities "Humanities"). In less developed nations, the government provides the bulk of the funds for their basic scientific research.

Many governments have dedicated agencies to support scientific research, such as the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") in the United States, the [National Scientific and Technical Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Scientific_and_Technical_Research_Council "National Scientific and Technical Research Council") in Argentina, the [Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_Organisation "Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation") in Australia, the [National Centre for Scientific Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Centre_for_Scientific_Research "National Centre for Scientific Research") in France, the [Max Planck Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Society "Max Planck Society") in Germany, and the [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_National_Research_Council "Spanish National Research Council") in Spain. In commercial research and development, all but the most research-oriented corporations focus more heavily on near-term commercialisation possibilities than research driven by curiosity.

[Science policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_policy "Science policy") is concerned with policies that affect the conduct of the scientific enterprise, including [research funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_funding "Research funding"), often in pursuance of other national policy goals such as technological innovation to promote commercial product development, weapons development, health care, and environmental monitoring. Science policy sometimes refers to the act of applying scientific knowledge and consensus to the development of public policies. In accordance with public policy being concerned about the well-being of its citizens, science policy's goal is to consider how science and technology can best serve the public. Public policy can directly affect the funding of [capital equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_equipment "Capital equipment") and intellectual infrastructure for industrial research by providing tax incentives to those organisations that fund research.

### Education and awareness

[Science education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_education "Science education") for the general public is embedded in the school curriculum, and is supplemented by [online pedagogical content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube_in_education "YouTube in education") (for example, [YouTube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube "YouTube") and [Khan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_Academy "Khan Academy")), museums, and science magazines and blogs. Major organisations of scientists such as the [American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "American Association for the Advancement of Science") consider the sciences to be a part of the liberal arts traditions of learning, along with philosophy and history. [Scientific literacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_literacy "Scientific literacy") is chiefly concerned with an understanding of the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method"), units and methods of [measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement "Measurement"), [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism "Empiricism"), a basic understanding of statistics ([correlations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlations "Correlations"), [qualitative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_research "Qualitative research") versus [quantitative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_research "Quantitative research") observations, [aggregate statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregate_statistics "Aggregate statistics")), and a basic understanding of core scientific fields such as physics, [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology"), ecology, geology, and [computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computation "Computation"). As a student advances into higher stages of [formal education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_education "Formal education"), the curriculum becomes more in-depth. Traditional subjects usually included in the curriculum are natural and formal sciences, although recent movements include social and applied sciences as well.

The mass media face pressures that can prevent them from accurately depicting competing scientific claims in terms of their credibility within the scientific community as a whole. Determining how much weight to give different sides in a [scientific debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_debate "Scientific debate") may require considerable expertise regarding the matter. Few journalists have real scientific knowledge, and even [beat reporters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_reporter "Beat reporter") who are knowledgeable about certain scientific issues may be ignorant about other scientific issues that they are suddenly asked to cover.

[Science magazines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_magazines "Science magazines") such as *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist "New Scientist")*, *[Science & Vie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%26_Vie "Science & Vie")*, and *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American "Scientific American")* cater to the needs of a much wider readership and provide a non-technical summary of popular areas of research, including notable discoveries and advances in certain fields of research. The science fiction genre, primarily [speculative fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speculative_fiction "Speculative fiction"), can transmit the ideas and methods of science to the general public. Recent efforts to intensify or develop links between science and non-scientific disciplines, such as literature or poetry, include the *Creative Writing Science* resource developed through the [Royal Literary Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Literary_Fund "Royal Literary Fund").

### Anti-science attitudes

While the scientific method is broadly accepted in the scientific community, some fractions of society reject certain scientific positions or are sceptical about science. Examples are the common notion that [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19 "COVID-19") is not a major health threat to the US (held by 39% of Americans in August 2021) or the belief that [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") is not a major threat to the US (also held by 40% of Americans, in late 2019 and early 2020). Psychologists have pointed to several factors driving rejection of scientific results:

* Scientific authorities are sometimes seen as inexpert, untrustworthy, or [biased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_bias "Academic bias").
* Some [marginalised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginalised "Marginalised") social groups hold anti-science attitudes, in part because these groups have often been exploited in [unethical experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unethical_human_experimentation "Unethical human experimentation").
* Messages from scientists may contradict deeply held existing beliefs or morals.

Anti-science attitudes often seem to be caused by fear of rejection in social groups. For instance, climate change is perceived as a threat by only 22% of Americans on the right side of the political spectrum, but by 85% on the left. That is, if someone on the left would not consider climate change as a threat, this person may face contempt and be rejected in that social group. In fact, people may rather deny a scientifically accepted fact than lose or jeopardise their social status.

### Politics

Attitudes towards science are often determined by political opinions and goals. Government, business, and [advocacy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advocacy_group "Advocacy group") have been known to use legal and economic pressure to influence scientific researchers. Many factors can act as facets of the [politicisation of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politicisation_of_science "Politicisation of science"), such as [anti-intellectualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-intellectualism "Anti-intellectualism"), perceived threats to religious beliefs, and fear for business interests. Politicisation of science is usually accomplished when scientific information is presented in a way that emphasises the uncertainty associated with the [scientific evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_evidence "Scientific evidence"). Tactics such as shifting conversation, failing to acknowledge facts, and capitalising on doubt of [scientific consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus "Scientific consensus") have been used to gain more attention for views that have been undermined by scientific evidence. Examples of issues that have involved the politicisation of science include the [global warming controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming_controversy "Global warming controversy"), [health effects of pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_pesticides "Health effects of pesticides"), and [health effects of tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_tobacco "Health effects of tobacco").

### Challenges

The [replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis "Replication crisis") is an ongoing systemic crisis that affects parts of science, where the results of a fraction of scientific studies have been proven to be [unreproducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducibility "Reproducibility"). The crisis has long-standing roots; the phrase was coined in the early 2010s as part of a growing awareness of the problem. A 2026 replication study found low replication rates in social and behavioural sciences ([business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business "Business"), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics"), [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education "Education"), [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science "Political science"), [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology") and [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology "Sociology")). The replication crisis represents an important body of research in [metascience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metascience "Metascience"), which aims to improve scientific research quality and [integrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_integrity "Scientific integrity") while reducing waste. Low replicability of science can reduce its [trustworthiness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_(social_science) "Trust (social science)").

The term [scientific misconduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_misconduct "Scientific misconduct") refers to situations such as where researchers have intentionally misrepresented their published data or have purposely given credit for a discovery to the wrong person.
An area of study or speculation that masquerades as science in an attempt to claim legitimacy that it would not otherwise be able to achieve is sometimes referred to as [pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience"), [fringe science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fringe_science "Fringe science"), or [junk science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junk_science "Junk science"). Physicist [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") coined the term "[cargo cult science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_cult_science "Cargo cult science")" for cases in which researchers believe, and at a glance, look like they are doing science but lack the honesty to allow their results to be rigorously evaluated. Various types of commercial advertising, ranging from hype to fraud, may fall into these categories. Science has been described as "the most important tool" for separating valid claims from invalid ones. Sometimes, research can be well-intended but is incorrect, obsolete, incomplete, or over-simplified expositions of scientific ideas.

[Academic freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_freedom "Academic freedom") can be reduced by scientific [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship "Censorship") caused by governments, institutions, other scientists, or self-censorship. Some scientists were found to justify scientific censorship with [harm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harm "Harm") avoidance. There can also be an element of [political bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_bias "Political bias") or ideological bias in science. Scientists in some countries were found to have a bias in political party preferences compared to the general population. The average political bias of a scientific field can change over time and can be explained with field-environment interaction and [self-selection bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-selection_bias "Self-selection bias"). Science [activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activism "Activism") that overlaps with political [advocacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advocacy "Advocacy") can decrease the [credibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credibility "Credibility") of science.
