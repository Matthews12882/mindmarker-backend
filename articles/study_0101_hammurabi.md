# Hammurabi

**Hammurabi** ([/ˌhæmʊˈrɑːbi/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Old Babylonian Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Babylonian_Akkadian_language "Old Babylonian Akkadian language"): [𒄩𒄠𒈬𒊏𒁉](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%92%84%A9%F0%92%84%A0%F0%92%88%AC%F0%92%8A%8F%F0%92%81%89 "wikt:𒄩𒄠𒈬𒊏𒁉"), romanized: **Ḫammu-rāpi**; Akkadian: [xammuˈraːpʰi]; c. 1810 BC – c. 1750 BC), also spelled **Hammurapi**, was the sixth [Amorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorites "Amorites") king of [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Babylonian_Empire "Old Babylonian Empire"), reigning from c. 1792 BC to c. 1750 BC. He was preceded by his father, [Sin-muballit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin-muballit "Sin-muballit").

During his reign, he conquered the powerful kingdoms of [Larsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larsa "Larsa"), [Eshnunna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eshnunna "Eshnunna"), and [Mari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari,_Syria "Mari, Syria"), bringing almost all of [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") under [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia "Babylonia") rule. It was under his rule that Babylon rose to the status of a leading kingdom on the political stage of the Middle East, and that the foundations were laid for it to become one of the major cities of ancient history.

Hammurabi is best known for having issued his [law code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi "Code of Hammurabi"), said to have been inspired by [Shamash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamash "Shamash"), the Babylonian god of Justice. It is one of the oldest legal texts ever discovered, and the longest to have survived from the ancient Near East. His reign is also one of the Mesopotamian reigns best documented by written records, particularly thanks to the correspondence unearthed in two of the cities he subjugated, [Larsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larsa "Larsa") and [Mari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari,_Syria "Mari, Syria"), which sheds light on various facets of how he exercised power during the second half of his reign.

His son [Samsu-iluna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsu-iluna "Samsu-iluna") succeeded him but failed to maintain the empire he had built. After his death, Hammurabi was revered as a great conqueror, but later his military accomplishments became de-emphasized and his role as the ideal lawgiver became the primary aspect of his legacy. Hammurabi was rediscovered by archaeologists in the late 19th century and has since been seen as an important figure in the [history of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history "Legal history").

## Name

The meaning of the name Hammurabi is a matter of debate. The most common translation is, in [Amorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorite "Amorite") or [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_(language) "Akkadian (language)"), “the ancestor is great,” *Ḫammu-rabi*, divided in two parts: *ḫammu(m)* or *‘ammu*, meaning “ancestor,” and *rabi*, meaning “great” or “to be great.” An alternative reading, in Amorite, translates the name as “the ancestor heals,” *‘Ammu-rāpi*. M. Streck, for his part, translates *‘ammu* as “paternal uncle,” which he interprets as a reference to a protective deity.

This name was borne by kings contemporary with Hammurabi, the most important being [Hammurabi I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi_I "Hammurabi I") of [Yamhad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamhad "Yamhad") ([Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo "Aleppo")), as well as by other [Amorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorite "Amorite") kings of Syria who reigned subsequently. It was common among the Amorites for a man holding the rank of chieftain to bear a name referring to his ancestors, as connection to a lineage and dynastic continuity were essential to his legitimacy.
