# Society

A **society** is a large [social group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_group "Social group") sharing the same spatial or social [territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territory "Territory"), typically subject to the same [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics "Politics") authority and dominant cultural expectations. Societies are characterized by patterns of relationships ([social relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_relation "Social relation")) between individuals who share a distinctive [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture "Culture") and [institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutions "Institutions"); a given society may be described as the sum total of such relationships among its constituent members.

Human [social structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_structure "Social structure") are complex and highly cooperative, featuring the [specialization of labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labour "Division of labour") via [social roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role "Role"). Societies construct roles and other patterns of behavior by deeming certain actions or concepts acceptable or unacceptable—these expectations around behavior within a given society are known as [societal norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Societal_norms "Societal norms"). So far as it is [collaborative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaborative "Collaborative"), a society can enable its members to benefit in ways that would otherwise be difficult on an individual basis.

Societies vary based on level of [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology "Technology") and type of economic activity. Larger societies with larger food surpluses often exhibit [stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stratification "Social stratification") or [dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_hierarchy "Dominance hierarchy") patterns. Societies can have many different forms of government, various ways of understanding [kinship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinship "Kinship"), and different gender roles. Human behavior varies immensely between different societies; humans shape society, but society in turn shapes human beings.

## Etymology and usage

The term "society" commonly refers to a large group of people living in an ordered community within a country or across several similar countries, or to the "state of being with other people", as in the phrase "they lived in medieval society".

The term dates back to at least 1513 and originates from the 12th-century French *societe* (modern French *société*), meaning "company". The French term was derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") word *[societas](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/societas "wikt:societas")* ("fellowship", "alliance", or "association"), which itself comes from the noun *socius* ("[comrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comrade "Comrade")", [friend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friendship "Friendship"), or ally).

The word "social" derives from the Latin word *socii* ('allies'). It is particularly derived from the Italian *[Socii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socii "Socii")* states, historical allies of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic"), though they rebelled against Rome in the [Social War of 91–87 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_War_(91%E2%80%9387_BC) "Social War (91–87 BC)").

## Conceptions

### In biology

[Humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human "Human"), along with their closest relatives, [bonobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonobo "Bonobo") and [chimpanzees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimpanzee "Chimpanzee"), are highly social animals. This biological context suggests that the underlying sociability required for the formation of societies is hardwired into human nature. Human society features high degrees of cooperation, and differs in important ways from groups of chimps and bonobos, including the parental role of males, the use of language to communicate, the specialization of labor, and the tendency to build "nests" (multi-generational camps, towns, or cities).

Some biologists, including entomologist [E.O. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._O._Wilson "E. O. Wilson"), categorize humans as [eusocial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusocial "Eusocial"), placing humans with [ants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant "Ant") in the highest level of sociability on the spectrum of [animal ethology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethology "Ethology"), although others disagree. Social group living may have evolved in humans due to [group selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_selection "Group selection") in physical environments that made survival difficult.

### In sociology

In Western sociology, there are three dominant [paradigms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm "Paradigm") for understanding society: [functionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_functionalism "Structural functionalism") (also known as structural functionalism), [conflict theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_theories "Conflict theories"), and [symbolic interactionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism "Symbolic interactionism").

In the view of [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx"), human beings are intrinsically, necessarily and by definition social beings who, beyond being "gregarious creatures", cannot survive and meet their needs other than through social cooperation and association. Their social characteristics are therefore to a large extent an objectively given fact, stamped on them from birth and affirmed by [socialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialization "Socialization") processes; and, according to Marx, in producing and reproducing their material life, people must necessarily enter into [relations of production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relations_of_production "Relations of production") which are "independent of their will".

By contrast, the sociologist [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber"), for example, defines human action as "social" if—by virtue of the subjective meanings attached to the action by individuals—it "takes account of the behavior of others, and is thereby oriented in its course".

#### Functionalism

According to the functionalist school of thought, individuals in society work together like organs in the body to create [emergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence "Emergence") behavior, sometimes referred to as [collective consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_consciousness "Collective consciousness"). 19th century sociologists [Auguste Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte "Auguste Comte") and [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim"), for example, believed that society constitutes a separate "level" of reality, distinct from both biological and inorganic matter. Explanations of [social phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_phenomenon "Social phenomenon") had therefore to be constructed within this level, individuals being merely transient occupants of comparatively stable social roles.

#### Conflict theory

Conflict theorists take the opposite view, and posit that individuals and social groups or [social classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class "Social class") within society interact on the basis of [conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_conflict "Social conflict") rather than agreement. One prominent conflict theorist is [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx"), who conceived of society as operating on an economic ["base" with a "superstructure"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_and_superstructure "Base and superstructure") of government, family, religion, and culture. Marx argues that the economic base determines the superstructure, and that throughout history, societal change has been driven by conflict between [laborers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proletariat "Proletariat") and [those who own](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie "Bourgeoisie") the [means of production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Means_of_production "Means of production").

#### Symbolic interactionism

Symbolic interactionism is a [microsociological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsociology "Microsociology") theory that focuses on individuals and how the individual relates to society. Symbolic interactionists study humans' use of shared language to create common symbols and meanings, and use this frame of reference to understand how individuals interact to create symbolic worlds, and in turn, how these worlds [shape individual behaviors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialization "Socialization").

In the latter half of the 20th century, theorists began to view society as [socially constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructionism "Social constructionism"). In this vein, sociologist [Peter L. Berger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_L._Berger "Peter L. Berger") describes society as "dialectic": Society is created by humans, but this creation in turn creates or molds humans.

#### Non-Western views

The sociological emphasis placed on functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism has been criticized as [Eurocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism "Eurocentrism"). The Malaysian sociologist [Syed Farid al-Attas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syed_Farid_al-Attas "Syed Farid al-Attas"), for example, argues that Western thinkers are particularly interested in the implications of [modernity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernity "Modernity"), and that their analysis of non-Western cultures is therefore limited in scope. As examples of non-Western thinkers who took a systematic approach to understanding society, al-Attas mentions [Ibn Khaldun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun "Ibn Khaldun") (1332–1406) and [José Rizal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Rizal "José Rizal") (1861–1896).

[Khaldun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun "Ibn Khaldun"), an [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs "Arabs") living in the 14th century, understood society, along with the rest of the universe, as having "meaningful configuration", with its perceived randomness attributable to hidden causes. Khaldun conceptualized social structures as having two fundamental forms: nomadic and sedentary. Nomadic life has high social cohesion (*asabijja*), which Khaldun argued arose from kinship, shared customs, and a shared need for defense. Sedentary life, in Khaldun's view, was marked by secularization, decreased social cohesion, and increased interest in luxury. Rizal was a [Filipino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filipinos "Filipinos") nationalist living toward the end of the [Spanish Colonial Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Colonial_Period_(Philippines) "Spanish Colonial Period (Philippines)") who theorized about colonial societies. Rizal argued that [indolence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laziness "Laziness"), which the Spanish used to justify their colonial occupation, was instead caused by the colonial occupation. Rizal compared the pre-colonial era, when the Filipinos controlled trade routes and had higher economic activity, to the period of colonial rule, and argued that exploitation, economic disorder, and colonial policies that discouraged farming led to a decreased interest in work.

## Types

[Sociologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociologists "Sociologists") tend to classify societies based on their level of technology, and place societies in three broad categories: [pre-industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-industrial "Pre-industrial"), [industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_society "Industrial society"), and [post-industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postindustrial "Postindustrial").

Subdivisions of these categories vary, and classifications are often based on level of technology, communication, and economy. One example of such a classification comes from sociologist [Gerhard Lenski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Lenski "Gerhard Lenski"), who lists: (1) hunting and gathering; (2) horticultural; (3) agricultural; and (4) industrial; as well as specialized societies (e.g., fishing or herding).

Some cultures have developed over time toward more complex forms of organization and control. This [cultural evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_evolution "Cultural evolution") has a profound effect on patterns of community. Hunter-gatherer tribes have, at times, settled around seasonal food stocks to become agrarian villages. Villages have grown to become towns and cities. Cities have turned into [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state "City-state") and [nation-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation-state "Nation-state"). However, these processes are not unidirectional.

### Pre-industrial

In a pre-industrial society, food production, which is carried out through the use of human and animal [labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manual_labour "Manual labour"), is the main economic activity. These societies can be subdivided according to their level of technology and their method of producing food. These subdivisions are hunting and gathering, pastoral, horticultural, and agrarian.

#### Hunting and gathering

The main form of food production in hunter-gatherer societies is the daily collection of wild plants and the hunting of wild animals. Hunter-gatherers move around constantly in search of food. As a result, they do not build permanent [villages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villages "Villages") or create a wide variety of [artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_(archaeology) "Artifact (archaeology)"). The need for mobility also limits the size of these societies, and they usually only form small groups such as [bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_society "Band society") and [tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe "Tribe"), usually with fewer than 50 people per community. Bands and tribes are relatively [egalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarian_community "Egalitarian community"), and decisions are reached through [consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_decision-making "Consensus decision-making"). There are no formal political offices containing real power in band societies; rather, a [chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal_chief "Tribal chief") is merely a person of influence, and [leadership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership "Leadership") is based on personal qualities. The family forms the main [social unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_group "Social group"), with most members being related by birth or marriage.

The [anthropologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropologist "Anthropologist") [Marshall Sahlins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Sahlins "Marshall Sahlins") described hunter-gatherers as the "[original affluent society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_affluent_society "Original affluent society")" due to their extended leisure time: Sahlins estimated that adults in hunter-gatherer societies work three to five hours per day. This perspective has been challenged by other researchers, who have pointed out high mortality rates and perennial warfare in hunter-gatherer societies. Proponents of Sahlins' view argue that the general well-being of humans in hunter-gatherer societies challenges the purported relationship between technological advancement and [human progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progress "Progress").

#### Pastoral

Rather than searching for food on a daily basis, members of a [pastoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism "Pastoralism") society rely on domesticated herd animals to meet their food needs. Pastoralists typically live a nomadic life, moving their herds from one pasture to another. Community size in pastoral societies is similar to hunter-gatherers (about 50 individuals), but unlike hunter-gatherers, pastoral societies usually consist of multiple communities—the average pastoral society contains thousands of people. This is because pastoral groups tend to live in open areas where movement is easy, which enables political integration. Pastoral societies tend to create a food surplus, and they have [specialized labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labour "Division of labour") and high levels of inequality.

#### Horticultural

Fruits and vegetables grown in garden plots that have been cleared from the jungle or forest provide the main source of food in a horticultural society. These societies have a similar level of technology and complexity to pastoral societies. Along with pastoral societies, horticultural societies emerged about 10,000 years ago, after technological changes of the [Agricultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution") made it possible to cultivate crops and raise animals. Horticulturists use human labor and simple tools to cultivate the land for one or more seasons. When the land becomes barren, horticulturists clear a new plot and leave the old plot to revert to its natural state. They may return to the original land several years later and begin the process again. By rotating their garden plots, horticulturists can stay in one area for a long period of time. This allows them to build permanent or semi-permanent villages.

As with pastoral societies, surplus food leads to a more complex division of labor. Specialized roles in horticultural societies include craftspeople, [shamans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamans "Shamans") (religious leaders), and traders. This role specialization allows horticultural societies to create a variety of artifacts. Scarce, defensible resources can lead to wealth inequalities in horticultural political systems.

#### Agrarian

[Agrarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrarianism "Agrarianism") societies use agricultural [technological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological "Technological") advances to cultivate crops over a large area. Lenski differentiates between horticultural and agrarian societies by the use of the [plow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough "Plough"). Larger food supplies due to improved technology mean agrarian communities are larger than horticultural communities. A greater food surplus results in towns that become centers of trade. Economic trade in turn leads to increased specialization, including a ruling class, as well as educators, craftspeople, merchants, and religious figures, who do not directly participate in the production of food.

Agrarian societies are especially noted for their extremes of social classes and rigid social mobility. As land is the major source of wealth, social hierarchy develops based on [landownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landownership "Landownership") and not labor. The system of [stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stratification "Social stratification") is characterized by three coinciding contrasts: governing class versus the [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commoner "Commoner"), urban minority versus peasant majority, and literate minority versus illiterate majority. This results in two distinct subcultures: the urban elite versus the peasant masses. Moreover, this means cultural differences within agrarian societies are greater than differences between them.

The landowning strata typically combine government, religious, and military institutions to justify and enforce their ownership, and support elaborate patterns of consumption; [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery "Slavery"), [serfdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfdom "Serfdom"), or [peonage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peonage "Peonage") is commonly the lot of the primary producer. Rulers of agrarian societies often do not manage their empire for the [common good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_good "Common good") or in the name of the [public interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_interest "Public interest"), but as property they own. [Caste systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system_in_India "Caste system in India"), as historically found in South Asia, are associated with agrarian societies, where lifelong agricultural routines depend upon a rigid sense of duty and discipline. The scholar Donald Brown suggests that an emphasis in the modern West on personal liberties and freedoms was in large part a reaction to the steep and rigid stratification of agrarian societies.

### Industrial

Industrial societies, which emerged in the 18th century during the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"), rely heavily on machines powered by external sources for the mass production of goods. Whereas in [pre-industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-industrial_society "Pre-industrial society") societies the majority of labor takes place in primary industries focused on extracting raw materials (farming, fishing, mining, etc.), in industrial societies, labor is mostly focused on processing raw materials into finished products. Present-day societies vary in their degree of industrialization, with some using mostly newer energy sources (e.g., [coal, oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel "Fossil fuel"), and [nuclear energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power")), and others continuing to rely on human and animal power.

Industrialization is associated with population booms and the growth of cities. Increased productivity, as well as the stability caused by improved transportation, leads to decreased mortality and resulting population growth. Centralized production of goods in factories and a decreased need for agricultural labor lead to [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization "Urbanization"). Industrial societies are often [capitalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism "Capitalism"), and they have high degrees of inequality along with high [social mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_mobility "Social mobility"), as [businesspeople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Businessperson "Businessperson") use the market to amass large amounts of wealth. Working conditions in factories are generally restrictive and harsh. Workers, who have common interests, may organize into [labor unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_union "Trade union") to advance those interests.

On the whole, industrial societies are marked by the increased power of human beings. Technological advancements mean that industrial societies have increased potential for deadly [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfare "Warfare"). Governments use [information technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology "Information technology") to exert greater control over the populace. Industrial societies also have an increased [environmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_environment "Natural environment") impact.

### Post-industrial

Post-industrial societies are societies dominated by information and services, rather than the production of goods. Advanced industrial societies see a shift toward an increase in service sectors, over manufacturing. Service industries include education, health, and finance.

#### Information

An information society is a society where the usage, [creation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_creation "Content creation"), [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_distribution "Information distribution"), manipulation, and [integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_integration "Information integration") of information is a significant activity. Proponents of the idea that modern-day global society is an information society posit that information technologies are impacting most important forms of social organization, including education, economy, health, government, [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfare "Warfare"), and levels of democracy. Although the concept of information society has been discussed since the 1930s, in the present day, it is almost always applied to ways that information technologies impact society and culture. It therefore covers the effects of computers and [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications "Telecommunications") on the home, the workplace, schools, government, and various communities and organizations, as well as the emergence of new social forms in cyberspace.

#### Knowledge

As access to electronic information resources increased at the beginning of the 21st century, special attention was extended from the information society to the knowledge society. A knowledge society generates, shares, and makes available to all members of the society knowledge that may be used to improve the [human condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_condition "Human condition"). A knowledge society differs from an information society in that it transforms information into resources that allow society to take effective action, rather than only creating and disseminating [raw data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_data "Raw data").

## Characteristics

### Norms and roles

[Social norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_norm "Social norm") are shared standards of [acceptable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceptance "Acceptance") behavior by groups. Social norms—which can be both informal understandings that govern the behavior of members of a society, as well as be codified into [rules](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rule "wiktionary:rule") and laws—are powerful drivers of human behavior.

[Social roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_roles "Social roles") are norms, [duties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duty "Duty"), and patterns of behavior that relate to an individual's social status. In functionalist thought, individuals form the structure of society by occupying social roles. According to symbolic interactionism, individuals use symbols to navigate and communicate roles. [Erving Goffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erving_Goffman "Erving Goffman") used the [metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor "Metaphor") of a theater to develop the [dramaturgical lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgy_(sociology) "Dramaturgy (sociology)"), which argues that roles provide scripts that govern social interactions.

### Gender and kinship

The division of humans into male and female gender roles has been marked culturally by a corresponding division of norms, [practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practice_(social_theory) "Practice (social theory)"), [dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothing "Clothing"), [behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_behavior "Social behavior"), rights, duties, [privileges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privilege_(social_inequality) "Privilege (social inequality)"), [status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_status "Social status"), and [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_(social_and_political) "Power (social and political)"). Some argue that gender roles arise naturally from [sex differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism "Sexual dimorphism"), which lead to a division of labor where women take on [reproductive labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_labor "Reproductive labor") and other domestic roles. Gender roles have varied historically, and challenges to predominant gender norms have recurred in many societies.

All human societies organize, recognize, and classify types of social relationships based on relations between parents, children, and other descendants ([consanguinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consanguinity "Consanguinity")), and relations through marriage ([affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinity_(law) "Affinity (law)")). There is also a third type of familial relationship applied to godparents or [adoptive children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption "Adoption") ([fictive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictive_kinship "Fictive kinship")). These culturally defined relationships are referred to as kinship. In many societies, it is one of the most important social organizing principles and plays a role in transmitting status and [inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance "Inheritance"). All societies have rules of [incest taboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest_taboo "Incest taboo"), according to which marriage between certain kinds of kin relations is prohibited; and some societies also have rules of [preferential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preference "Preference") marriage with certain other kin relations.

### Ethnicity

Human ethnic groups are a social category that [identify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_(social_science) "Identity (social science)") together as a group based on shared attributes that distinguish them from other groups. These shared attributes can be a common set of traditions, [ancestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestry "Ancestry"), [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language "Language"), history, society, culture, nation, religion, or social treatment within their residing area. There is no generally accepted definition of what constitutes an ethnic group, and humans have evolved the ability to change affiliation with social groups relatively easily, including leaving groups with previously strong [alliances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance "Alliance"), if doing so is seen as providing personal advantages. Ethnicity is separate from the concept of [race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_(human_categorization) "Race (human categorization)"), which is based on physical characteristics, although both are [socially constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructionism "Social constructionism"). Assigning ethnicity to a certain population is complicated, as even within common ethnic designations there can be a diverse range of subgroups, and the makeup of these ethnic groups can change over time at both the collective and individual level. Ethnic groupings can play a powerful role in the [social identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_identity "Social identity") and [solidarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidarity "Solidarity") of ethnopolitical units. Ethnic identity has been closely tied to the rise of the [nation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_state "Nation state") as the predominant form of political organization in the 19th and 20th centuries.

### Government and politics

Governments create laws and [policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policies "Policies") that affect the people that they govern. There have been [many forms of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_forms_of_government "List of forms of government") throughout human history, with various ways of allocating power, and with different levels and means of control over the population. In early history, distribution of political power was determined by the availability of [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water "Fresh water"), [fertile soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility "Soil fertility"), and [temperate climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_climate "Temperate climate") of different locations. As farming populations gathered in larger and denser communities, interactions between different groups increased, leading to the further development of governance within and between communities.

As of 2022[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Society&action=edit), according to *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist "The Economist")*, 43% of national governments were [democracies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy "Democracy"), 35% [autocracies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocracy "Autocracy"), and 22% containing elements of both. Many countries have formed [international political organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_organization "Intergovernmental organization") and alliances, the largest being the United Nations with 193 member states.

### Trade and economics

Trade, the voluntary exchange of goods and services, has long been an aspect of human societies, and it is seen as a characteristic that differentiates humans from other animals. Trade has even been cited as a practice that gave *[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens")* a major advantage over other hominids; evidence suggests early *H. sapiens* made use of long-distance trade routes to exchange goods and ideas, leading to [cultural explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_explosion "Cultural explosion") and providing additional food sources when hunting was sparse. Such trade networks did not exist for the now-extinct [Neanderthals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neanderthal "Neanderthal"). Early trade involved materials for creating tools, like [obsidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian "Obsidian"), exchanged over short distances. In contrast, throughout antiquity and the medieval period, some of the most influential long-distance routes carried food and luxury goods, such as the [spice trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_trade "Spice trade").

Early human [economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy "Economy") were more likely to be based around [gift-giving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gift_economy "Gift economy") than a [bartering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barter "Barter") system. Early money consisted of [commodities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_money "Commodity money"); the oldest being in the form of cattle and the most widely used being [cowrie shells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowrie_shells "Cowrie shells"). Money has since evolved into government-issued [coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins "Coins"), [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_money "Paper money"), and [electronic money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_money "Electronic money"). Human study of economics is a [social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science") that looks at how societies distribute scarce resources among different people. There are massive [inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality "Economic inequality") in the division of wealth among humans; as of 2018 in China, Europe, and the United States, the richest tenth of humans hold more than seven-tenths of those regions' total wealth.

### Conflict

The willingness of humans to kill other members of their species *en masse* through organized conflict (i.e., war) has long been the subject of debate. One school of thought is that war evolved as a means to eliminate competitors, and that violence is an innate human characteristic. Humans commit violence against other humans at a rate comparable to other primates (although humans kill adults at a relatively high rate and have a relatively low rate of [infanticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide_(zoology) "Infanticide (zoology)")).

Another school of thought suggests that war is a relatively recent phenomenon and appeared due to changing social conditions. While not settled, the current evidence suggests warlike behavior only became common about 10,000 years ago, and in many regions even more recently.

[Phylogenetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylogenetics "Phylogenetics") analysis predicts 2% of human deaths to be caused by [homicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homicide "Homicide"), which approximately matches the rate of homicide in band societies. However, rates of violence vary widely according to societal norms, and rates of homicide in societies that have [legal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_systems "Legal systems") and strong cultural attitudes against violence stand at about 0.01%.
