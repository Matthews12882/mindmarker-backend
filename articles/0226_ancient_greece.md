# Ancient Greece

**Ancient Greece** ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): Ἑλλάς, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"): *Hellás*) was an ancient [civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization "Civilization") existing from the [Greek Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages "Greek Dark Ages") of the 12th–9th centuries BC to the end of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") (c. 600 AD), comprising a loose collection of [culturally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Greece "Culture of Greece") and [linguistically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_languages "Hellenic languages") related [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis "Polis") and [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_Ancient_Greece "Regions of Ancient Greece"), primarily centered in the northeastern area of the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea"). Prior to the [Roman period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece_in_the_Roman_era "Greece in the Roman era"), most of this area was [officially unified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Corinth "League of Corinth") only once, from 338 to 323 BC under the [Kingdom of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Macedon "Kingdom of Macedon"). In [Western history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world"), the era of classical antiquity was immediately followed by the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages "Early Middle Ages") and the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine "Byzantine") period.

Scholars mark Ancient Greece as beginning with [Mycenaean Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece "Mycenaean Greece"), which declined during the [Bronze Age collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_collapse "Bronze Age collapse") c. 1100 BC. Three centuries later, Greek urban city-states, or *[poleis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poleis "Poleis")*, began to form in the 8th century BC, ushering in the [Archaic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece "Archaic Greece") and [Greek colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonisation "Greek colonisation") of the Mediterranean Basin. This was followed by the intellectual flourishing of the [Classical period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece "Classical Greece") of Ancient Greece, from the [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars "Greco-Persian Wars") to the death of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") in 323 BC, which included the [Golden Age of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age_of_Athens "Golden Age of Athens"), the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War "Peloponnesian War") between [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens "Classical Athens") and [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta "Sparta"), the short-lived [Spartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartan_hegemony "Spartan hegemony") and [Theban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theban_hegemony "Theban hegemony") hegemonies, the creation of the Hellenic league led by [Philip II of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon") and the subsequent conquest of Persia's [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") by his son Alexander the Great. Alexander's territorial conquests led to the spread of Greek culture into Northern Africa and Western Asia: the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"), ending c. 30 BC, when the [Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_kingdom "Ptolemaic kingdom") of Egypt, centred in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"), was annexed by [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome").

Classical Greek culture, especially [Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophy "Greek philosophy"), had a powerful influence on [Ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome"), which propagated variants of it throughout the Mediterranean and much of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"). For this reason, Classical Greece is generally considered the [cradle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization "Cradle of civilization") of [Western civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture "Western culture"), the [seminal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/seminal "wikt:seminal") culture from which the modern West derives many of its founding archetypes and ideas in politics, philosophy, science, and art.

## Chronology

[Classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") in the Mediterranean region is commonly considered to have begun in the 8th century BC (around the time of the earliest recorded poetry of Homer) and ended in the 6th century AD.

Classical antiquity in Greece was preceded by the [Greek Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages "Greek Dark Ages") (c. 1200 – c. 800 BC), [archaeologically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeologically "Archaeologically") characterised by the [protogeometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protogeometric "Protogeometric") and [geometric styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_art "Geometric art") of designs on pottery. Following the Dark Ages was the [Archaic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece "Archaic Greece"), beginning around the 8th century BC, which saw early developments in Greek culture and society leading to the [Classical period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece "Classical Greece") from the Persian invasion of Greece in 480 BC until the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC. The Classical period is characterized by a "classical" style, i.e. one which was considered exemplary by later observers, most famously in the [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon "Parthenon") of Athens. Politically, the Classical period was dominated by [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens "Classical Athens") and the [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League "Delian League") during the 5th century, but displaced by [Spartan hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartan_hegemony "Spartan hegemony") during the early 4th century BC, before power shifted to [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Thebes_(Boeotia) "Ancient Thebes (Boeotia)") and the [Boeotian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeotian_League "Boeotian League") and finally to the [League of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Corinth "League of Corinth") led by [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon "Macedon"). This period was shaped by the [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars "Greco-Persian Wars"), the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War "Peloponnesian War"), and the [Rise of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_of_Macedon "Rise of Macedon").

Following the Classical period was the Hellenistic period (323–146 BC), during which Greek culture and power expanded into the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East "Near East") from the death of Alexander until the Roman conquest. [Roman Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece "Roman Greece") is usually counted from the Roman victory over the [Corinthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth "Ancient Corinth") at the [Battle of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Corinth_(146_BC) "Battle of Corinth (146 BC)") in 146 BC to the establishment of [Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantium "Byzantium") by [Constantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_I "Constantine I") as the capital of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") in 330 AD. Finally, [Late Antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Antiquity "Late Antiquity") refers to the period of [Christianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_of_Greco-Roman_polytheism "Decline of Greco-Roman polytheism") during the later 4th to early 6th centuries AD, consummated by the closure of the [Academy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato%27s_Academy "Plato's Academy") by [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I") in 529.

## Historiography

The historical period of ancient Greece is unique in world history as the first period attested directly in comprehensive, narrative [historiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography "Historiography"), while earlier ancient history or [protohistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protohistory "Protohistory") is known from much more fragmentary documents such as annals, king lists, and pragmatic [epigraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigraphy "Epigraphy").

[Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") is widely known as the "father of history": his *[Histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_(Herodotus) "Histories (Herodotus)")* are eponymous of [the entire field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History "History"). Written between the 450s and 420s BC, Herodotus' work reaches about a century into the past, discussing 6th-century BC historical figures such as [Darius I of Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I_of_Persia "Darius I of Persia"), [Cambyses II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambyses_II "Cambyses II") and [Psamtik III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psamtik_III "Psamtik III"), and alluding to some 8th-century BC persons such as [Candaules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candaules "Candaules"). The accuracy of Herodotus' works is debated.

Herodotus was succeeded by authors such as [Thucydides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thucydides "Thucydides"), [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon "Xenophon"), [Demosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosthenes "Demosthenes"), [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"). Most were either Athenian or pro-Athenian, which is why far more is known about the history and politics of Athens than of many other cities.
Their scope is further limited by a focus on political, military and diplomatic history, ignoring economic and social history.

## History

### Archaic period

The archaic period, lasting approximately from 800 to 500 BC, saw the culmination of political and social developments which had begun in the Greek Dark Age, with the *polis* (city-state) becoming the most important unit of political organisation in Greece. The absence of powerful states in Greece after the collapse of Mycenaean power, and the geography of Greece, where many settlements were separated from their neighbours by mountainous terrain, encouraged the development of small independent city-states. Several Greek states saw tyrants rise to power in this period, most famously at [Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth "Ancient Corinth") from 657 BC. The period also saw the founding of Greek colonies around the Mediterranean, with [Euboean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euboea "Euboea") settlements at [Al-Mina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mina "Al-Mina") in the east as early as 800 BC, and [Ischia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ischia "Ischia") in the west by 775. Increasing contact with non-Greek peoples in this period, especially in the Near East, inspired developments in art and architecture, the adoption of coinage, and the development of the Greek alphabet.

Athens developed its democratic system over the course of the archaic period. Already in the 7th century, the right of all citizen men to attend the [assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_(ancient_Greece) "Ecclesia (ancient Greece)") appears to have been established. After a failed coup led by [Cylon of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylon_of_Athens "Cylon of Athens") around 636 BC, [Draco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draco_(lawgiver) "Draco (lawgiver)") was appointed to establish a code of laws in 621. This failed to reduce the political tension between the poor and the elites, and in 594 [Solon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon "Solon") was given the authority to enact another set of reforms, which attempted to balance the power of the rich and the poor. In the middle of the 6th century, [Pisistratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisistratus "Pisistratus") established himself as a tyrant, and after his death in 527 his son [Hippias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippias "Hippias") inherited his position; by the end of the 6th century he had been overthrown, and [Cleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleisthenes "Cleisthenes") carried out further democratising reforms.

In Sparta, a political system with two kings, a [council of elders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerousia "Gerousia"), and five [ephors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephors "Ephors") developed over the course of the 8th and 7th centuries. According to Spartan tradition, this constitution was established by the legendary lawgiver [Lycurgus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycurgus "Lycurgus"). Over the course of the [First Messenian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Messenian_War "First Messenian War") and [Second Messenian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Messenian_War "Second Messenian War"), Sparta subjugated the neighbouring region of [Messenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messenia "Messenia"), enserfing the population.

In the 6th century, Greek city-states began to develop formal relationships with one another, where previously individual rulers had relied on personal relationships with the elites of other cities. Towards the end of the Archaic period, Sparta began to build a series of alliances, the [Peloponnesian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_League "Peloponnesian League"), with cities including [Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth "Ancient Corinth"), [Elis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elis "Elis"), and [Megara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megara "Megara"), isolating Messenia and reinforcing Sparta's position against [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argos,_Peloponnese "Argos, Peloponnese"), the other major power in the Peloponnese. Other alliances in the 6th century included those between Elis and [Heraea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraea_(Arcadia) "Heraea (Arcadia)") in the Peloponnese; and between the Greek colony [Sybaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sybaris "Sybaris") in southern Italy, its allies, and the Serdaioi.

### Classical Greece

In 499 BC, the [Ionian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia "Ionia") city-states under Persian rule rebelled against their Persian-supported tyrant rulers. Supported by troops sent from Athens and [Eretria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eretria "Eretria"), they advanced as far as [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis") and burnt the city before being driven back by a Persian counterattack. The revolt continued until 494, when the rebelling Ionians were defeated. Darius did not forget that Athens had assisted the Ionian revolt, and in 490 he assembled an armada to retaliate. Though heavily outnumbered, the Athenians—supported by their [Plataean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plataea "Plataea") allies—defeated the Persian hordes at the [Battle of Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marathon "Battle of Marathon"), and the Persian fleet turned tail.

Ten years later, a [second invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece "Second Persian invasion of Greece") was launched by Darius' son [Xerxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I"). The city-states of northern and central Greece submitted to the Persian forces without resistance, but a coalition of 31 Greek city-states, including Athens and Sparta, determined to resist the Persian invaders. At the same time, Greek Sicily was invaded by a Carthaginian force. In 480 BC, the first major battle of the invasion was fought at [Thermopylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae "Battle of Thermopylae"), where a small rearguard of Greeks, led by three hundred Spartans, held a crucial pass guarding the heart of Greece for several days; at the same time [Gelon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelo "Gelo"), tyrant of Syracuse, defeated the Carthaginian invasion at the [Battle of Himera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Himera_(480_BC) "Battle of Himera (480 BC)").

The Persians were decisively defeated at sea by a primarily Athenian naval force at the [Battle of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis "Battle of Salamis"), and on land in 479 BC at the [Battle of Plataea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Plataea "Battle of Plataea"). The alliance against Persia continued, initially led by the Spartan [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_(general) "Pausanias (general)") but from 477 by Athens, and by 460 Persia had been driven out of the Aegean. During this long campaign, the [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League "Delian League") gradually transformed from a defensive alliance of Greek states into an Athenian empire, as Athens' growing naval power intimidated the other league states. Athens ended its campaigns against Persia in 450, after a disastrous defeat in Egypt in 454, and the death of [Cimon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimon "Cimon") in action against the Persians on Cyprus in 450.

As the Athenian fight against the Persian empire waned, conflict grew between Athens and Sparta. Suspicious of the increasing Athenian power funded by the Delian League, Sparta offered aid to reluctant members of the League to rebel against Athenian domination. These tensions were exacerbated in 462 BC when Athens sent a force to aid Sparta in overcoming a [helot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helot "Helot") revolt, but this aid was rejected by the Spartans. In the 450s, Athens took control of Boeotia, and won victories over [Aegina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegina "Aegina") and Corinth. However, Athens failed to win a decisive victory, and in 447 lost Boeotia again. Athens and Sparta signed the [Thirty Years' Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years%27_Peace "Thirty Years' Peace") in the winter of 446/445, ending the conflict.

Despite the treaty, Athenian relations with Sparta declined again in the 430s, and in 431 BC the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War "Peloponnesian War") began. The [first phase of the war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archidamian_War "Archidamian War") saw a series of fruitless annual invasions of Attica by Sparta, while Athens successfully fought the Corinthian empire in northwest Greece and defended its own empire, despite a [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens "Plague of Athens") which killed the leading Athenian statesman [Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles "Pericles"). The war turned after Athenian victories led by [Cleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleon "Cleon") at [Pylos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Pylos "Battle of Pylos") and [Sphakteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sphacteria "Battle of Sphacteria"), and Sparta sued for peace, but the Athenians rejected the proposal. The Athenian failure to regain control of Boeotia at [Delium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Delium "Battle of Delium") and [Brasidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brasidas "Brasidas")' successes in northern Greece in 424 improved Sparta's position after Sphakteria. After the deaths of Cleon and Brasidas, the strongest proponents of war on each side, [a peace treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Nikias "Peace of Nikias") was negotiated in 421 by the Athenian general [Nicias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicias "Nicias").

The peace did not last, however. In 418 BC allied forces of Athens and Argos were defeated by Sparta at [Mantinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mantinea_(418_BC) "Battle of Mantinea (418 BC)"). In 415 Athens launched an ambitious [naval expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicilian_Expedition "Sicilian Expedition") to dominate Sicily; the expedition ended in disaster at the harbor of [Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse,_Sicily "Syracuse, Sicily"), with almost the entire army killed, and the ships destroyed. Soon after the Athenian defeat in Syracuse, Athens' Ionian allies began to rebel against the Delian league, while Persia began to once again involve itself in Greek affairs on the Spartan side. Initially the Athenian position continued relatively strong, with important victories at [Cyzicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cyzicus "Battle of Cyzicus") in 410 and [Arginusae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arginusae "Battle of Arginusae") in 406. However, in 405 the Spartan [Lysander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysander "Lysander") defeated Athens in the [Battle of Aegospotami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Aegospotami "Battle of Aegospotami"), and began to blockade Athens' harbour; driven by hunger, Athens sued for peace, agreeing to surrender their fleet and join the Spartan-led Peloponnesian League. Following the Athenian surrender, Sparta installed an oligarchic regime, the [Thirty Tyrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Tyrants "Thirty Tyrants"), in Athens, one of a number of Spartan-backed oligarchies which rose to power after the Peloponnesian war. Spartan predominance did not last: after only a year, the Thirty had been overthrown.

The first half of the 4th century saw the major Greek states attempt to dominate the mainland; none were successful, and their resulting weakness led to a power vacuum which was eventually filled by Macedon under Philip II and then Alexander the Great. In the immediate aftermath of the Peloponnesian war, Sparta attempted to extend their own power, leading Argos, Athens, Corinth, and Thebes to join against them. Aiming to prevent any single Greek state gaining the dominance that would allow it to challenge Persia, the Persian king initially joined the alliance against Sparta, before imposing the [Peace of Antalcidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Antalcidas "Peace of Antalcidas") ("King's Peace") which restored Persia's control over the Anatolian Greeks.

By 371 BC, Thebes was in the ascendancy, defeating Sparta at the [Battle of Leuctra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leuctra "Battle of Leuctra"), killing the Spartan king [Cleombrotus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleombrotus_I "Cleombrotus I"), and invading Laconia. Further Theban successes against Sparta in 369 led to Messenia gaining independence; Sparta never recovered from the loss of Messenia's fertile land and the helot workforce it provided. The rising power of Thebes led Sparta and Athens to join forces; in 362 they were defeated by Thebes at the [Battle of Mantinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mantinea_(362_BC) "Battle of Mantinea (362 BC)"). In the aftermath of Mantinea, none of the major Greek states were able to dominate. Though Thebes had won the battle, their general [Epaminondas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epaminondas "Epaminondas") was killed, and they spent the following decades embroiled in wars with their neighbours; Athens, meanwhile, saw its second naval alliance, formed in 377, collapse in the mid-350s.

The power vacuum in Greece after the Battle of Mantinea was filled by Macedon, under [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon"), starting from the [Battle of Crocus field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Crocus_field "Battle of Crocus field"). In 338 BC, he defeated a Greek alliance at the [Battle of Chaeronea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chaeronea_(338_BC) "Battle of Chaeronea (338 BC)"), and subsequently formed the [League of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Corinth "League of Corinth"). Philip planned to lead the League to invade Persia, but was murdered in 336 BC. His son [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") was left to fulfill his father's ambitions. After campaigns against Macedon's western and northern enemies, and those Greek states that had broken from the League of Corinth following the death of Philip, Alexander began his campaign against Persia in 334 BC. He conquered Persia, defeating [Darius III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_III "Darius III") at the [Battle of Issus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Issus "Battle of Issus") in 333 BC, and after the [Battle of Gaugamela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gaugamela "Battle of Gaugamela") in 331 BC proclaimed himself king of Asia. From 329 BC he led expeditions to [Bactria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Alexander_the_Great#Bactria "Wars of Alexander the Great") and then [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_campaign_of_Alexander_the_Great "Indian campaign of Alexander the Great"); further plans to invade Arabia and North Africa were halted by his death in 323 BC.

### Hellenistic period

The period from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC until the death of [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra"), the last Macedonian ruler of Egypt, is known as the Hellenistic period. In the early part of this period, a new form of kingship developed based on Macedonian and Near Eastern traditions. The first Hellenistic kings were previously Alexander's generals, and took power in the period following his death, though they were not part of existing royal lineages and lacked historic claims to the territories they controlled. The most important of these rulers in the decades after Alexander's death were [Antigonus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigonus_I "Antigonus I") and his son [Demetrius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Macedon "Demetrius I of Macedon") in Macedonia and the rest of Greece, [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_I_Soter "Ptolemy I Soter") in Egypt, and [Seleucus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I "Seleucus I") in Syria and the former Persian empire; smaller Hellenistic kingdoms included [Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epirus_(ancient_state) "Epirus (ancient state)") under the reign of [Pyrrhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrhus_of_Epirus "Pyrrhus of Epirus"), the [Attalids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attalid "Attalid") in Anatolia and the [Greco-Bactrian kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_kingdom "Greco-Bactrian kingdom").

In the early part of the Hellenistic period, the exact borders of the Hellenistic kingdoms were not settled. Antigonus attempted to expand his territory by attacking the other successor kingdoms until they joined against him, and he was killed at the [Battle of Ipsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ipsus "Battle of Ipsus") in 301 BC. His son Demetrius spent many years in Seleucid captivity, and his son, [Antigonus II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigonus_II "Antigonus II"), only reclaimed the Macedonian throne around 276. Meanwhile, the Seleucid kingdom gave up territory in the east to the Indian king [Chandragupta Maurya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandragupta_Maurya "Chandragupta Maurya") in exchange for war elephants, and later lost large parts of Persia to the [Parthian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire"). By the mid-3rd century, the kingdoms of Alexander's successors were mostly stable, though there continued to be disputes over border areas.

In mainland Greece, city-states grouped themselves into alliances, allowing them to compete against the power of the successor monarchies. The [Aetolian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aetolian_League "Aetolian League") which had begun in the fourth century expanded in the third to include first [Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi "Delphi"), then city-states as far away as [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete "Crete"), [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"), and on the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea"). In the 280s BC, the [Achaean League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaean_League "Achaean League") formed in the Peloponnese.

The conquests of Alexander had numerous consequences for the Greek city-states. It greatly widened the horizons of the Greeks and led to a steady emigration of the young and ambitious to the new Greek empires in the east. Many Greeks migrated to Alexandria, Antioch and the many other new Hellenistic cities founded in Alexander's wake, as far away as present-day [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan "Afghanistan") and [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan"), where the [Greco-Bactrian Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_Kingdom "Greco-Bactrian Kingdom") and the [Indo-Greek Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom "Indo-Greek Kingdom") survived until the end of the 1st century BC.

The Antigonid Kingdom became involved in a war with the Roman Republic in the late 3rd century. Although the [First Macedonian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Macedonian_War "First Macedonian War") was inconclusive, the Romans, in typical fashion, continued to fight Macedon until it was completely absorbed into the Roman Republic (by 149 BC). In the east, the unwieldy Seleucid Empire gradually disintegrated, although a rump survived until 64 BC, whilst the Ptolemaic Kingdom continued in Egypt until 30 BC when it too was conquered by the Romans. The Aetolian league grew wary of Roman involvement in Greece, and sided with the Seleucids in the [Roman–Seleucid War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman%E2%80%93Seleucid_War "Roman–Seleucid War"); when the Romans were victorious, the league was effectively absorbed into the Republic. Although the Achaean league outlasted both the Aetolian league and Macedon, it was also soon [defeated and absorbed by the Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaean_War#War "Achaean War") in 146 BC, bringing Greek independence to an end.

### Roman Greece

The Greek peninsula came under Roman rule during the 146 BC conquest of Greece after the Battle of Corinth. [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(Roman_province) "Macedonia (Roman province)") became a [Roman province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province "Roman province") while southern Greece came under the surveillance of Macedonia's [prefect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefect "Prefect"); however, some Greek *poleis* managed to maintain a partial independence and avoid taxation. The [Aegean Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Islands "Aegean Islands") were added to this territory in 133 BC. Athens and other Greek cities [revolted in 88 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolted_in_88_BC "Revolted in 88 BC"), and the peninsula was crushed by the Roman general [Sulla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulla "Sulla"). The Roman civil wars devastated the land even further, until [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus "Augustus") organized the peninsula as the province of [Achaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaea_(province) "Achaea (province)") in 27 BC.

Greece was a key eastern province of the Roman Empire, as the [Roman culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_culture "Roman culture") had long been in fact [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman "Greco-Roman"). The [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek "Koine Greek") served as a *[lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca "Lingua franca")* in the East and in [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italia_(Roman_province) "Italia (Roman province)"), and many Greek intellectuals such as [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen "Galen") would perform most of their work in Rome.

## Geography

### Regions

The territory of Greece is mountainous, and as a result, ancient Greece consisted of many smaller regions, each with its own dialect, cultural peculiarities, and identity. Regionalism and regional conflicts were prominent features of ancient Greece. Cities tended to be located in valleys between mountains, or on coastal plains, and dominated a certain area around them.

In the south lay the [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese "Peloponnese"), consisting of the regions of Laconia (southeast), Messenia (southwest), Elis (west), Achaia (north), Korinthia (northeast), Argolis (east), and Arcadia (center). These names survive to the present day as [regional units of modern Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_units_of_Greece "Regional units of Greece"), though with somewhat different boundaries. Mainland Greece to the north, nowadays known as [Central Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Greece_(geographic_region) "Central Greece (geographic region)"), consisted of [Aetolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aetolia "Aetolia") and [Acarnania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acarnania "Acarnania") in the west, [Locris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locris "Locris"), [Doris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doris_(Greece) "Doris (Greece)"), and [Phocis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phocis_(ancient_region) "Phocis (ancient region)") in the center, while in the east lay [Boeotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeotia "Boeotia"), [Attica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attica "Attica"), and [Megaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaris "Megaris"). Northeast lay [Thessaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaly "Thessaly"), while [Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epirus "Epirus") lay to the northwest. Epirus stretched from the [Ambracian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambracian_Gulf "Ambracian Gulf") in the south to the [Ceraunian Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceraunian_Mountains "Ceraunian Mountains") and the [Aoos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aoos "Aoos") river in the north, and consisted of [Chaonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaonia "Chaonia") (north), [Molossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molossia "Molossia") (center), and Thesprotia (south). In the northeast corner was [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom) "Macedonia (ancient kingdom)"), originally consisting [Lower Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Macedonia "Lower Macedonia") and its regions, such as [Emathia (Macedonia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emathia_(Macedonia) "Emathia (Macedonia)"), [Pieria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieria_(regional_unit) "Pieria (regional unit)"), and [Bottiaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottiaea "Bottiaea"), and later [Almopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almopia "Almopia"), [Amphaxitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphaxitis "Amphaxitis"). The original capital of Macedon was [Aigai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegae_(Macedonia) "Aegae (Macedonia)") and then moved to [Pella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pella "Pella") in the 4th century BC. Around the time of [Alexander I of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Macedon "Alexander I of Macedon"), the [Argead kings of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argead_dynasty "Argead dynasty") started to expand into [Eordaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eordaia "Eordaia") and the rest of [Upper Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Macedonia "Upper Macedonia"), lands inhabited by independent Upper [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians "Ancient Macedonians") tribes like the [Lyncestae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyncestae "Lyncestae"), [Orestae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orestae "Orestae") and the [Elimiotae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elimiotis "Elimiotis") and to the east, beyond the [Axius river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axius_river "Axius river"), into [Mygdonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mygdonia "Mygdonia") and regions settled by Thracian tribes. To the north of Macedonia lay various non-Greek peoples such as the [Paeonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paeonians "Paeonians") due north, the [Thracians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thracians "Thracians") to the northeast, and the [Illyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrians "Illyrians"), with whom the Macedonians were frequently in conflict, to the northwest. [Chalcidice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcidice "Chalcidice") was settled early on by southern Greek colonists and was considered part of the Greek world, while from the late 2nd millennium BC substantial Greek settlement also occurred on the eastern shores of the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea "Aegean Sea"), in [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia").

### Colonies

During the [Archaic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece "Archaic Greece"), the Greek [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_demography#Ancient_Greece_and_Greek_colonies "Classical demography") grew beyond the capacity of the limited [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land "Arable land") of Greece proper, [resulting in the large-scale establishment of colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonisation "Greek colonisation") elsewhere: according to one estimate, the population of the widening area of Greek settlement increased roughly ten-fold from 800 to 400 BC, from 800,000 to as many as 7+1⁄2–10 million. This was not simply for trade, but also to found settlements. These [Greek colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonies "Greek colonies") were not, as Roman colonies were, dependent on their mother-city, but were independent city-states in their own right.

Greeks settled outside of Greece in two distinct ways. The first was in permanent settlements founded by Greeks, which formed as independent poleis. The second form was in what historians refer to as *[emporia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emporium_(antiquity) "Emporium (antiquity)")*; trading posts which were occupied by both Greeks and non-Greeks and which were primarily concerned with the manufacture and sale of goods. Examples of this latter type of settlement are found at [Al Mina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Mina "Al Mina") in the east and [Pithekoussai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pithekoussai "Pithekoussai") in the west. From around 750 to 500 BC, Greeks settled colonies in all directions. To the east, the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea "Aegean Sea") coast of [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor") was colonized first, followed by [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history_of_Cyprus "Ancient history of Cyprus") and the coasts of [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace"), the [Sea of Marmara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Marmara "Sea of Marmara") and the south coast of the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea").

Eventually, Greek colonization reached as far northeast as present-day [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine") and Russia ([Taganrog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taganrog "Taganrog")). To the west the coasts of [Illyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria "Illyria"), [Southern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Italy "Southern Italy") (called "[Magna Graecia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Graecia "Magna Graecia")") were settled, followed by [Southern France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_France "Southern France"), [Corsica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsica "Corsica"), and even eastern [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain "Spain"). Greek colonies were also founded in [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Libya "Ancient Libya"). Modern [Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse,_Italy "Syracuse, Italy"), [Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naples "Naples"), [Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marseille "Marseille") and [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul "Istanbul") had their beginnings as the Greek colonies Syracusae (Συράκουσαι), Neapolis (Νεάπολις), Massalia (Μασσαλία) and [Byzantion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantion "Byzantion") (Βυζάντιον). These colonies played an important role in the spread of Greek influence throughout Europe and also aided in the establishment of long-distance trading networks between the Greek city-states, boosting the [economy of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_ancient_Greece "Economy of ancient Greece").

## Politics and society

### Political structure

Ancient Greece consisted of several hundred relatively independent [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state "City-state") (*[poleis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poleis "Poleis")*). This was a situation unlike that in most other contemporary societies, which were either [tribal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal "Tribal") or [kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realm "Realm") ruling over relatively large territories. Undoubtedly, the [geography of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Greece "Geography of Greece")—divided and sub-divided by hills, mountains, and rivers—contributed to the fragmentary nature of ancient Greece. On the one hand, the ancient Greeks had no doubt that they were "one people"; they had the same [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion "State religion"), same basic culture, and same language. Furthermore, the Greeks were very aware of their tribal origins; [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") was able to extensively categorise the city-states by tribe. Yet, although these higher-level relationships existed, they seem to have rarely had a major role in Greek politics. The independence of the *poleis* was fiercely defended; unification was something rarely contemplated by the ancient Greeks. Even when, during the second Persian invasion of Greece, a group of city-states allied themselves to defend Greece, the vast majority of *poleis* remained neutral, and after the Persian defeat, the allies quickly returned to infighting.

Thus, the major peculiarities of the ancient Greek political system were its fragmented nature (and that this does not particularly seem to have tribal origin), and the particular focus on urban centers within otherwise tiny states. The peculiarities of the Greek system are further evidenced by the colonies that they set up throughout the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean "Mediterranean"), which, though they might count a certain Greek *polis* as their 'mother' (and remain sympathetic to her), were completely independent of the founding city.

Inevitably smaller *poleis* might be dominated by larger neighbors, but conquest or direct rule by another city-state appears to have been quite rare. Instead the *poleis* grouped themselves into leagues, membership of which was in a constant state of flux. Later in the Classical period, the leagues would become fewer and larger, be dominated by one city (particularly [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"), [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta "Sparta") and [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece "Thebes, Greece")); and often *poleis* would be compelled to join under threat of war (or as part of a peace treaty). Even after [Philip II of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon") conquered the heartlands of ancient Greece, he did not attempt to annex the territory or unify it into a new province, but compelled most of the *poleis* to join his own [Corinthian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_League "Corinthian League").

### Government and law

Initially many Greek city-states seem to have been petty kingdoms; there was often a city official carrying some residual, ceremonial functions of the king (*[basileus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basileus "Basileus")*), e.g., the *[archon basileus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archon_basileus "Archon basileus")* in Athens. However, by the Archaic period and the first historical consciousness, most had already become aristocratic [oligarchies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligarchies "Oligarchies"). It is unclear exactly how this change occurred. For instance, in Athens, the kingship had been reduced to a hereditary, lifelong chief magistracy (*[archon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archon_of_Athens "Archon of Athens")*) by c. 1050 BC; by 753 BC this had become a decennial, elected archonship, and finally an annually elected archonship by 683 BC. Through each stage, more power would have been transferred to the aristocracy as a whole, and away from a single individual.

Inevitably, the domination of politics and concomitant aggregation of wealth by small groups of families was apt to cause social unrest in many *poleis*. In many cities a [tyrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrant "Tyrant") (not in the modern sense of repressive autocracies), would at some point seize control and govern according to their own will; often a populist agenda would help sustain them in power. In a system wracked with [class conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_conflict "Class conflict"), government by a 'strongman' was often the best solution.

Athens fell under a tyranny in the second half of the 6th century BC. When this tyranny was ended, the Athenians founded [the world's first democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy "Athenian democracy") as a radical solution to prevent the aristocracy regaining power. A [citizens' assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_assembly "Popular assembly") (the *[Ecclesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_(ancient_Athens) "Ecclesia (ancient Athens)")*), for the discussion of city policy, had existed since the reforms of [Draco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draco_(lawgiver) "Draco (lawgiver)") in 621 BC; all citizens were permitted to attend after the reforms of [Solon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon "Solon") (early 6th century), but the poorest citizens could not address the assembly or run for office. With the establishment of the democracy, the assembly became the *de jure* mechanism of government; all citizens had equal privileges in the assembly. However, non-citizens, such as [metics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metic "Metic") (foreigners living in Athens) or [slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_ancient_Greece "Slavery in ancient Greece"), had no political rights at all.

After the rise of democracy in Athens, other city-states founded democracies. However, many retained more traditional forms of government. As so often in other matters, Sparta was a notable exception to the rest of Greece, ruled through the whole period by not one, but two hereditary monarchs. This was a form of [diarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarchy "Diarchy"). The [Kings of Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_of_Sparta "Kings of Sparta") belonged to the Agiads and the Eurypontids, descendants respectively of [Eurysthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurysthenes "Eurysthenes") and [Procles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procles "Procles"). Both dynasties' founders were believed to be twin sons of [Aristodemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristodemus "Aristodemus"), a [Heraclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclid "Heraclid") ruler. However, the powers of these kings were held in check by both a council of elders (the *[Gerousia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerousia "Gerousia")*) and magistrates specifically appointed to watch over the kings (the *[Ephors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephor "Ephor")*).

### Social structure

Only free, land-owning, native-born men could be citizens entitled to the full protection of the law in a city-state. In most city-states, unlike the situation in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome"), social prominence did not allow special rights. Sometimes families controlled public religious functions, but this ordinarily did not give any extra power in the government. In Athens, the population was divided into four social classes based on wealth. People could change classes if they made more money. In Sparta, all male citizens were called *[homoioi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoioi "Homoioi")*, meaning "peers". However, Spartan kings, who served as the city-state's dual military and religious leaders, came from two families.

[Women in Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_Greece#Women_in_ancient_Greece "Women in Greece") appear to have primarily performed domestic tasks, managed households, and borne and reared children.

#### Slavery

Slaves had no power or status. Slaves had the right to have a family and own property, subject to their master's goodwill and permission, but they had no political rights. By 600 BC, [chattel slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chattel_slavery "Chattel slavery") had spread in Greece. By the 5th century BC, slaves made up one-third of the total population in some city-states. Between 40 and 80% of the population of [Classical Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens "Classical Athens") were slaves. Slaves outside of Sparta almost never revolted because they were made up of too many nationalities and were too scattered to organize. However, unlike later [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture "Western culture"), the ancient Greeks did not think in terms of [race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_(human_categorization) "Race (human categorization)").

Most families owned slaves as household servants and laborers, and even poor families might have owned a few slaves. Owners were not allowed to beat or kill their slaves. Owners often promised to free slaves in the future to encourage slaves to work hard. Unlike in Rome, [freedmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedmen "Freedmen") did not become citizens. Instead, they were mixed into the population of *[metics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metic "Metic")*, which included people from foreign countries or other city-states who were officially allowed to live in the state.

City-states legally owned slaves. These public slaves had a larger measure of independence than slaves owned by families, living on their own and performing specialized tasks. In Athens, public slaves were trained to look out for [counterfeit coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfeit_coinage "Counterfeit coinage"), while temple slaves acted as servants of the temple's [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lists_of_Greek_mythological_figures "Lists of Greek mythological figures") and [Scythian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythian "Scythian") slaves were employed in Athens as a police force corralling citizens to political functions.

Sparta had a special type of slaves called *[helots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helots "Helots")*. Helots were [Messenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messenians "Messenians") enslaved en masse during the [Messenian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Messenian_War "First Messenian War") by the state and assigned to families where they were forced to stay. Helots raised food and did household chores so that women could concentrate on raising strong children while men could devote their time to training as [hoplites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoplite "Hoplite"). Their masters treated them harshly, and helots [revolted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_rebellion "Slave rebellion") against their masters several times. In 370/369 BC, as a result of [Epaminondas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epaminondas "Epaminondas")' liberation of Messenia from Spartan rule, the helot system there came to an end and the helots won their freedom. However, it persisted in Laconia until the 2nd century BC.

### Education

For most of Greek history, education was private, except in Sparta. During the Hellenistic period, some city-states established [public schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_school_(government_funded) "Public school (government funded)"). Only wealthy families could afford a teacher. Boys learned how to read, write and quote literature. They also learned to sing and play one musical instrument and were trained as athletes for military service. They studied not for a job but to become an effective citizen. Girls also learned to read, write and do simple arithmetic so they could manage the household. They almost never received education after childhood.

Boys went to school at the age of seven, or went to the barracks, if they lived in Sparta. The three types of teachings were: grammatistes for arithmetic, kitharistes for music and dancing, and Paedotribae for sports.

Boys from wealthy families attending the private school lessons were taken care of by a *paidagogos*, a household slave selected for this task who accompanied the boy during the day. Classes were held in teachers' private houses and included reading, writing, mathematics, singing, and playing the lyre and flute. When the boy became 12 years old the schooling started to include sports such as wrestling, running, and throwing discus and javelin. In Athens, some older youths attended academy for the finer disciplines such as culture, sciences, music, and the arts. The schooling ended at age 18, followed by military training in the army usually for one or two years.

Some of Athens' greatest such schools included the [Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum "Lyceum") (the so-called [Peripatetic school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripatetic_school "Peripatetic school") founded by [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") of [Stageira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stageira "Stageira")) and the [Platonic Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy "Platonic Academy") (founded by [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") of Athens). The education system of the wealthy ancient Greeks is also called [Paideia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paideia "Paideia").

### Economy

At its economic height in the 5th and 4th centuries BC, the free citizenry of [Classical Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece "Classical Greece") represented perhaps the most prosperous society in the ancient world, some economic historians considering Greece one of the most advanced pre-industrial economies. In terms of wheat, wages reached an estimated 7–12 kg (15–26 lb) daily for an unskilled worker in urban Athens, 2–3 times the 3.75 kg (8.3 lb) of an unskilled rural labourer in Roman Egypt, though Greek farm incomes too were on average lower than those available to urban workers.

While slave conditions varied widely, the institution served to sustain the incomes of the free citizenry: an estimate of economic development drawn from the latter (or derived from urban incomes alone) is therefore likely to overstate the true overall level despite widespread evidence for high living standards.

### Warfare

At least in the Archaic period, the fragmentary nature of ancient Greece, with many competing city-states, increased the frequency of conflict but conversely limited the scale of warfare. Unable to maintain professional armies, the city-states relied on their own citizens to fight. This inevitably reduced the potential duration of campaigns, as citizens would need to return to their own professions (especially in the case of, for example, farmers). Campaigns would therefore often be restricted to summer. When battles occurred, they were usually set piece and intended to be decisive. Casualties were slight compared to later battles, rarely amounting to more than five percent of the losing side, but the slain often included the most prominent citizens and generals who led from the front.

The scale and scope of warfare in ancient Greece changed dramatically as a result of the [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars "Greco-Persian Wars"). To fight the enormous armies of the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") was effectively beyond the capabilities of a single city-state. The eventual triumph of the Greeks was achieved by alliances of city-states (the exact composition changing over time), allowing the pooling of resources and division of labor. Although alliances between city-states occurred before this time, nothing on this scale had been seen before. The rise of Athens and Sparta as pre-eminent powers during this conflict led directly to the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War "Peloponnesian War"), which saw further development of the nature of warfare, strategy and tactics. Fought between leagues of cities dominated by Athens and Sparta, the increased manpower and financial resources increased the scale and allowed the diversification of warfare. Set-piece battles during the Peloponnesian war proved indecisive and instead there was increased reliance on attritionary strategies, naval battles and blockades and sieges. These changes greatly increased the number of casualties and the disruption of Greek society.

Athens owned one of the largest war fleets in ancient Greece. It had over 200 [triremes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme "Trireme") each powered by 170 oarsmen who were seated in 3 rows on each side of the ship. The city could afford such a large fleet—it had over 34,000 oarsmen—because it owned a lot of silver mines that were worked by slaves.

According to [Josiah Ober](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Ober "Josiah Ober"), Greek city-states faced approximately a one-in-three chance of destruction during the archaic and classical period.

## Culture

### Philosophy

Ancient Greek philosophy focused on the role of [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason") and [inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inquiry "Inquiry"). In many ways, it had an important influence on modern philosophy, as well as modern science. Clear unbroken lines of influence lead from ancient Greek and [Hellenistic philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophers "Hellenistic philosophers"), to medieval [Muslim philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Islamic_philosophy "Early Islamic philosophy") and [Islamic scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_science "Islamic science"), to the European [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") and [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment"), to the secular sciences of the modern day.

Neither reason nor inquiry began with the ancient Greeks. Defining the difference between the Greek quest for knowledge and the quests of the elder civilizations, such as the [ancient Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia "Babylonia"), has long been a topic of study by theorists of civilization.

The first known philosophers of Greece were the [pre-Socratics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratic "Pre-Socratic"), who attempted to provide naturalistic, non-mythical descriptions of the world. They were followed by [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates"), one of the first philosophers based in Athens during [its golden age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth-century_Athens "Fifth-century Athens") whose ideas, despite being known by second-hand accounts instead of writings of his own, laid the basis of Western philosophy. Socrates' disciple [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato"), who wrote *[The Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republic_(Plato) "The Republic (Plato)")* and established a radical difference between ideas and the concrete world, and Plato's disciple [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"), who wrote extensively about nature and ethics, are also immensely influential in Western philosophy to this day. The later [Hellenistic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophy "Hellenistic philosophy"), also originating in Greece, is defined by names such as [Antisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisthenes "Antisthenes") ([cynicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynicism_(philosophy) "Cynicism (philosophy)")), [Zeno of Citium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_of_Citium "Zeno of Citium") ([stoicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism "Stoicism")) and [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus "Plotinus") ([Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism "Neoplatonism")).

### Literature and theatre

The earliest Greek literature was poetry and was composed for performance rather than private consumption. The earliest Greek poet known is [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer"), although he was certainly part of an existing tradition of oral poetry. Homer's poetry, though it was developed around the same time that the Greeks developed writing, would have been composed orally; the first poet to certainly compose their work in writing was [Archilochus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archilochus "Archilochus"), a [lyric poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_lyric "Greek lyric") from the mid-7th century BC. [Tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_tragedy "Greek tragedy") developed around the end of the archaic period, taking elements from across the pre-existing genres of late archaic poetry. Towards the beginning of the classical period, comedy began to develop—the earliest date associated with the genre is 486 BC, when a competition for comedy became an official event at the [City Dionysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Dionysia "City Dionysia") in Athens, though the first preserved ancient comedy is [Aristophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes "Aristophanes")' *[Acharnians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acharnians "Acharnians")*, produced in 425.

Like poetry, Greek prose had its origins in the archaic period, and the earliest writers of Greek philosophy, history, and medical literature all date to the 6th century BC. Prose first emerged as the writing style adopted by the [presocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presocratic "Presocratic") philosophers [Anaximander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaximander "Anaximander") and [Anaximenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaximenes_of_Miletus "Anaximenes of Miletus")—though [Thales of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus "Thales of Miletus"), considered the first Greek philosopher, apparently wrote nothing. Prose as a genre reached maturity in the classical era, and the major Greek prose genres—philosophy, history, rhetoric, and dialogue—developed in this period.

The Hellenistic period saw the literary centre of the Greek world move from Athens, where it had been in the classical period, to Alexandria. At the same time, other Hellenistic kings such as the [Antigonids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigonids "Antigonids") and the [Attalids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attalids "Attalids") were patrons of scholarship and literature, turning [Pella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pella "Pella") and [Pergamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pergamon "Pergamon") respectively into cultural centres. It was thanks to this cultural patronage by Hellenistic kings, and especially the Museum at Alexandria, that so much ancient Greek literature has survived. The [Library of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria "Library of Alexandria"), part of the Museum, had the previously unenvisaged aim of collecting together copies of all known authors in Greek. Almost all of the surviving non-technical Hellenistic literature is poetry, and Hellenistic poetry tended to be highly intellectual, blending different genres and traditions, and avoiding linear narratives. The Hellenistic period also saw a shift in the ways literature was consumed—while in the archaic and classical periods literature had typically been experienced in public performance, in the Hellenistic period it was more commonly read privately. At the same time, Hellenistic poets began to write for private, rather than public, consumption.

With Octavian's victory at Actium in 31 BC, Rome began to become a major centre of Greek literature, as important Greek authors such as [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo "Strabo") and [Dionysius of Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus "Dionysius of Halicarnassus") came to Rome. The period of greatest innovation in Greek literature under Rome was the "long second century" from approximately 80 AD to around 230 AD. This innovation was especially marked in prose, with the development of the novel and a revival of prominence for display oratory both dating to this period.

### Music and dance

In Ancient Greek society, music was ever-present and considered a fundamental component of civilisation. It was an important part of public religious worship, private ceremonies such as weddings and funerals, and household entertainment. Men sang and played music at the [symposium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_(ancient_Greece) "Symposium (ancient Greece)"); both men and women sang at work; and children's games involved song and dance.

Ancient Greek music was primarily vocal, sung either by a solo singer or a chorus, and usually accompanied by an instrument; purely instrumental music was less common. The Greeks used stringed instruments, including lyres, harps, and lutes; and wind instruments, of which the most important was the *aulos*, a [reed instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_instrument "Reed instrument"). Percussion instruments played a relatively unimportant role supporting stringed and wind instruments, and were used in certain religious cults.

### Science and technology

Ancient Greek mathematics contributed many important developments to mathematics, including the basic rules of [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), the idea of [formal mathematical proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_proof "Formal proof"), and discoveries in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis"), [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics"), and approached close to establishing [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus "Integral calculus"). The discoveries of several Greek mathematicians, including [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras "Pythagoras"), [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid"), and [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes"), are still used in mathematical teaching today.

The Greeks developed astronomy, which they treated as a branch of mathematics, to a highly sophisticated level. The first geometrical, three-dimensional models to explain the apparent motion of the planets were developed in the 4th century BC by [Eudoxus of Cnidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudoxus_of_Cnidus "Eudoxus of Cnidus") and [Callippus of Cyzicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callippus_of_Cyzicus "Callippus of Cyzicus"). Their younger contemporary [Heraclides Ponticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclides_Ponticus "Heraclides Ponticus") proposed that the Earth rotates around its axis. In the 3rd century BC, [Aristarchus of Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchus_of_Samos "Aristarchus of Samos") was the first to suggest a [heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric "Heliocentric") system. Archimedes in his treatise [The Sand Reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sand_Reckoner#Estimation_of_the_size_of_the_universe "The Sand Reckoner") revives Aristarchus' hypothesis that *"the fixed stars and the Sun remain unmoved, while the Earth revolves about the Sun on the circumference of a circle"*. Otherwise, only fragmentary descriptions of Aristarchus' idea survive. [Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes "Eratosthenes"), using the angles of shadows created at widely separated regions, estimated the [circumference of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumference_of_the_Earth "Circumference of the Earth") with great accuracy. In the 2nd century BC, [Hipparchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus "Hipparchus") of Nicea made a number of contributions, including the first measurement of [precession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession "Precession") and the compilation of the first star catalog in which he proposed the modern system of [apparent magnitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_magnitude "Apparent magnitude").

The [Antikythera mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_mechanism "Antikythera mechanism"), a device for calculating the movements of planets, dates from about 80 BC and was the first ancestor of the astronomical computer. It was discovered in an ancient shipwreck off the Greek island of [Antikythera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera "Antikythera"), between [Kythera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kythera "Kythera") and [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete "Crete"). The device became famous for its use of a [differential gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_gear "Differential gear"), previously believed to have been invented in the 16th century, and the miniaturization and complexity of its parts, comparable to a clock made in the 18th century. The original mechanism is displayed in the Bronze collection of the [National Archaeological Museum of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archaeological_Museum_of_Athens "National Archaeological Museum of Athens"), accompanied by a replica.

The ancient Greeks also made important discoveries in the medical field. [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates "Hippocrates") was a physician of the Classical period, and is considered one of the most outstanding figures in the [history of medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_medicine "History of medicine"). He is referred to as the "[father of medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_persons_considered_father_or_mother_of_a_field#Natural_and_social_sciences "List of persons considered father or mother of a field")" in recognition of his lasting contributions to the field as the founder of the Hippocratic school of medicine. This intellectual school revolutionized [medicine in ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_in_ancient_Greece "Medicine in ancient Greece"), establishing it as a discipline distinct from other fields that it had traditionally been associated with (notably [theurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theurgy "Theurgy") and philosophy), thus making medicine a profession.

### Art and architecture

The art of ancient Greece has exercised an enormous influence on the culture of many countries from ancient times to the present day, particularly in the areas of [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture "Sculpture") and architecture. In the West, the art of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") was largely derived from Greek models. In the East, Alexander the Great's conquests initiated several centuries of exchange between Greek, Central Asian and Indian cultures, resulting in [Greco-Buddhist art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art "Greco-Buddhist art"), with ramifications as far as Japan. Following the Renaissance in Europe, the [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanist "Humanist") aesthetic and the high technical standards of Greek art inspired generations of European artists. Well into the 19th century, the classical tradition derived from Greece dominated the art of the Western world.

### Religion

Religion was a central part of ancient Greek life. Though the Greeks of different cities and [tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_tribes "Ancient Greek tribes") worshipped similar gods, religious practices were not uniform and the gods were thought of differently in different places. The Greeks were [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheistic "Polytheistic"), worshipping many gods, but as early as the 6th century BC a pantheon of [twelve Olympians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Olympians "Twelve Olympians") began to develop. Greek religion was influenced by the practices of the Greeks' near eastern neighbours at least as early as the archaic period, and by the Hellenistic period this influence was seen in both directions.

The most important religious act in ancient Greece was [animal sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_sacrifice "Animal sacrifice"), most commonly of sheep and goats. Sacrifice was accompanied by public prayer, and prayer and hymns were themselves a major part of ancient Greek religious life.

## Legacy

The civilization of ancient Greece has been immensely influential on language, politics, educational systems, philosophy, science, and the arts. It became the *[Leitkultur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitkultur "Leitkultur")* of the Roman Empire to the point of marginalizing native [Italic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_Italy "Iron Age Italy") traditions. As [Horace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace "Horace") put it,

> *Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit et artis / intulit agresti Latio* (*[Epistulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistles_(Horace) "Epistles (Horace)")* 2.1.156f.)
>
> Captive Greece took captive her uncivilised conqueror and instilled her arts in rustic [Latium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latium "Latium").

Via the Roman Empire, Greek culture came to be foundational to [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture "Western culture") in general.
The [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") inherited Classical Greek-Hellenistic culture directly, without Latin intermediation, and the preservation of Classical Greek learning in medieval Byzantine tradition further exerted a strong influence on the [Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavs "Slavs") and later on the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age") and the Western European [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance"). A modern revival of Classical Greek learning took place in the [Neoclassicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassicism "Neoclassicism") movement in 18th- and 19th-century Europe and the Americas.
