# Water

**Water** is an [inorganic compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_compound "Inorganic compound") with the [chemical formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_formula "Chemical formula") H2O. It is a transparent, tasteless, [odorless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odorless "Odorless"), and [nearly colorless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_of_water "Color of water") [chemical substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance "Chemical substance"). It is the main constituent of [Earth's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") [streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream "Stream"), [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake "Lake"), and [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean"). Water is also the [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid "Fluid") of all known living [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism"), in which it acts as a [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent"). Water, being a [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_polarity "Chemical polarity") molecule, undergoes strong intermolecular [hydrogen bonding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bonding "Hydrogen bonding") which is a large contributor to its physical and chemical properties. It is vital for all known forms of life, despite not providing [food energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_energy "Food energy") or being an organic [micronutrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronutrient "Micronutrient"). Due to its presence in all organisms, its chemical stability, its worldwide abundance, and its strong polarity relative to its small molecular size, water is often referred to as the "universal solvent".

Because Earth's surface temperature and pressure is relatively close to water's [triple point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_point "Triple point"), water exists on Earth as a solid, a liquid, and a gas. It forms [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation") in the form of rain and [aerosols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosol "Aerosol") in the form of [fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog "Fog"). Clouds consist of suspended droplets of water and [ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice "Ice"), its solid state. When finely divided, [crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal "Crystal") ice may precipitate in the form of snow. The [gaseous state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas "Gas") of water is [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam "Steam") or [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor "Water vapor").

Water covers about 71% of the Earth's surface, with seas and [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean") making up most of the water volume (about 96.5%). Small portions of water occur as [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater "Groundwater") (1.7%), in the [glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciers "Glaciers") and the [ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_caps "Ice caps") of [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica "Antarctica") and [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland "Greenland") (1.7%), and in the air as [vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor "Vapor"), clouds (consisting of ice and liquid water suspended in air), and precipitation (0.001%). Water moves continuously through the [water cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle "Water cycle") of [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation "Evaporation"), [transpiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpiration "Transpiration") ([evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration "Evapotranspiration")), [condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation "Condensation"), [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation"), and [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff "Surface runoff"), usually reaching the sea.

Water plays an important role in the [world economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_economy "World economy"). Approximately 70% of the [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater "Freshwater") used by humans [goes to agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation"). Fishing in [salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saline_water "Saline water") and [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water "Fresh water") bodies has been, and continues to be, a major source of food for many parts of the world, providing 6.5% of global protein. Much of the long-distance trade of commodities (such as oil, natural gas, and manufactured products) is [transported by boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_transport "Maritime transport") through seas, rivers, lakes, and canals. Large quantities of water, ice, and steam are used for cooling and heating in industry and homes. Water is an excellent solvent for a wide variety of substances, both mineral and organic; as such, it is widely used in industrial processes and in cooking and washing. Water, ice, and snow are also central to many sports and other forms of entertainment, such as swimming, pleasure boating, [boat racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boat_racing "Boat racing"), [surfing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfing "Surfing"), [sport fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_fishing "Recreational fishing"), [diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_(sport) "Diving (sport)"), [ice skating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_skating "Ice skating"), [snowboarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowboarding "Snowboarding"), and [skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skiing "Skiing").

## Etymology

The word *water* comes from [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") ***wæter***, from [Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic_language "Proto-Germanic language") \**watar* (source also of [Old Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Saxon "Old Saxon") *watar*, [Old Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Frisian "Old Frisian") *wetir*, [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language "Dutch language") *water*, [Old High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_High_German "Old High German") *wazzar*, German *Wasser*, *vatn*, [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language "Gothic language") 𐍅𐌰𐍄𐍉 (*wato*)), from [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language "Proto-Indo-European language") \**wod-or*, suffixed form of root \**wed-* ('water'; 'wet'). Also [cognate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate "Cognate"), through the Indo-European root, with Greek ύδωρ (*ýdor*; from Ancient Greek ὕδωρ (*hýdōr*), whence English 'hydro-'), Russian вода́ (*vodá*), Irish *uisce*, and [Albanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_language "Albanian language") *ujë*.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## History

### On Earth

One factor in estimating when water appeared on Earth is that water continually escapes into space. H2O molecules in the atmosphere are broken up by [photolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photodissociation "Photodissociation"), and the resulting free [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") atoms can sometimes [escape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_escape "Atmospheric escape") Earth's gravitational pull. When the Earth was younger and less [massive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass "Mass"), water would have been lost to space more easily. Lighter elements like [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") and [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium") are expected to leak from the atmosphere continually, but [isotopic ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_abundance "Natural abundance") of heavier [noble gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas "Noble gas") in the modern atmosphere suggest that even the heavier elements in the early atmosphere were subject to significant losses. In particular, [xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon "Xenon") is useful for calculations of water loss over time. Not only is it a noble gas (and therefore is not removed from the atmosphere through chemical reactions with other elements), but comparisons between the abundances of its nine stable isotopes in the modern atmosphere reveal that the Earth lost an amount of water approximately equal to the modern ocean volume early in its history. This is likely to have occurred between the [Hadean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadean "Hadean") and [Archean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archean "Archean") eons in cataclysmic events such as the moon forming impact.

Any water on Earth during the latter part of its accretion would have been disrupted by the [Moon-forming impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_impact_hypothesis "Giant impact hypothesis") (~4.5 billion years ago), which likely vaporized much of Earth's crust and [upper mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_mantle_(Earth) "Upper mantle (Earth)") and created a rock-vapor atmosphere around the young planet. The rock vapor would have condensed within two thousand years, leaving behind hot volatiles which probably resulted in a majority [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") atmosphere with hydrogen and [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor "Water vapor"). Afterward, liquid water oceans may have existed despite the surface temperature of 230 °C (446 °F) due to the increased atmospheric pressure of the CO2 atmosphere. As the cooling continued, most CO2 was removed from the atmosphere by [subduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction "Subduction") and dissolution in ocean water, but levels oscillated wildly as new surface and [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_(geology) "Mantle (geology)") cycles appeared.

Geological evidence also helps constrain the time frame for liquid water existing on Earth. A sample of [pillow basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillow_basalt "Pillow basalt") (a type of rock formed during an underwater eruption) was recovered from the [Isua Greenstone Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isua_Greenstone_Belt "Isua Greenstone Belt") and provides evidence that water existed on Earth 3.8 billion years ago. In the [Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuvvuagittuq_Greenstone_Belt "Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt"), Quebec, Canada, rocks dated at 3.8 billion years old by one study and 4.28 billion years old by another show evidence of the presence of water at these ages. If oceans existed earlier than this, any geological evidence has yet to be discovered (which may be because such potential evidence has been destroyed by geological processes like [crustal recycling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustal_recycling "Crustal recycling")). More recently, in August 2020, researchers reported that sufficient water to fill the oceans may have always been on the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") since the beginning of the [planet's formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth#Formation "Earth").

Unlike rocks, minerals called [zircons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zircon "Zircon") are highly resistant to weathering and geological processes and so are used to understand conditions on the very early Earth. Mineralogical evidence from zircons has shown that liquid water and an atmosphere must have existed 4.404 ± 0.008 billion years ago, very soon after the formation of Earth. This presents somewhat of a paradox, as the [cool early Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_early_Earth "Cool early Earth") hypothesis suggests temperatures were cold enough to freeze water between about 4.4 billion and 4.0 billion years ago. Other studies of zircons found in Australian Hadean rock point to the existence of [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics") as early as 4 billion years ago. If true, that implies that rather than a hot, [molten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting "Melting") surface and an atmosphere full of carbon dioxide, early Earth's surface was much as it is today (in terms of [thermal insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_insulation "Thermal insulation")). The action of plate tectonics traps vast amounts of CO2, thereby reducing [greenhouse effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect "Greenhouse effect"), leading to a much lower surface temperature and the formation of solid rock and liquid water.

## Properties

Water ([H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen")2[O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen")) is a [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_polarity "Chemical polarity") [inorganic compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_compound "Inorganic compound"). At [room temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_temperature "Room temperature") it is a [tasteless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taste "Taste") and [odorless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odor "Odor") liquid, nearly [colorless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transparency_and_translucency "Transparency and translucency") with a [hint of blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_of_water "Color of water"). The simplest [hydrogen chalcogenide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chalcogenide "Hydrogen chalcogenide"), it is by far the most studied chemical compound and is sometimes described as the "universal solvent" for its ability to dissolve more substances than any other liquid, though it is poor at dissolving nonpolar substances. This allows it to be the "[solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent") of life": indeed, water as found in nature almost always includes various dissolved substances, and special steps are required to obtain chemically [pure water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_water "Pure water"). Water is the only common substance to exist as a solid, liquid, and gas in normal terrestrial conditions.

### States

Along with *oxidane*, *water* is one of the two official names for the chemical compound H
2O; it is also the liquid phase of H
2O. The other two common [states of matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_matter "States of matter") of water are the solid phase, which is ice, and the gaseous phase, [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor "Water vapor") or [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam "Steam"). The addition or removal of heat can cause [phase transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition"): [freezing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freezing "Freezing") (water to ice), [melting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting "Melting") (ice to water), [vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaporization "Vaporization") (water to vapor), [condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation "Condensation") (vapor to water), [sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_(phase_transition) "Sublimation (phase transition)") (ice to vapor) and [deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_(phase_transition) "Deposition (phase transition)") (vapor to ice).

#### Density

Water is one of the few common substances that expands and becomes less [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density "Density") as it cools within certain temperature ranges; it is the only known naturally occurring substance which does so while liquid. In addition, it is unusual because it becomes significantly less dense as it freezes, though it is not unique in that respect.

At 1 atm pressure, it reaches its maximum density of 999.972 kg/m3 (62.4262 lb/cu ft) at 3.98 °C (39.16 °F).

Below that temperature, but above the freezing point of 0 °C (32 °F), water expands (becoming less dense) until it reaches the freezing point, at which its density in the liquid phase is 999.8 kg/m3 (62.4155 lb/cu ft). As water cools to 3.98 °C (39.16 °F), water volume decreases.

As it freezes and becomes ice, water expands by about 9%, reaching a density of 917 kg/m3 (57.25 lb/cu ft). This expansion can exert enormous pressure, bursting pipes and cracking rocks. As a solid, it displays the usual behavior of contracting and becoming more dense as it cools. These unusual thermal properties have important consequences for life on earth.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In a lake or ocean, water at 4 °C (39 °F) sinks to the bottom, and ice forms on the surface, floating on the liquid water. This ice insulates the water below, preventing it from freezing solid. Without this protection, most aquatic organisms residing in lakes would perish during the winter. In addition, this anomalous behavior is an important part of the [thermohaline circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermohaline_circulation "Thermohaline circulation") which distributes heat around the planet's oceans.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### Magnetism

Water is a [diamagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamagnetism "Diamagnetism") material. Though interaction is weak, with superconducting magnets it can attain a notable interaction.

#### Phase transitions

At a pressure of one [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_atmosphere_(unit) "Standard atmosphere (unit)") (atm), ice melts or water freezes (solidifies) at 0 °C (32 °F) and water boils or vapor condenses at 100 °C (212 °F). However, even below the boiling point, water can change to vapor at its surface by [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation "Evaporation") (vaporization throughout the liquid is known as [boiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling "Boiling")). Sublimation and deposition also occur on surfaces. For example, [frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost "Frost") is deposited on cold surfaces while [snowflakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowflake "Snowflake") form by deposition on an aerosol particle or ice nucleus. In the process of [freeze-drying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeze-drying "Freeze-drying"), a food is frozen and then stored at low pressure so the ice on its surface sublimates.

In most substances, the volume increases when melting occurs, so the melting temperature increases with pressure. However, because ice is less dense than water, the melting temperature decreases. The Clausius-Clapeyron relation also applies to the boiling point, but with the liquid/gas transition the vapor phase has a much lower density than the liquid phase, so the boiling point increases with pressure. Water can remain in a liquid state at high temperatures in the deep ocean or underground. Conversely, a [pressure cooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_cooker "Pressure cooker") can be used to decrease cooking times by raising the boiling temperature. In a [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum "Vacuum"), water will boil at room temperature.

#### Triple and critical points

On a pressure/temperature [phase diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_diagram "Phase diagram") (see figure), there are curves separating solid from vapor, vapor from liquid, and liquid from solid. These meet at a single point called the [triple point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_point "Triple point"), where all three phases can coexist. The triple point is at a temperature of 273.16 K (0.01 °C; 32.02 °F) and a pressure of 611.657 pascals (0.00604 atm; 0.0887 psi); it is the lowest pressure at which liquid water can exist. [Until 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_revision_of_the_SI "2019 revision of the SI"), the triple point was used to define the [Kelvin temperature scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin "Kelvin").

The water/vapor phase curve terminates at 647.096 K (373.946 °C; 705.103 °F) and 22.064 megapascals (3,200.1 psi; 217.75 atm). This is known as the [critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_(thermodynamics) "Critical point (thermodynamics)"). At higher temperatures and pressures the liquid and vapor phases form a continuous phase called a [supercritical fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_fluid "Supercritical fluid"). It can be gradually compressed or expanded between gas-like and liquid-like densities; its properties (which are quite different from those of ambient water) are sensitive to density. For example, for suitable pressures and temperatures it can [mix freely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscibility "Miscibility") with [nonpolar compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonpolar_molecule "Nonpolar molecule"), including most [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound"). This makes it useful in a variety of applications including high-temperature [electrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry "Electrochemistry") and as an ecologically benign solvent or [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis "Catalysis") in chemical reactions involving organic compounds. In Earth's mantle, it acts as a solvent during mineral formation, dissolution and deposition.

#### Phases of ice and water

The normal form of ice on the surface of Earth is [ice Ih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_Ih "Ice Ih"), a phase that forms crystals with [hexagonal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_crystal_family "Hexagonal crystal family"). Another with [cubic crystalline symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_crystal_system "Cubic crystal system"), [ice Ic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_Ic "Ice Ic"), can occur in the upper atmosphere. As the pressure increases, ice forms other [crystal structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure "Crystal structure"). As of 2024, twenty have been experimentally confirmed and several more are predicted theoretically. The eighteenth form of ice, [ice XVIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_XVIII "Ice XVIII"), a face-centred-cubic, superionic ice phase, was discovered when a droplet of water was subject to a shock wave that raised the water's pressure to millions of atmospheres and its temperature to thousands of degrees, resulting in a structure of rigid oxygen atoms in which hydrogen atoms flowed freely. When sandwiched between layers of [graphene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphene "Graphene"), ice forms a square lattice.

The details of the chemical nature of liquid water are not well understood; some theories suggest that its unusual behavior is due to the existence of two liquid states.

### Taste and odor

Pure water is usually described as tasteless and odorless, although humans have specific sensors that can feel the presence of water in their mouths, and frogs are known to be able to smell it. However, water from ordinary sources (including [mineral water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_water "Mineral water")) usually has many dissolved substances that may give it varying tastes and odors. Humans and other animals have developed senses that enable them to evaluate the [potability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potability "Potability") of water to avoid water that is too salty or putrid.

### Color and appearance

Pure water is [visibly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_perception "Visual perception") blue due to [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_absorption_by_water "Electromagnetic absorption by water") of light in the region c. 600–800 nm. The color can be easily observed in a glass of tap-water placed against a pure white background, in daylight. The principal absorption bands responsible for the color are [overtones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overtone_band "Overtone band") of the O–H stretching [vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_vibration "Molecular vibration"). The apparent intensity of the color increases with the depth of the water column, following [Beer's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer%27s_law "Beer's law"). This also applies, for example, with a swimming pool when the light source is sunlight reflected from the pool's white tiles.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In nature, the color may also be modified from blue to green due to the presence of suspended solids or algae.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In industry, [near-infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-infrared_spectroscopy "Near-infrared spectroscopy") is used with aqueous solutions as the greater intensity of the lower overtones of water means that glass [cuvettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuvette "Cuvette") with short path-length may be employed. To observe the fundamental stretching absorption spectrum of water or of an aqueous solution in the region around 3,500 cm−1 (2.85 μm) a path length of about 25 μm is needed. Also, the cuvette must be both transparent around 3500 cm−1 and insoluble in water; [calcium fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_fluoride "Calcium fluoride") is one material that is in common use for the cuvette windows with aqueous solutions.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The [Raman-active](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raman_spectroscopy "Raman spectroscopy") fundamental vibrations may be observed with, for example, a 1 cm sample cell.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Aquatic plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_plant "Aquatic plant"), [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae "Algae"), and other [photosynthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis "Photosynthesis") organisms can live in water up to hundreds of meters deep, because [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight "Sunlight") can reach them. Practically no sunlight reaches the parts of the oceans below 1,000 metres (3,300 ft) of depth.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The [refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index "Refractive index") of liquid water (1.333 at 20 °C (68 °F)) is much higher than that of air (1.0), similar to those of [alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane "Alkane") and [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol"), but lower than those of [glycerol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycerol "Glycerol") (1.473), [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene "Benzene") (1.501), [carbon disulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_disulfide "Carbon disulfide") (1.627), and common types of glass (1.4 to 1.6). The refraction index of ice (1.31) is lower than that of liquid water.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Molecular polarity

In a water molecule, the hydrogen atoms form a 104.5° angle with the oxygen atom. The hydrogen atoms are close to two corners of a tetrahedron centered on the oxygen. At the other two corners are *[lone pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_pairs "Lone pairs")* of valence electrons that do not participate in the bonding. In a perfect tetrahedron, the atoms would form a 109.5° angle, but the repulsion between the lone pairs is greater than the repulsion between the hydrogen atoms. The O–H [bond length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_length "Bond length") is about 0.096 nm.

Other substances have a tetrahedral molecular structure, for example [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane") (CH
4) and [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide "Hydrogen sulfide") (H
2S). However, oxygen is more [electronegative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity "Electronegativity") than most other elements, so the oxygen atom has a partial negative charge while the hydrogen atoms are partially positively charged. Along with the bent structure, this gives the molecule an [electrical dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_dipole_moment "Electrical dipole moment") and it is classified as a [polar molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_molecule "Polar molecule").

Water is a good polar [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent"), dissolving many [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_(chemistry) "Salt (chemistry)") and [hydrophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophilic "Hydrophilic") organic molecules such as sugars and simple alcohols such as [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol"). Water also dissolves many gases, such as oxygen and [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") – the latter giving the fizz of [carbonated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonation "Carbonation") beverages, [sparkling wines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparkling_wine "Sparkling wine") and [beers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer "Beer"). In addition, many substances in living organisms, such as [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"), [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") and [polysaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysaccharide "Polysaccharide"), are dissolved in water. The interactions between water and the subunits of these biomacromolecules shape [protein folding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_folding "Protein folding"), [DNA base pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_pairing "Base pairing"), and other phenomena crucial to life ([hydrophobic effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophobic_effect "Hydrophobic effect")).[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Many organic substances (such as [fats and oils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipids "Lipids") and [alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkanes "Alkanes")) are [hydrophobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophobic "Hydrophobic"); that is, insoluble in water. Many inorganic substances are insoluble too, including most metal [oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide "Oxide"), [sulfides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfide "Sulfide"), and [silicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate "Silicate").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Hydrogen bonding

Because of its polarity, a molecule of water in the liquid or solid state can form up to four [hydrogen bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bonds "Hydrogen bonds") with neighboring molecules. Hydrogen bonds are about ten times as strong as the [Van der Waals force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waals_force "Van der Waals force") that attracts molecules to each other in most liquids. This is the reason why the melting and boiling points of water are much higher than those of [other analogous compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chalcogenide "Hydrogen chalcogenide") like hydrogen sulfide. They also explain its exceptionally high [specific heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat_capacity "Specific heat capacity") (about 4.2 [J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule "Joule")/(g·K)), [heat of fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_fusion "Heat of fusion") (about 333 J/g), [heat of vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_vaporization "Heat of vaporization") (2257 J/g), and [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity "Thermal conductivity") (between 0.561 and 0.679 W/(m·K)). These properties make water more effective at moderating Earth's [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate"), by storing heat and transporting it between the oceans and the atmosphere. The hydrogen bonds of water are around 23 kJ/mol (compared to a covalent O–H bond at 492 kJ/mol). Of this, it is estimated that 90% is attributable to electrostatics, while the remaining 10% is partially covalent.

These bonds are the cause of water's high [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension "Surface tension") and capillary forces. [Capillary action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary_action "Capillary action") refers to the tendency of water to move up a narrow tube against the force of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity"). This property is relied upon by all [vascular plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_plant "Vascular plant"), such as trees.

, in combination with [solvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvation "Solvation") of the resulting [hydronium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydronium "Hydronium") and [hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxide "Hydroxide") ions.

### Electrical conductivity and electrolysis

Pure water has a low [electrical conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity "Electrical conductivity"), which increases with the [dissolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_(chemistry) "Dissolution (chemistry)") of a small amount of ionic material such as [common salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride "Sodium chloride").

Liquid water can be split into the [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element") hydrogen and oxygen by passing an electric current through it—a process called [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis_of_water "Electrolysis of water"). The decomposition requires more energy input than the [heat released by the inverse process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_enthalpy_of_formation "Standard enthalpy of formation") (285.8 kJ/[mol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_(unit) "Mole (unit)"), or 15.9 MJ/kg).

### Mechanical properties

Liquid water can be assumed to be incompressible for most purposes: its compressibility ranges from 4.4 to 5.1×10−10 Pa−1 in ordinary conditions. Even in oceans at 4 km depth, where the pressure is 400 atm, water suffers only a 1.8% decrease in volume.

The [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity "Viscosity") of water is about 10−3 Pa·[s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second "Second") or 0.01 [poise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poise_(unit) "Poise (unit)") at 20 °C (68 °F), and the [speed of sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound "Speed of sound") in liquid water ranges between 1,400 and 1,540 metres per second (4,600 and 5,100 ft/s) depending on temperature. Sound travels long distances in water with little [attenuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuation "Attenuation"), especially at low frequencies (roughly 0.03 [dB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel "Decibel")/km for 1 k[Hz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz "Hertz")), a property that is exploited by [cetaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetaceans "Cetaceans") and humans for communication and environment sensing ([sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar "Sonar")).

### Reactivity

Metallic elements which are more [electropositive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity "Electronegativity") than hydrogen, particularly the [alkali metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metals "Alkali metals") and, to a lesser extent, [alkaline earth metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_earth_metal "Alkaline earth metal"), displace hydrogen from water, forming [hydroxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxide "Hydroxide") and releasing hydrogen.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

At high temperatures, [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_(fuel) "Coke (fuel)"), a form of carbon, reacts with steam to form [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide "Carbon monoxide") and hydrogen.

## On Earth

Hydrology is the study of the movement, distribution, and quality of water throughout the Earth. The study of the distribution of water is [hydrography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrography "Hydrography"). The study of the distribution and movement of [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater "Groundwater") is [hydrogeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogeology "Hydrogeology"), of glaciers is [glaciology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciology "Glaciology"), of inland waters is [limnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limnology "Limnology") and distribution of oceans is [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography "Oceanography"). Ecological processes with hydrology are in the focus of [ecohydrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecohydrology "Ecohydrology").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The collective mass of water found on, under, and over the surface of a planet is called the [hydrosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosphere "Hydrosphere"). Earth's approximate water volume (the total water supply of the world) is 1.386 billion cubic kilometres (333 million cubic miles).

Liquid water is found in [bodies of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water "Body of water"), such as an ocean, sea, lake, river, stream, [canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal "Canal"), pond, or [puddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puddle "Puddle"). The majority of water on Earth is [seawater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater "Seawater"). Water is also present in the atmosphere in solid, liquid, and vapor states. It also exists as groundwater in [aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer "Aquifer").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water is important in many geological processes. Groundwater is present in most [rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_(geology) "Rock (geology)"), and the pressure of this groundwater affects patterns of [faulting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_(geology) "Fault (geology)"). Water in the [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_(geology) "Mantle (geology)") is responsible for the melt that produces [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano "Volcano") at [subduction zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction_zone "Subduction zone"). On the surface of the Earth, water is important in both chemical and physical [weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weathering "Weathering") processes. Water, and to a lesser but still significant extent, ice, are also responsible for a large amount of [sediment transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment_transport "Sediment transport") that occurs on the surface of the earth. [Deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_(geology) "Deposition (geology)") of transported sediment forms many types of [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock "Sedimentary rock"), which make up the [geologic record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_record "Geologic record") of [Earth history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Earth "History of the Earth").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Water cycle

The water cycle (known scientifically as the hydrologic cycle) is the continuous exchange of water within the [hydrosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosphere "Hydrosphere"), between the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_atmosphere "Earth atmosphere"), [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil "Soil") water, [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water "Surface water"), groundwater, and plants.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water moves perpetually through each of these regions in the *water cycle* consisting of the following transfer processes:

* [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation "Evaporation") from oceans and other water bodies into the air and [transpiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpiration "Transpiration") from land plants and animals into the air.
* [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_(meteorology) "Precipitation (meteorology)"), from water vapor condensing from the air and falling to the earth or ocean.
* [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_(water) "Runoff (water)") from the land usually reaching the sea.

Most water vapors found mostly in the ocean returns to it, but winds carry water vapor over land at the same rate as runoff into the sea, about 47 [Tt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tonne_unit "Metric tonne unit") per year while evaporation and transpiration happening in land masses also contribute another 72 Tt per year. Precipitation, at a rate of 119 Tt per year over land, has several forms: most commonly rain, snow, and [hail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hail "Hail"), with some contribution from [fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog "Fog") and [dew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dew "Dew"). Dew is small drops of water that are condensed when a high density of water vapor meets a cool surface. Dew usually forms in the morning when the temperature is the lowest, just before sunrise and when the temperature of the earth's surface starts to increase. Condensed water in the air may also [refract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refract "Refract") [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight "Sunlight") to produce [rainbows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow "Rainbow").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water runoff often collects over [watersheds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin "Drainage basin") flowing into rivers. Through [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion "Erosion"), runoff shapes the environment creating river [valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley "Valley") and [deltas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_delta "River delta") which provide rich soil and level ground for the establishment of population centers. A flood occurs when an area of land, usually low-lying, is covered with water which occurs when a river overflows its banks or a storm surge happens. On the other hand, drought is an extended period of months or years when a region notes a deficiency in its water supply. This occurs when a region receives consistently below average precipitation either due to its topography or due to its location in terms of [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude "Latitude").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Water resources

Water resources are [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resource "Natural resource") of water that are potentially useful for humans, for example as a source of drinking [water supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply "Water supply") or [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation") water. Water occurs as both "stocks" and "flows". Water can be stored as lakes, water vapor, groundwater or aquifers, and ice and snow. Of the total volume of global freshwater, an estimated 69 percent is stored in glaciers and permanent snow cover; 30 percent is in groundwater; and the remaining 1 percent in lakes, rivers, the atmosphere, and biota. The length of time water remains in storage is highly variable: some aquifers consist of water stored over thousands of years, but lake volumes may fluctuate on a seasonal basis, decreasing during dry periods and increasing during wet ones. A substantial fraction of the water supply for some regions consists of water extracted from water stored in stocks, and when withdrawals exceed recharge, stocks decrease. By some estimates, as much as 30 percent of total water used for irrigation comes from unsustainable withdrawals of groundwater, causing [groundwater depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overdrafting "Overdrafting").

### Seawater and tides

Seawater contains about 3.5% [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride "Sodium chloride") on average, plus smaller amounts of other substances. The physical properties of seawater differ from [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water "Fresh water") in some important respects. It freezes at a lower temperature (about −1.9 °C (28.6 °F)) and its density increases with decreasing temperature to the freezing point, instead of reaching maximum density at a temperature above freezing. The salinity of water in major seas varies from about 0.7% in the [Baltic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_Sea "Baltic Sea") to 4.0% in the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea"). (The [Dead Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea "Dead Sea"), known for its ultra-high salinity levels of between 30 and 40%, is really a [salt lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_lake "Salt lake").)[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide "Tide") are the cyclic rising and falling of local sea levels caused by the [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force "Tidal force") of the Moon and the Sun acting on the oceans. Tides cause changes in the depth of the marine and [estuarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary "Estuary") water bodies and produce oscillating currents known as tidal streams. The changing tide produced at a given location is the result of the changing positions of the Moon and Sun relative to the Earth coupled with the [effects of Earth rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_effect "Coriolis effect") and the local [bathymetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathymetry "Bathymetry"). The strip of seashore that is submerged at high tide and exposed at low tide, the [intertidal zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertidal_zone "Intertidal zone"), is an important ecological product of ocean tides.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Effects on life

From a [biological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology") standpoint, water has many distinct properties that are critical for the proliferation of life. It carries out this role by allowing [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound") to react in ways that ultimately allow [replication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-replication "Self-replication"). All known forms of life depend on water. Water is vital both as a [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent") in which many of the body's solutes dissolve and as an essential part of many [metabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism "Metabolism") processes within the body. Metabolism is the sum total of [anabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anabolism "Anabolism") and [catabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catabolism "Catabolism"). In anabolism, water is removed from molecules (through energy requiring enzymatic chemical reactions) to grow larger molecules (e.g., starches, triglycerides, and proteins for storage of fuels and information). This process is known as dehydration synthesis, or [dehydration reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehydration_reaction "Dehydration reaction"). In catabolism, water is used to break bonds to generate smaller molecules (e.g., glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids to be used for fuels for energy use or other purposes). This process is known as [hydrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolysis "Hydrolysis"). Without water, these particular metabolic processes could not exist.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water is fundamental to both photosynthesis and respiration. Photosynthetic cells use the sun's energy to split off water's hydrogen from oxygen. In the presence of sunlight, hydrogen is combined with CO
2 (absorbed from air or water) to form glucose and release oxygen. All living cells use such fuels and oxidize the hydrogen and carbon to capture the sun's energy and reform water and CO
2 in the process (cellular respiration).[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water is also central to acid-base neutrality and enzyme function. An acid, a hydrogen ion (H+
, that is, a proton) donor, can be neutralized by a base, a proton acceptor such as a hydroxide ion (OH−
) to form water. Water is considered to be neutral, with a [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH "PH") (the negative log of the hydrogen ion concentration) of 7 in an ideal state. [Acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acids "Acids") have pH values less than 7 while [bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_(chemistry) "Base (chemistry)") have values greater than 7.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Aquatic life forms

Earth's surface waters are filled with life. The earliest life forms appeared in water; nearly all fish live exclusively in water, and there are many types of marine mammals, such as dolphins and whales. Some kinds of animals, such as [amphibians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibian "Amphibian"), spend portions of their lives in water and portions on land. Plants such as [kelp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelp "Kelp") and [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae "Algae") grow in the water and are the basis for some underwater ecosystems. [Plankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plankton "Plankton") is generally the foundation of the ocean [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain "Food chain").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Aquatic vertebrates must obtain oxygen to survive, and they do so in various ways. Fish have [gills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gills "Gills") instead of [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lungs "Lungs"), although some species of fish, such as the [lungfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lungfish "Lungfish"), have both. [Marine mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal "Marine mammal"), such as dolphins, whales, [otters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otter "Otter"), and [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinniped "Pinniped") need to surface periodically to breathe air. Some amphibians are able to absorb oxygen through their skin. Invertebrates exhibit a wide range of modifications to survive in poorly oxygenated waters including breathing tubes (see [insect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siphon_(insect) "Siphon (insect)") and [mollusc siphons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siphon_(mollusc) "Siphon (mollusc)")) and gills (*[Carcinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinus "Carcinus")*). However, as invertebrate life evolved in an aquatic habitat, most have little or no specialization for respiration in water.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Effects on human civilization

Civilization has historically flourished around rivers and major waterways; [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), one of the so-called [cradles of civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradles_of_civilization "Cradles of civilization"), was situated between the major rivers [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris") and [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates"); the ancient society of the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians "Egyptians") depended entirely upon the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile"). The early [Indus Valley civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilization "Indus Valley civilization") (c. 3300 BCE – c. 1300 BCE) developed along the Indus River and tributaries that flowed out of the [Himalayas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayas "Himalayas"). [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome "Rome") was also founded on the banks of the Italian river [Tiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiber "Tiber"). Large [metropolises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis "Metropolis") like [Rotterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotterdam "Rotterdam"), London, Montreal, Paris, New York City, [Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buenos_Aires "Buenos Aires"), Shanghai, Tokyo, Chicago, and Hong Kong owe their success in part to their easy accessibility via water and the resultant expansion of trade. Islands with safe water ports, like Singapore, have flourished for the same reason. In places such as North Africa and the Middle East, where water is more scarce, access to clean drinking water was and is a major factor in human development.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Health and pollution

Water fit for human consumption is called [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water "Drinking water") or potable water. Water that is not potable may be made potable by filtration or [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation "Distillation"), or by a range of [other methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_treatment "Water treatment"). More than 660 million people do not have access to safe drinking water.

Water that is not fit for drinking but is not harmful to humans when used for swimming or bathing is called by various names other than potable or drinking water, and is sometimes called [safe water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_water "Safe water"), or "safe for bathing". Chlorine is a skin and mucous membrane irritant that is used to make water safe for bathing or drinking. Its use is highly technical and is usually monitored by government regulations (typically 1 part per million (ppm) for drinking water, and 1–2 ppm of chlorine not yet reacted with impurities for bathing water). Water for bathing may be maintained in satisfactory microbiological condition using chemical disinfectants such as [chlorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine "Chlorine") or [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone "Ozone") or by the use of [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet") light.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Water reclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaimed_water "Reclaimed water") is the process of converting wastewater (most commonly [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage "Sewage"), also called municipal wastewater) into water that can be [reused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuse "Reuse") for other purposes. There are 2.3 billion people who reside in nations with water scarcities, which means that each individual receives less than 1,700 cubic metres (60,000 cu ft) of water annually. 380 billion cubic metres (13×10~~^~~12 cu ft) of municipal wastewater are produced globally each year.

Freshwater is a renewable resource, recirculated by the natural [hydrologic cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrologic_cycle "Hydrologic cycle"), but pressures over access to it result from the naturally uneven distribution in space and time, growing economic demands by agriculture and industry, and rising populations. Currently, nearly a billion people around the world lack access to safe, affordable water. In 2000, the United Nations established the [Millennium Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Development_Goals "Millennium Development Goals") for water to halve by 2015 the proportion of people worldwide without access to safe water and [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation "Sanitation"). Progress toward that goal was uneven, and in 2015 the UN committed to the [Sustainable Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals "Sustainable Development Goals") of achieving universal access to safe and affordable water and sanitation by 2030. Poor [water quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_quality "Water quality") and bad sanitation are deadly; some five million deaths a year are caused by water-related diseases. The [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization") estimates that [safe water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_water "Safe water") could prevent 1.4 million child deaths from [diarrhea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarrhea "Diarrhea") each year.

In developing countries, 90% of all [municipal wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage "Sewage") still goes untreated into local rivers and streams. Some 50 countries, with roughly a third of the world's population, also suffer from medium or high [water scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_scarcity "Water scarcity") and 17 of these extract more water annually than is recharged through their natural water cycles. The strain not only affects surface freshwater bodies like rivers and lakes, but it also degrades groundwater resources.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Human uses

#### Agriculture

The most substantial human use of water is for agriculture, including irrigated agriculture, which accounts for as much as 80 to 90 percent of total human water consumption. In the United States, 42% of freshwater withdrawn for use is for irrigation, but the vast majority of water "consumed" (used and not returned to the environment) goes to agriculture.

Access to fresh water is often taken for granted, especially in developed countries that have built sophisticated water systems for collecting, purifying, and delivering water, and removing wastewater. But growing economic, demographic, and climatic pressures are increasing concerns about water issues, leading to increasing competition for fixed water resources, giving rise to the concept of [peak water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_water "Peak water"). As populations and economies continue to grow, consumption of water-thirsty meat expands, and new demands rise for biofuels or new water-intensive industries, new water challenges are likely.

An assessment of water management in agriculture was conducted in 2007 by the [International Water Management Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Water_Management_Institute "International Water Management Institute") in Sri Lanka to see if the world had sufficient water to provide food for its growing population. It assessed the current availability of water for agriculture on a global scale and mapped out locations suffering from water scarcity. It found that a fifth of the world's people, more than 1.2 billion, live in areas of [physical water scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_water_scarcity "Physical water scarcity"), where there is not enough water to meet all demands. A further 1.6 billion people live in areas experiencing [economic water scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_water_scarcity "Economic water scarcity"), where the lack of investment in water or insufficient human capacity make it impossible for authorities to satisfy the demand for water. The report found that it would be possible to produce the food required in the future, but that continuation of today's food production and environmental trends would lead to crises in many parts of the world. To avoid a global water crisis, farmers will have to strive to increase productivity to meet growing demands for food, while industries and cities find ways to use water more efficiently.

Water scarcity is also caused by production of water intensive products. For example, cotton: 1 kg of cotton—equivalent of a pair of jeans—requires 10.9 cubic metres (380 cu ft) water to produce. While cotton accounts for 2.4% of world water use, the water is consumed in regions that are already at a risk of water shortage. Significant environmental damage has been caused: for example, the diversion of water by the former [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") from the [Amu Darya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amu_Darya "Amu Darya") and [Syr Darya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syr_Darya "Syr Darya") rivers to produce cotton was largely responsible for the disappearance of the [Aral Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aral_Sea "Aral Sea").

#### As a scientific standard

On 7 April 1795, the gram was defined in France to be equal to "the absolute weight of a volume of pure water equal to a cube of one-hundredth of a meter, and at the temperature of melting ice". For practical purposes though, a metallic reference standard was required, one thousand times more massive, the kilogram. Work was therefore commissioned to determine precisely the mass of one liter of water. In spite of the fact that the decreed definition of the gram specified water at 0 °C (32 °F)—a highly reproducible *temperature*—the scientists chose to redefine the standard and to perform their measurements at the temperature of highest water *density*, which was measured at the time as 4 °C (39 °F).

The [Kelvin temperature scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin_temperature_scale "Kelvin temperature scale") of the [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units "International System of Units") system was based on the [triple point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_point "Triple point") of water, defined as exactly 273.16 K (0.01 °C; 32.02 °F), but as of May 2019 is based on the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant "Boltzmann constant") instead. The scale is an [absolute temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_temperature "Absolute temperature") scale with the same increment as the Celsius temperature scale, which was originally defined according to the [boiling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point "Boiling point") (set to 100 °C (212 °F)) and [melting point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point "Melting point") (set to 0 °C (32 °F)) of water.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Natural water consists mainly of the isotopes hydrogen-1 and oxygen-16, but there is also a small quantity of heavier isotopes [oxygen-18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen-18 "Oxygen-18"), [oxygen-17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen-17 "Oxygen-17"), and [hydrogen-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen-2 "Hydrogen-2") ([deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium "Deuterium")). The percentage of the heavier isotopes is very small, but it still affects the properties of water. Water from rivers and lakes tends to contain less heavy isotopes than seawater. Therefore, standard water is defined in the [Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Standard_Mean_Ocean_Water "Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water") specification.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### For drinking

The [human body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_body "Human body") contains, on average, 50–60% water, depending on age, gender and body size, although individuals may have anywhere between 45% and 75%. The [U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academies_of_Sciences,_Engineering,_and_Medicine "National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine") recommends a daily intake of 3.7 liters (0.98 U.S. gallons) of water for adult men and 2.7 L (0.71 U.S. gal) for women. The precise amount depends on the level of activity, temperature, humidity, and other factors. Most of this is ingested through foods or beverages other than drinking straight water. Medical literature favors a lower consumption, typically 1 liter of water for an average male, excluding extra requirements due to fluid loss from exercise or warm weather.

Healthy kidneys can excrete 0.8 to 1 liter of water per hour, but stress such as exercise can reduce this amount. People can drink far more water than necessary while exercising, putting them at risk of [water intoxication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_intoxication "Water intoxication") (hyperhydration), which can be fatal. The popular claim that "a person should consume eight glasses of water per day" seems to have no real basis in science. Studies have shown that extra water intake, especially up to 500 millilitres (18 imp fl oz; 17 US fl oz) at mealtime, was associated with weight loss. Adequate fluid intake is helpful in preventing constipation.

An original recommendation for water intake in 1945 by the Food and Nutrition Board of the [U.S. National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._National_Research_Council "U.S. National Research Council") read: "An ordinary standard for diverse persons is 1 milliliter for each calorie of food. Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods." The latest dietary reference intake report by the U.S. National Research Council in general recommended, based on the median total water intake from US survey data (including food sources): 3.7 litres (0.81 imp gal; 0.98 US gal) for men and 2.7 litres (0.59 imp gal; 0.71 US gal) of water total for women, noting that water contained in food provided approximately 19% of total water intake in the survey.

Specifically, pregnant and breastfeeding women need additional fluids to stay hydrated. The US [Institute of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Medicine "Institute of Medicine") recommends that, on average, men consume 3 litres (0.66 imp gal; 0.79 US gal) and women 2.2 litres (0.48 imp gal; 0.58 US gal); pregnant women should increase intake to 2.4 litres (0.53 imp gal; 0.63 US gal) and breastfeeding women should get 3 liters (12 cups), since an especially large amount of fluid is lost during nursing. Also noted is that normally, about 20% of water intake comes from food, while the rest comes from drinking water and beverages ([caffeinated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caffeine "Caffeine") included). Water is excreted from the body in multiple forms; through [urine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urine "Urine") and [feces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feces "Feces"), through [sweating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweat "Sweat"), and by exhalation of water vapor in the breath. With physical exertion and heat exposure, water loss will increase and daily fluid needs may increase as well.

Humans require water with few impurities. Common impurities include metals like copper and lead; chemical compounds such as [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide "Pesticide"), [PFAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PFAS "PFAS"), or [bleach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleach "Bleach"); and harmful bacteria, such as *[Campylobacter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campylobacter "Campylobacter")*, [*E. coli* O157](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli_O157:H7 "Escherichia coli O157:H7"), and *[Vibrio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrio "Vibrio")*. Some [solutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solutes "Solutes") are acceptable and even desirable for taste enhancement and to provide needed [electrolytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte "Electrolyte").

The single largest (by volume) freshwater resource suitable for drinking is [Lake Baikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Baikal "Lake Baikal") in Siberia.

#### Washing

[Washing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washing "Washing") is a method of [cleaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleaning "Cleaning"), usually with water and [soap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap "Soap") or [detergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detergent "Detergent"). Regularly washing and then rinsing both body and clothing is an essential part of good [hygiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hygiene "Hygiene") and health.

Often people use soaps and detergents to assist in the [emulsification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emulsification "Emulsification") of oils and dirt particles so they can be washed away. The soap can be applied directly, or with the aid of a [washcloth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towel "Towel") or assisted with [sponges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_(tool) "Sponge (tool)") or similar [cleaning tools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cleaning_tools "List of cleaning tools").

In social contexts, washing refers to the act of [bathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathing "Bathing"), or washing different parts of the body, such as [hands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_washing "Hand washing"), [hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hair_washing "Hair washing"), or [faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_washing "Face washing"). Excessive washing may damage the hair, causing dandruff, or cause rough skin/skin lesions. Some washing of the body is done ritually in religions like Christianity and Judaism, as an [act of purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_purification "Ritual purification").

Washing can also refer to washing objects. For example, [washing of clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laundry "Laundry") or other cloth items, like bedsheets, or [washing dishes or cookware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dishwashing "Dishwashing"). Keeping objects clean, especially if they interact with food or the skin, can help with sanitation. Other kinds of washing focus on maintaining cleanliness and durability of objects that get dirty, such [washing one's car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_wash "Car wash") like car wash parts by lathering the exterior with car soap, or washing tools used in a dirty process.

#### Transportation

[Maritime transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_transport "Maritime transport") (or ocean transport) or more generally waterborne transport, is the transport of people (passengers) or goods (cargo) via [waterways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterways "Waterways"). [Freight transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight_transport "Freight transport") by [watercraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercraft "Watercraft") has been widely used throughout recorded history, as it provides a higher-capacity mode of transportation for passengers and cargo than land transport, the latter typically being more costly per unit [payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload "Payload") due to it being affected by [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain "Terrain") conditions and road/rail infrastructures. The advent of aviation during the 20th century has diminished the importance of sea travel for passengers, though it is still popular for [short trips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferry "Ferry") and [pleasure cruises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_ship "Cruise ship"). Transport by watercraft is much cheaper than transport by aircraft or land vehicles (both road and rail), but is significantly slower for longer journeys and heavily dependent on adequate port facilities. Maritime transport accounts for roughly 80% of international trade, according to [UNCTAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Conference_on_Trade_and_Development "United Nations Conference on Trade and Development") in 2020.

Maritime transport can be realized over any distance as long as there are connecting [bodies of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodies_of_water "Bodies of water") that are navigable to boats, ships or [barges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barge "Barge") such as oceans, lakes, rivers and canals. Shipping may be for commerce, [recreation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreation "Recreation"), or military purposes, and is an important aspect of logistics in human societies since early shipbuilding and [river engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_engineering "River engineering") were developed, leading to [canal ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal_age "Canal age") in various civilizations. While extensive inland shipping is less critical today, the major waterways of the world including many canals are still very important and are integral parts of worldwide economies. Particularly, especially any material can be moved by water; however, water transport becomes impractical when material delivery is time-critical such as various types of perishable [produce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Produce "Produce"). Still, water transport is highly cost effective with regular schedulable cargoes, such as trans-oceanic shipping of consumer products – and especially for heavy loads or [bulk cargos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulk_cargo "Bulk cargo"), such as coal, [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_(fuel) "Coke (fuel)"), [ores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore "Ore") or grains. Arguably, the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution") had its first impacts where cheap water transport by canal, navigations, or [shipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight_transport "Freight transport") by all types of watercraft on natural waterways supported cost-effective [bulk transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulk_transport "Bulk transport").

[Containerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Containerization "Containerization") revolutionized maritime transport starting in the 1970s. "General cargo" includes goods packaged in boxes, cases, pallets, and barrels. When a cargo is carried in more than one mode, it is [intermodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_freight_transport "Intermodal freight transport") or [co-modal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-modality "Co-modality").

#### Chemical uses

Water is widely used in chemical reactions as a [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent") or [reactant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactant "Reactant") and less commonly as a [solute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solute "Solute") or catalyst. In inorganic reactions, water is a common solvent, dissolving many ionic compounds, as well as other polar compounds such as [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia") and [compounds closely related to water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chalcogenide "Hydrogen chalcogenide"). In organic reactions, it is not usually used as a reaction solvent, because it does not dissolve the reactants well and is [amphoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphoteric "Amphoteric") (acidic *and* basic) and [nucleophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleophilic "Nucleophilic"). Nevertheless, these properties are sometimes desirable. Also, acceleration of [Diels-Alder reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diels-Alder_reaction "Diels-Alder reaction") by water has been observed. [Supercritical water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_water "Supercritical water") has recently been a topic of research. Oxygen-saturated supercritical water combusts organic pollutants efficiently.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### Heat exchange

Water and steam are a common fluid used for [heat exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exchanger "Heat exchanger"), due to its availability and high [heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_capacity_of_water "Heat capacity of water"), both for cooling and heating. Cool water may even be naturally available from a lake or the sea. It is especially effective to transport heat through [vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaporization "Vaporization") and [condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation "Condensation") of water because of its large [latent heat of vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_heat_of_vaporization "Latent heat of vaporization"). A disadvantage is that metals commonly found in industries such as steel and copper are [oxidized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation "Oxidation") faster by untreated water and steam. In almost all [thermal power stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_power_station "Thermal power station"), water is used as the working fluid (used in a closed-loop between boiler, steam turbine, and condenser), and the coolant (used to exchange the waste heat to a water body or carry it away by [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation "Evaporation") in a [cooling tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooling_tower "Cooling tower")). In the United States, cooling power plants is the largest use of water.

In the [nuclear power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power") industry, water can also be used as a [neutron moderator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator"). In most [nuclear reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor"), water is both a coolant and a moderator. This provides something of a passive safety measure, as removing the water from the reactor also [slows the nuclear reaction down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Void_coefficient "Void coefficient"). However other methods are favored for stopping a reaction and it is preferred to keep the nuclear core covered with water so as to ensure adequate cooling.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### Fire considerations

Water has a high heat of vaporization and is relatively inert, which makes it a good [fire extinguishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_fighting#Use_of_water "Fire fighting") fluid. The evaporation of water carries heat away from the fire. It is dangerous to use water on fires involving oils and organic solvents because many organic materials float on water and the water tends to spread the burning liquid.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Use of water in fire fighting should also take into account the hazards of a [steam explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_explosion "Steam explosion"), which may occur when water is used on very hot fires in confined spaces, and of a hydrogen explosion, when substances which react with water, such as certain metals or hot carbon such as coal, [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal "Charcoal"), or [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_(fuel) "Coke (fuel)") graphite, decompose the water, producing [water gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_gas "Water gas").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The power of such explosions was seen in the [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster "Chernobyl disaster"), although the water involved in this case did not come from fire-fighting but from the reactor's own water cooling system. A steam explosion occurred when the extreme overheating of the core caused water to flash into steam. A hydrogen explosion may have occurred as a result of a reaction between steam and hot [zirconium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium "Zirconium").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Some metallic oxides, most notably those of [alkali metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metals "Alkali metals") and [alkaline earth metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_earth_metals "Alkaline earth metals"), produce so much heat in reaction with water that a fire hazard can develop. The alkaline earth oxide [quicklime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_oxide "Calcium oxide"), also known as calcium oxide, is a mass-produced substance that is often transported in paper bags. If these are soaked through, they may ignite as their contents react with water.

#### Recreation

Humans use water for many recreational purposes, as well as for exercising and for sports. Some of these include swimming, [waterskiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterskiing "Waterskiing"), [boating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boating "Boating"), [surfing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfing "Surfing") and [diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_diving "Underwater diving"). In addition, some sports, like [ice hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_hockey "Ice hockey") and [ice skating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_skating "Ice skating"), are played on ice. Lakesides, beaches and [water parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_park "Water park") are popular places for people to go to relax and enjoy recreation. Many find the sound and appearance of flowing water to be calming, and fountains and other flowing water structures are popular decorations. Some keep fish and other flora and fauna inside [aquariums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquarium "Aquarium") or ponds for show, fun, and companionship. Humans also use water for snow sports, such as [skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skiing "Skiing"), [sledding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sledding "Sledding"), [snowmobiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowmobiling "Snowmobiling") or [snowboarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowboarding "Snowboarding"), which require the water to be at a low temperature either as ice or crystallized into snow.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### Water industry

The [water industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_industry "Water industry") provides drinking water and [wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater "Wastewater") services (including [sewage treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage_treatment "Sewage treatment")) to households and industry. [Water supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply "Water supply") facilities include [water wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_well "Water well"), [cisterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistern "Cistern") for [rainwater harvesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainwater_harvesting "Rainwater harvesting"), [water supply networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_network "Water supply network"), and [water purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_purification "Water purification") facilities, [water tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_tank "Water tank"), [water towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_tower "Water tower"), [water pipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pipe "Water pipe") including old [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_(watercourse) "Aqueduct (watercourse)"). [Atmospheric water generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_water_generator "Atmospheric water generator") are in development.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Drinking water is often collected at [springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_(hydrosphere) "Spring (hydrosphere)"), extracted from artificial [borings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boring_(earth) "Boring (earth)") (wells) in the ground, or pumped from lakes and rivers. Building more wells in adequate places is thus a possible way to produce more water, assuming the aquifers can supply an adequate flow. Other water sources include rainwater collection. Water may require purification for human consumption. This may involve the removal of undissolved substances, dissolved substances and harmful [microbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbe "Microbe"). Popular methods are [filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_(water) "Filter (water)") with sand which only removes undissolved material, while [chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_chlorination "Water chlorination") and [boiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling "Boiling") kill harmful microbes. [Distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation "Distillation") does all three functions. More advanced techniques exist, such as [reverse osmosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_osmosis "Reverse osmosis"). [Desalination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desalination "Desalination") of abundant [seawater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater "Seawater") is a more expensive solution used in coastal [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid "Arid") [climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The distribution of drinking water is done through [municipal water systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_water_system "Municipal water system"), tanker delivery or as [bottled water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottled_water "Bottled water"). Governments in many countries have programs to distribute water to the needy at no charge.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Reducing usage by using drinking (potable) water only for human consumption is another option. In some cities such as Hong Kong, seawater is extensively used for flushing toilets citywide to [conserve freshwater resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conservation "Water conservation").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Polluting water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution "Water pollution") may be the biggest single misuse of water; to the extent that a pollutant limits other uses of the water, it becomes a waste of the resource, regardless of benefits to the polluter. Like other types of pollution, this does not enter standard accounting of market costs, being conceived as [externalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality "Externality") for which the market cannot account. Thus other people pay the price of water pollution, while the private firms' profits are not redistributed to the local population, victims of this pollution. [Pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceuticals "Pharmaceuticals") consumed by humans often end up in the waterways and can have detrimental effects on [aquatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_biology "Marine biology") life if they [bioaccumulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioaccumulation "Bioaccumulation") and if they are not [biodegradable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodegradable "Biodegradable").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Municipal and [industrial wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_wastewater_treatment "Industrial wastewater treatment") are typically treated at [wastewater treatment plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater_treatment_plant "Wastewater treatment plant"). Mitigation of polluted [surface runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff "Surface runoff") is addressed through a variety of [prevention and treatment techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff#Mitigation_and_treatment "Surface runoff").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

* A manual water [pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pump "Pump") in China
* [Water purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_purification "Water purification") facility
* [Reverse osmosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_osmosis "Reverse osmosis") (RO) [desalination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desalination "Desalination") plant in [Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelona "Barcelona"), Spain

#### Industrial applications

Many industrial processes rely on reactions using chemicals dissolved in water, suspension of solids in water [slurries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slurry "Slurry") or using water to dissolve and extract substances, or to wash products or process equipment. Processes such as mining, [chemical pulping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_pulping "Chemical pulping"), [pulp bleaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pulp_bleaching&action=edit&redlink=1 "Pulp bleaching (page does not exist)"), [paper manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_manufacturing "Paper manufacturing"), textile production, dyeing, printing, and cooling of power plants use large amounts of water, requiring a dedicated water source, and often cause significant water pollution.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water is used in [power generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_generation "Power generation"). [Hydroelectricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectricity "Hydroelectricity") is electricity obtained from [hydropower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydropower "Hydropower"). Hydroelectric power comes from water driving a water turbine connected to a generator. Hydroelectricity is a low-cost, non-polluting, renewable energy source. The energy is supplied by the motion of water. Typically a dam is constructed on a river, creating an artificial lake behind it. Water flowing out of the lake is forced through turbines that turn generators.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Pressurized water is used in [water blasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodemolition "Hydrodemolition") and [water jet cutters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_jet_cutter "Water jet cutter"). High pressure water guns are used for precise cutting. It works very well, is relatively safe, and is not harmful to the environment. It is also used in the cooling of machinery to prevent overheating, or prevent saw blades from overheating.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Water is also used in many industrial processes and machines, such as the [steam turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine "Steam turbine") and [heat exchanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exchanger "Heat exchanger"), in addition to its use as a chemical [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent"). Discharge of untreated water from industrial uses is [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution "Water pollution"). Pollution includes discharged solutes (chemical pollution) and discharged coolant water ([thermal pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_pollution "Thermal pollution")). Industry requires pure water for many applications and uses a variety of purification techniques both in water supply and discharge.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The digital sector, especially [Artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") use large amount of water, so AI expansion can "threaten global and national water security".

#### Food processing

[Boiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling "Boiling"), [steaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steaming "Steaming"), and [simmering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simmering "Simmering") are popular cooking methods that often require immersing food in water or its gaseous state, steam. Water is also used for [dishwashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dishwashing "Dishwashing"). Water also plays many critical roles within the field of [food science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_science "Food science").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Solutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solutes "Solutes") such as salts and sugars found in water affect the physical properties of water. The boiling and freezing points of water are affected by solutes, as well as [air pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pressure "Air pressure"), which is in turn affected by altitude. Water boils at lower temperatures with the lower air pressure that occurs at higher elevations. One [mole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_(unit) "Mole (unit)") of sucrose (sugar) per kilogram of water raises the boiling point of water by 0.51 °C (0.918 °F), and one mole of salt per kg raises the boiling point by 1.02 °C (1.836 °F); similarly, increasing the number of dissolved particles lowers water's freezing point.

Solutes in water also affect water activity that affects many chemical reactions and the growth of microbes in food. Water activity can be described as a ratio of the vapor pressure of water in a solution to the vapor pressure of pure water. Solutes in water lower water activity—this is important to know because most bacterial growth ceases at low levels of water activity. Not only does microbial growth affect the safety of food, but also the preservation and shelf life of food.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Water hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_hardness "Water hardness") is also a critical factor in food processing and may be altered or treated by using a chemical ion exchange system. It can dramatically affect the quality of a product, as well as playing a role in sanitation. Water hardness is classified based on concentration of calcium carbonate the water contains. Water is classified as soft if it contains less than 100 mg/L (UK) or less than 60 mg/L (US).

According to a report published by the Water Footprint organization in 2010, a single kilogram of beef requires 15 thousand litres (3.3×10~~^~~3 imp gal; 4.0×10~~^~~3 US gal) of water; however, the authors also make clear that this is a global average and circumstantial factors determine the amount of water used in beef production.

#### Medical use

[Water for injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_for_injection "Water for injection") is on the [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization")'s [list of essential medicines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization%27s_list_of_essential_medicines "World Health Organization's list of essential medicines").

## Distribution in nature

### In the universe

Much of the universe's water is produced as a byproduct of [star formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_formation "Star formation"). The formation of stars is accompanied by a strong outward wind of gas and dust. When this outflow of material eventually impacts the surrounding gas, the shock waves that are created compress and heat the gas. The water observed is quickly produced in this warm dense gas.

On 22 July 2011, a report described the discovery of a gigantic cloud of water vapor containing "140 trillion times more water than all of Earth's oceans combined" around a [quasar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar "Quasar") located 12 billion light years from Earth. According to the researchers, the "discovery shows that water has been prevalent in the universe for nearly its entire existence".

Water has been detected in [interstellar clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_cloud "Interstellar cloud") within the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way "Milky Way"). Water probably exists in abundance in other galaxies, too, because its components, hydrogen, and oxygen, are among the most abundant elements in the universe. Based on models of the [formation and evolution of the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System "Formation and evolution of the Solar System") and that of other star systems, most other [planetary systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_system "Planetary system") are likely to have similar ingredients.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### Exotic forms

Water and other [volatiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_(astrogeology) "Volatile (astrogeology)") probably comprise much of the internal structures of [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus "Uranus") and [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune "Neptune") and the water in the deeper layers may be in the form of [ionic water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_water "Ionic water") in which the molecules break down into a soup of hydrogen and oxygen ions, and deeper still as [superionic water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superionic_water "Superionic water") in which the oxygen crystallizes, but the hydrogen ions float about freely within the oxygen lattice.

### Water and planetary habitability

The existence of liquid water, and to a lesser extent its gaseous and solid forms, on Earth are vital to the existence of [life on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism") as we know it. The Earth is located in the [habitable zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitable_zone "Habitable zone") of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System"); if it were slightly closer to or farther from the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun") (about 5%, or about 8 million kilometers), the conditions which allow the three forms to be present simultaneously would be far less likely to exist. Earth's size also plays a role: its [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity") allows it to hold an [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere "Atmosphere"), including [air moisture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor "Water vapor"). Smaller planets like Mars have extremely thin or no atmospheres. Water vapor and other [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas") in the atmosphere provide a temperature buffer ([greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect "Greenhouse effect")) which helps maintain a relatively steady surface temperature when present in regular quantities.

The state of water on a planet depends on ambient pressure, which is determined by the planet's gravity. If a planet is sufficiently massive, the water on it may be solid even at high temperatures, because of the high pressure caused by gravity, as it was observed on exoplanets [Gliese 436 b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliese_436_b "Gliese 436 b") and [GJ 1214 b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GJ_1214_b "GJ 1214 b").

## Law, politics, and crisis

[Water politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_politics "Water politics") is politics affected by water and [water resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resources "Water resources"). Water, particularly fresh water, is a strategic resource across the world and an important element in many political conflicts. It causes health impacts and damage to biodiversity.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Access to safe drinking water has improved over the last decades in almost every part of the world, but approximately one billion people still lack access to safe water and over 2.5 billion lack access to adequate [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation "Sanitation"). However, some observers have estimated that by 2025 more than half of the [world population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population "World population") will be facing water-based vulnerability. A report, issued in November 2009, suggests that by 2030, in some developing regions of the world, water demand will exceed supply by 50%.

1.6 billion people have gained access to a safe water source since 1990. The proportion of people in [developing countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_country "Developing country") with [access to safe water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WASH "WASH") is calculated to have improved from 30% in 1970 to 71% in 1990, 79% in 2000, and 84% in 2004.

A 2006 United Nations report stated that "there is enough water for everyone", but that access to it is hampered by mismanagement and corruption. In addition, global initiatives to improve the efficiency of aid delivery, such as the [Paris Declaration on Aid Effectiveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Declaration_on_Aid_Effectiveness "Paris Declaration on Aid Effectiveness"), have not been taken up by water sector donors as effectively as they have in education and health, potentially leaving multiple donors working on overlapping projects and recipient governments without empowerment to act.

The authors of the 2007 [Comprehensive Assessment of Water Management in Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_Assessment_of_Water_Management_in_Agriculture "Comprehensive Assessment of Water Management in Agriculture") cited poor governance as one reason for some forms of water scarcity. Water governance is the set of formal and informal processes through which decisions related to water management are made. Good water governance is primarily about knowing what processes work best in a particular physical and socioeconomic context. Mistakes have sometimes been made by trying to apply 'blueprints' that work in the developed world to developing world locations and contexts. The Mekong river is one example; a review by the [International Water Management Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Water_Management_Institute "International Water Management Institute") of policies in six countries that rely on the Mekong river for water found that thorough and transparent cost-benefit analyses and environmental impact assessments were rarely undertaken. They also discovered that Cambodia's draft water law was much more complex than it needed to be.

In 2004, the UK charity [WaterAid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WaterAid "WaterAid") reported that a child dies every 15 seconds from easily preventable water-related diseases, which are often tied to a lack of adequate sanitation.

Since 2003, the [UN World Water Development Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_World_Water_Development_Report "UN World Water Development Report"), produced by the [UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO") [World Water Assessment Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Water_Assessment_Programme "World Water Assessment Programme"), has provided decision-makers with tools for developing sustainable [water policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_politics "Water politics"). The 2023 report states that two billion people (26% of the population) do not have access to [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water "Drinking water") and 3.6 billion (46%) lack access to safely managed sanitation. People in urban areas (2.4 billion) will face [water scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_scarcity "Water scarcity") by 2050. Water scarcity has been described as endemic, due to [overconsumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overconsumption_(economics) "Overconsumption (economics)") and pollution. The report states that 10% of the world's population lives in countries with high or critical water stress. Yet over the past 40 years, water consumption has increased by around 1% per year, and is expected to grow at the same rate until 2050. Since 2000, flooding in the tropics has quadrupled, while flooding in northern mid-latitudes has increased by a factor of 2.5. The cost of these floods between 2000 and 2019 was 100,000 deaths and $650 million.

Organizations concerned with water protection include the [International Water Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Water_Association "International Water Association") (IWA), WaterAid, Water 1st, and the American Water Resources Association. The [International Water Management Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Water_Management_Institute "International Water Management Institute") undertakes projects with the aim of using effective water management to reduce poverty. Water related conventions are [United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Convention_to_Combat_Desertification "United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification") (UNCCD), [International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Convention_for_the_Prevention_of_Pollution_from_Ships "International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships"), [United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Convention_on_the_Law_of_the_Sea "United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea") and [Ramsar Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsar_Convention "Ramsar Convention"). [World Day for Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Day_for_Water "World Day for Water") takes place on 22 March and [World Oceans Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Oceans_Day "World Oceans Day") on 8 June.

In 2026, the United Nations declared that humanity had entered the era of "[Water bankruptcy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_bankruptcy "Water bankruptcy")" because more than 50% of large lakes have declined since the 1990s, 35% of wetlands have disappeared since 1970, 75% of the world's population live in water-insecure countries, 4 billion people experience water scarcity for at least one month per year, and droughts cost $307 billion per year. It called for policies that match reality rather than past norms.

## In culture

### Religion

Water is considered a purifier in most religions. Faiths that incorporate ritual washing ([ablution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_purification "Ritual purification")) include Christianity, [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam"), Judaism, the [Rastafari movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rastafari_movement "Rastafari movement"), [Shinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto "Shinto"), [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism"), and [Wicca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicca "Wicca"). Immersion (or [aspersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspersion "Aspersion") or [affusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affusion "Affusion")) of a person in water is a central [Sacrament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament "Sacrament") of Christianity (where it is called [baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism "Baptism")); it is also a part of the practice of other religions, including Islam (*[Ghusl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghusl "Ghusl")*), Judaism (*[mikvah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikvah "Mikvah")*) and [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism "Sikhism") (*[Amrit Sanskar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amrit_Sanskar "Amrit Sanskar")*). In addition, a ritual bath in pure water is performed for the dead in many religions including Islam and Judaism. In Islam, the five daily prayers can be done in most cases after washing certain parts of the body using clean water (*[wudu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wudu "Wudu")*), unless water is unavailable (see *[Tayammum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tayammum "Tayammum")*). In Shinto, water is used in almost all rituals to cleanse a person or an area (e.g., in the ritual of *[misogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misogi "Misogi")*).[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In Christianity, [holy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_water "Holy water") is water that has been sanctified by a priest for the purpose of [baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism "Baptism"), the [blessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blessing_(Roman_Catholic_Church) "Blessing (Roman Catholic Church)") of persons, places, and objects, or as a means of repelling evil.

In [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism"), water (*[āb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aban "Aban")*) is respected as the source of life.

### Philosophy

The Ancient Greek philosopher [Empedocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empedocles "Empedocles") saw [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_(classical_element) "Water (classical element)") as one of the four [classical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_elements "Classical elements") (along with fire, earth, and [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_(classical_element) "Air (classical element)")), and regarded it as an [ylem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ylem "Ylem"), or basic substance of the universe. [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales "Thales"), whom Aristotle portrayed as an astronomer and an engineer, theorized that the earth, which is denser than water, emerged from the water. Thales, a [monist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monist "Monist"), believed further that all things are made from water. [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") believed that the shape of water is an [icosahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedron "Icosahedron") – flowing easily compared to the cube-shaped earth.

The theory of the [four bodily humors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humorism "Humorism") associated water with [phlegm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlegm "Phlegm"), as being cold and moist. The [classical element of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_(classical_element) "Water (classical element)") was also one of the [five elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_elements_(Chinese_philosophy) "Five elements (Chinese philosophy)") in traditional [Chinese philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_philosophy "Chinese philosophy") (along with [earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_(classical_element) "Earth (classical element)"), [fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_(classical_element) "Fire (classical element)"), [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_(classical_element) "Wood (classical element)"), and [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_(classical_element) "Metal (classical element)")).[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Some traditional and popular [Asian philosophical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_philosophy "Asian philosophy") take water as a role-model. [James Legge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Legge "James Legge")'s 1891 translation of the *[Dao De Jing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dao_De_Jing "Dao De Jing")* states, "The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water appears in its benefiting all things, and in its occupying, without striving (to the contrary), the low place which all men dislike. Hence (its way) is near to (that of) the [Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao "Tao")" and "There is nothing in the world more soft and weak than water, and yet for attacking things that are firm and strong there is nothing that can take precedence of it—for there is nothing (so effectual) for which it can be changed." *[Guanzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanzi_(text) "Guanzi (text)")* in the "Shui di" 水地 chapter further elaborates on the symbolism of water, proclaiming that "man is water" and attributing natural qualities of the people of different Chinese regions to the character of local water resources.

### Folklore

"Living water" features in Germanic and Slavic [folktales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore "Folklore") as a means of bringing the dead back to life. ### Art and activism

In the significant [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_literature "Modernist literature") novel *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_(novel) "Ulysses (novel)")* (1922) by Irish writer [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"), the chapter "Ithaca" takes the form of a [catechism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catechism "Catechism") of 309 questions and answers, one of which is known as the "water hymn". According to Richard E. Madtes, the hymn is not merely a "monotonous string of facts", rather, its phrases, like their subject, "ebb and flow, heave and swell, gather and break, until they subside into the calm quiescence of the concluding 'pestilential fens, faded flowerwater, stagnant pools in the waning moon.'" The hymn is considered one of the most remarkable passages in Ithaca, and according to literary critic [Hugh Kenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Kenner "Hugh Kenner"), achieves "the improbable feat of raising to poetry all the clutter of footling information that has accumulated in schoolbooks." The [literary motif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(narrative) "Motif (narrative)") of water represents the novel's theme of "everlasting, everchanging life," and the hymn represents the culmination of the motif in the novel. The following is the hymn quoted in full.

> What in water did Bloom, waterlover, drawer of water, watercarrier returning to the range, admire?
> Its universality: its democratic equality and constancy to its nature in seeking its own level: its vastness in the ocean of Mercator's projection: its unplumbed profundity in the Sundam trench of the Pacific exceeding 8,000 fathoms: the restlessness of its waves and surface particles visiting in turn all points of its seaboard: the independence of its units: the variability of states of sea: its hydrostatic quiescence in calm: its hydrokinetic turgidity in neap and spring tides: its subsidence after devastation: its sterility in the circumpolar icecaps, arctic and antarctic: its climatic and commercial significance: its preponderance of 3 to 1 over the dry land of the globe: its indisputable hegemony extending in square leagues over all the region below the subequatorial tropic of Capricorn: the multisecular stability of its primeval basin: its luteofulvous bed: its capacity to dissolve and hold in solution all soluble substances including millions of tons of the most precious metals: its slow erosions of peninsulas and downwardtending promontories: its alluvial deposits: its weight and volume and density: its imperturbability in lagoons and highland tarns: its gradation of colours in the torrid and temperate and frigid zones: its vehicular ramifications in continental lakecontained streams and confluent oceanflowing rivers with their tributaries and transoceanic currents: gulfstream, north and south equatorial courses: its violence in seaquakes, waterspouts, artesian wells, eruptions, torrents, eddies, freshets, spates, groundswells, watersheds, waterpartings, geysers, cataracts, whirlpools, maelstroms, inundations, deluges, cloudbursts: its vast circumterrestrial ahorizontal curve: its secrecy in springs, and latent humidity, revealed by rhabdomantic or hygrometric instruments and exemplified by the well by the hole in the wall at Ashtown gate, saturation of air, distillation of dew: the simplicity of its composition, two constituent parts of hydrogen with one constituent part of oxygen: its healing virtues: its buoyancy in the waters of the Dead Sea: its persevering penetrativeness in runnels, gullies, inadequate dams, leaks on shipboard: its properties for cleansing, quenching thirst and fire, nourishing vegetation: its infallibility as paradigm and paragon: its metamorphoses as vapour, mist, cloud, rain, sleet, snow, hail: its strength in rigid hydrants: its variety of forms in loughs and bays and gulfs and bights and guts and lagoons and atolls and archipelagos and sounds and fjords and minches and tidal estuaries and arms of sea: its solidity in glaciers, icebergs, icefloes: its docility in working hydraulic millwheels, turbines, dynamos, electric power stations, bleachworks, tanneries, scutchmills: its utility in canals, rivers, if navigable, floating and graving docks: its potentiality derivable from harnessed tides or watercourses falling from level to level: its submarine fauna and flora (anacoustic, photophobe) numerically, if not literally, the inhabitants of the globe: its ubiquity as constituting 90% of the human body: the noxiousness of its effluvia in lacustrine marshes, pestilential fens, faded flowerwater, stagnant pools in the waning moon.

Painter and activist [Fredericka Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredericka_Foster "Fredericka Foster") curated *The Value of Water*, at the [Cathedral of St. John the Divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._John_the_Divine "Cathedral of St. John the Divine") in New York City, which anchored a year-long initiative by the Cathedral on our dependence on water. The largest exhibition to ever appear at the Cathedral, it featured over forty artists, including [Jenny Holzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_Holzer "Jenny Holzer"), [Robert Longo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Longo "Robert Longo"), [Mark Rothko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Rothko "Mark Rothko"), [William Kentridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kentridge "William Kentridge"), [April Gornik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Gornik "April Gornik"), [Kiki Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiki_Smith "Kiki Smith"), [Pat Steir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Steir "Pat Steir"), [Alice Dalton Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Dalton_Brown "Alice Dalton Brown"), [Teresita Fernandez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teresita_Fernandez "Teresita Fernandez") and [Bill Viola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Viola "Bill Viola"). Foster created Think About Water,[*[full citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#What_information_to_include "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] an ecological collective of artists who use water as their subject or medium. Members include Basia Irland,[*[full citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#What_information_to_include "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] [Aviva Rahmani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviva_Rahmani "Aviva Rahmani"), [Betsy Damon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betsy_Damon "Betsy Damon"), [Diane Burko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Burko "Diane Burko"), [Leila Daw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leila_Daw "Leila Daw"), [Stacy Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stacy_Levy "Stacy Levy"), Charlotte Coté, [Meridel Rubenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridel_Rubenstein "Meridel Rubenstein"), and [Anna Macleod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Macleod "Anna Macleod").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

To mark the 10th anniversary of access to water and sanitation being declared a human right by the UN, the charity WaterAid commissioned ten visual artists to show the impact of clean water on people's lives.

### Dihydrogen monoxide parody

'Dihydrogen monoxide' is a technically correct but rarely used [chemical name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_name "Chemical name") of water. This name has been used in a series of [hoaxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoaxes "Hoaxes") and [pranks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pranks "Pranks") that mock [scientific illiteracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_illiteracy "Scientific illiteracy"). This began in 1983, when an [April Fools' Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Fools%27_Day "April Fools' Day") article appeared in a newspaper in [Durand, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durand,_Michigan "Durand, Michigan"). The false story consisted of safety concerns about the substance.

### Music

The word "Water" has been used by many Florida based [rappers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapping "Rapping") as a sort of catchphrase or adlib. Rappers who have done this include [BLP Kosher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLP_Kosher "BLP Kosher") and [Ski Mask the Slump God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ski_Mask_the_Slump_God "Ski Mask the Slump God"). To go even further some rappers have made whole songs dedicated to the water in Florida, such as the 2023 [Danny Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Towers "Danny Towers") song "Florida Water".
