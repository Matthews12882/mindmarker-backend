# History of science

The **history of science** covers the development of [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science "Science") from [ancient times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history "Ancient history") to the [present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present "Present"). It encompasses all three major [branches of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branches_of_science "Branches of science"): [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science"), [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science"), and [formal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_science "Formal science"). [Protoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoscience "Protoscience"), [early sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_ancient_world "Science in the ancient world"), and natural philosophies such as [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy "Alchemy") and [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology") that existed during the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age"), [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age "Iron Age"), [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") and the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), declined after the emergence of modern sciences during the [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution").

The earliest roots of scientific thinking and practice can be traced to [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") during the 3rd and 2nd millennia BCE. These civilizations' contributions to [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), and [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine") influenced later Greek [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_classical_antiquity "Science in classical antiquity"), wherein formal attempts were made to provide explanations of events in the [physical world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") based on natural causes. After the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Fall of the Western Roman Empire"), knowledge of [Greek conceptions of the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_ancient_Greece "Science in ancient Greece") deteriorated in Latin-speaking [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe "Western Europe") during the early centuries (400 to 1000 CE) of [the Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_science_in_the_Middle_Ages "European science in the Middle Ages"), but continued to thrive in the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language")-speaking [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"). Aided by translations of Greek texts, the [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period") worldview was preserved and absorbed into the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic")-speaking [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world") during the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age"). The recovery and assimilation of [Greek works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_literature "Ancient Greek literature") and [Islamic inquiries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Science in the medieval Islamic world") into Western Europe from the 10th to 13th century revived the learning of natural philosophy in the West. Traditions of early science were also developed in [ancient India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_and_technology_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "History of science and technology in the Indian subcontinent") and separately in [ancient China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_China "Ancient China"), the [Chinese model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_and_technology_in_China "History of science and technology in China") having influenced [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Vietnam "Science and technology in Vietnam"), [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_and_technology_in_Korea "History of science and technology in Korea") and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_and_technology_in_Japan "History of science and technology in Japan") before [Western exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Sail "Age of Sail"). Among the [Pre-Columbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian "Pre-Columbian") peoples of [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"), the [Zapotec civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapotec_civilization "Zapotec civilization") established their first known traditions of astronomy and mathematics for [producing calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_calendars "Mesoamerican calendars"), followed by other civilizations such as the [Maya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization").

Natural philosophy was transformed by the Scientific Revolution that transpired during the 16th and 17th centuries in Europe, as [new ideas and discoveries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution#New_ideas "Scientific Revolution") departed from [previous Greek conceptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution#Ancient_and_medieval_background "Scientific Revolution") and traditions. The New Science that emerged was more [mechanistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_philosophy "Mechanical philosophy") in its worldview, more integrated with mathematics, and more reliable and open as its knowledge was based on a newly defined [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method"). More "revolutions" in subsequent centuries soon followed. The [chemical revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_revolution "Chemical revolution") of the 18th century, for instance, introduced new quantitative methods and measurements for [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"). In the [19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th_century_in_science "19th century in science"), new perspectives regarding the [conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy"), [age of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Earth "Age of Earth"), and [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution") came into focus. And in the 20th century, new discoveries in [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") and [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") laid the foundations for new sub disciplines such as [molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology") and [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"). Moreover, industrial and military concerns as well as the increasing complexity of new research endeavors ushered in the era of "[big science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_science "Big science")," particularly after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II").

## Approaches to history of science

The nature of the history of science - including both the definition of science and whether [the English word "science" is a misleading term for pre-modern scholarship as well as non-scholarly knowledge of the natural world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_science#Terminology "Historiography of science") - is a topic of ongoing debate and sometimes [significant friction between scientists, sociologists and historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_science#Science_wars "Historiography of science"). The history of science is often seen as a linear [story of progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_science#Narratives_of_progress "Historiography of science"),
but historians have come to see the story as more complex.
[Alfred Edward Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Edward_Taylor "Alfred Edward Taylor") has characterised lean periods in the advance of scientific discovery as "periodical bankruptcies of science".

The [professionalization of the history of science in the 20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_science#Professionalization "Historiography of science") was accompanied by a prodigious and proliferating specialization, with the field seeming to strive to match the protean diversity of [modern science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution") itself. Science is a human activity, and scientific contributions have come from people from a wide range of different backgrounds and cultures. Historians of science increasingly see their field as part of a global history of exchange, conflict and collaboration.

The [relationship between science and religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relationship_between_science_and_religion "Relationship between science and religion") has been variously characterized in terms of "conflict", "harmony", "complexity", and "mutual independence", among others. Events in Europe such as the [Galileo affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_affair "Galileo affair") of the early 17th century led scholars such as [John William Draper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Draper "John William Draper") to postulate (c. 1874) a [conflict thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_thesis "Conflict thesis"), suggesting that religion and science have been in conflict methodologically, factually and politically throughout history. The "conflict thesis" has since lost favor among the majority of contemporary scientists and historians of science. However, some contemporary philosophers and scientists, such as [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins"), still subscribe to this thesis.

Historians have emphasized that trust is necessary for agreement on claims about nature. In this light, the 1660 establishment of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") and its code of experiment – trustworthy because witnessed by its members – has become an [important chapter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_and_the_Air-Pump "Leviathan and the Air-Pump") in the history of science. Many people in modern history (typically [women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_science "Women in science") and persons of color) were excluded from elite scientific communities and [characterized by the science establishment as inferior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism "Scientific racism"). Historians in the 1980s and 1990s described the structural barriers to participation and began to recover the contributions of overlooked individuals. Historians have also investigated the mundane practices of science such as fieldwork and specimen collection, correspondence, drawing, record-keeping, and the use of laboratory and field equipment.

## Prehistory

In [prehistoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory "Prehistory") times, knowledge and technique were passed from generation to generation in an [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition"). For instance, the domestication of [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") for agriculture has been dated to about 9,000 years ago in southern [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico"), before the development of [writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system "Writing system"). Similarly, [archaeological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology "Archaeology") evidence indicates the development of [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") knowledge in preliterate societies.

The oral tradition of preliterate societies had several features, the first of which was its fluidity. New information was constantly absorbed and adjusted to new circumstances or community needs. There were no archives or reports. This fluidity was closely related to the practical need to explain and justify a present state of affairs. Another feature was the tendency to describe the universe as just sky and earth, with a potential [underworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underworld "Underworld"). They were also prone to identify causes with beginnings, thereby providing a historical origin with an explanation. There was also a reliance on a "[medicine man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_man "Medicine man")" or "[wise woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunning_folk "Cunning folk")" for healing, knowledge of divine or demonic causes of diseases, and in more extreme cases, for rituals such as [exorcism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism "Exorcism"), [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination "Divination"), songs, and [incantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incantation "Incantation"). Finally, there was an inclination to unquestioningly accept explanations that might be deemed implausible in more modern times while at the same time not being aware that such credulous behaviors could have posed problems.

The development of writing enabled humans to store and communicate knowledge across generations with much greater accuracy. Its invention was a prerequisite for the development of philosophy and later [science in ancient times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_ancient_world "Science in the ancient world"). Moreover, the extent to which philosophy and science would flourish in ancient times depended on the efficiency of a writing system (e.g., use of alphabets).

## Ancient Near East and North East Africa

The earliest roots of science can be traced to the [Ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East "Ancient Near East") and [North East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_Africa "North East Africa") c. 3000–1200 BCE – in particular to [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mesopotamia "History of Mesopotamia").

### Ancient Egypt

Archaeological evidence has suggested that the Ancient Egyptian counting system had origins in Sub-Saharan Africa. Also, fractal geometry designs which are widespread among Sub-Saharan African cultures are also found in Egyptian architecture and cosmological signs. The Ishango bone, according to scholar [Alexander Marshack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Marshack "Alexander Marshack"), may have influenced the later development of mathematics in Egypt as, like some entries on the Ishango bone, Egyptian arithmetic also made use of multiplication by 2; this however, is disputed. Megalithic structures located in [Nabta Playa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabta_Playa "Nabta Playa"), Upper Egypt featured [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), calendar arrangements in alignment with the heliacal rising of [Sirius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirius "Sirius") and supported calibration the yearly calendar for the annual Nile flood. These practices have been linked with the emergence of [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology") in Old Kingdom Egypt.

#### Number system and geometry

Starting c. 3000 BCE, the ancient Egyptians developed a numbering system that was decimal in character and had oriented their knowledge of geometry to solving practical problems such as those of surveyors and builders. Their development of [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") was itself a necessary development of [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying "Surveying") to preserve the layout and ownership of farmland, which was flooded annually by the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile"). The 3-4-5 [right triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_triangle "Right triangle") and other rules of geometry were used to build rectilinear structures, and the post and lintel architecture of Egypt.

#### Disease and healing

Egypt was also a center of [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_alchemy "History of alchemy") research for much of the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Basin "Mediterranean Basin"). According to the [medical papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_medical_papyri "Egyptian medical papyri") (written c. 2500–1200 BCE), the ancient Egyptians believed that disease was mainly caused by the invasion of bodies by evil forces or spirits. Thus, in addition to [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_medicine "Egyptian medicine"), therapies included prayer, [incantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incantation "Incantation"), and ritual. The [Ebers Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebers_Papyrus "Ebers Papyrus"), written c. 1600 BCE, contains medical recipes for treating diseases related to the eyes, mouth, skin, internal organs, and extremities, as well as abscesses, wounds, burns, ulcers, swollen glands, tumors, headaches, and bad breath. The [Edwin Smith Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Smith_Papyrus "Edwin Smith Papyrus"), written at about the same time, contains a surgical manual for treating wounds, fractures, and dislocations. The Egyptians believed that the effectiveness of their medicines depended on the preparation and administration under appropriate rituals. Medical historians believe that ancient Egyptian pharmacology, for example, was largely ineffective. Both the Ebers and Edwin Smith papyri applied the following components to the treatment of disease: examination, diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis, which display strong parallels to the basic [empirical method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_method "Empirical method") of science and, according to G. E. R. Lloyd, played a significant role in the development of this methodology.

#### Calendar

The ancient Egyptians even developed an official calendar that contained twelve months, thirty days each, and five days at the end of the year. Unlike the Babylonian calendar or the ones used in Greek city-states at the time, the official Egyptian calendar was much simpler as it was fixed and did not take [lunar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase "Lunar phase") and solar cycles into consideration.

### Ancient Nubia

#### Medicine

Nubian [mummies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummies "Mummies") studied in the 1990s revealed that Kush was a pioneer of [early antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_antibiotics "History of antibiotics").

[Tetracycline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetracycline "Tetracycline") was being used by Nubians, based on bone remains between 350 AD and 550 AD. The antibiotic was in wide commercial use only in the mid 20th century. The theory states that earthen jars containing grain used for making beer contained the bacterium [streptomyces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptomyces "Streptomyces"), which produced tetracycline. Although Nubians were not aware of tetracycline, they could have noticed that people fared better by drinking beer than just consuming the grain itself. According to Charlie Bamforth, a professor of biochemistry and brewing science at the University of California, Davis, "They must have consumed it because it was rather tastier than the grain from which it was derived."

### Mathematics

Based on engraved plans of Meroitic King [Amanikhabali](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amanikhabali&action=edit&redlink=1 "Amanikhabali (page does not exist)")'s pyramids, Nubians had a sophisticated understanding of mathematics as they appreciated the harmonic ratio. The engraved plans are indicative of much to be revealed about Nubian mathematics. The [ancient Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia") also established a system of geometry which they used in creating early versions of [sun clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_clock "Sun clock"). During the Meroitic period in Nubian history, the Nubians used a trigonometric methodology similar to the Egyptians.

### Mesopotamia

The ancient Mesopotamians had extensive knowledge about the [chemical properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_property "Chemical property") of clay, sand, metal ore, [bitumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen "Bitumen"), stone, and other natural materials, and applied this knowledge to practical use in manufacturing [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery "Pottery"), [faience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faience "Faience"), glass, soap, metals, [lime plaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_plaster "Lime plaster"), and waterproofing. [Metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy "Metallurgy") required knowledge about the properties of metals. Nonetheless, the Mesopotamians seem to have had little interest in gathering information about the natural world for the mere sake of gathering information and were far more interested in studying the manner in which the gods had ordered the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe"). Biology of non-human organisms was generally only written about in the context of mainstream academic disciplines. [Animal physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_physiology "Animal physiology") was studied extensively for the purpose of [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination "Divination"); the anatomy of the [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver "Liver"), which was seen as an important organ in [haruspicy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haruspicy "Haruspicy"), was studied in particularly intensive detail. [Animal behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_behavior "Animal behavior") was also studied for divinatory purposes. Most information about the training and domestication of animals was probably transmitted orally without being written down, but one text dealing with the training of horses has survived.

#### Mesopotamian medicine

The ancient [Mesopotamians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") had no distinction between "rational science" and [magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_(paranormal) "Magic (paranormal)"). When a person became ill, doctors prescribed magical formulas to be recited as well as medicinal treatments. The earliest medical prescriptions appear in [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_language "Sumerian language") during the [Third Dynasty of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Dynasty_of_Ur "Third Dynasty of Ur") (c. 2112 BCE – c. 2004 BCE). The most extensive [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia "Babylonia") medical text, however, is the *Diagnostic Handbook* written by the *ummânū*, or chief scholar, [Esagil-kin-apli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esagil-kin-apli "Esagil-kin-apli") of [Borsippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsippa "Borsippa"), during the reign of the Babylonian king [Adad-apla-iddina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adad-apla-iddina "Adad-apla-iddina") (1069–1046 BCE). In [East Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Semitic "East Semitic") cultures, the main medicinal authority was a kind of exorcist-healer known as an *[āšipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asipu "Asipu")*. The profession was generally passed down from father to son and was held in extremely high regard. Of less frequent recourse was another kind of healer known as an *asu*, who corresponds more closely to a modern physician and treated physical symptoms using primarily [folk remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_remedies "Folk remedies") composed of various herbs, animal products, and minerals, as well as potions, enemas, and ointments or [poultices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poultices "Poultices"). These physicians, who could be either male or female, also dressed wounds, set limbs, and performed simple surgeries. The ancient Mesopotamians also practiced [prophylaxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophylaxis "Prophylaxis") and took measures to prevent the spread of disease.

#### Astronomy and celestial divination

In [Babylonian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy "Babylonian astronomy"), records of the motions of the [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star "Star"), [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet "Planet"), and the [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon") are left on thousands of [clay tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_tablet "Clay tablet") created by [scribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribe "Scribe"). Even today, astronomical periods identified by Mesopotamian proto-scientists are still widely used in [Western calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar "Gregorian calendar") such as the [solar year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_year "Solar year") and the [lunar month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_month "Lunar month"). Using this data, they developed mathematical methods to compute the changing length of daylight in the course of the year, predict the appearances and disappearances of the Moon and planets, and eclipses of the Sun and Moon. Only a few astronomers' names are known, such as that of [Kidinnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidinnu "Kidinnu"), a [Chaldean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldea "Chaldea") astronomer and mathematician. Kiddinu's value for the solar year is in use for today's calendars. Babylonian astronomy was "the first and highly successful attempt at giving a refined mathematical description of astronomical phenomena." According to the historian A. Aaboe, "all subsequent varieties of scientific astronomy, in the Hellenistic world, in India, in Islam, and in the West—if not indeed all subsequent endeavour in the exact sciences—depend upon Babylonian astronomy in decisive and fundamental ways."

To the [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon") and other [Near Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East "Near East") cultures, messages from the gods or omens were concealed in all natural phenomena that could be deciphered and interpreted by those who are adept. Hence, it was believed that the gods could speak through all terrestrial objects (e.g., animal entrails, dreams, malformed births, or even the color of a dog urinating on a person) and celestial phenomena. Moreover, Babylonian astrology was inseparable from Babylonian astronomy.

#### Mathematics

The Mesopotamian [cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform "Cuneiform") tablet [Plimpton 322](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plimpton_322 "Plimpton 322"), dating to the 18th century BCE, records a number of [Pythagorean triplets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple "Pythagorean triple") (3, 4, 5) and (5, 12, 13) ..., hinting that the ancient Mesopotamians might have been aware of the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem") over a millennium before Pythagoras.

## Ancient and medieval South Asia and East Asia

Mathematical achievements from Mesopotamia had some influence on the development of mathematics in India, and there were confirmed transmissions of mathematical ideas between India and China, which were bidirectional. Nevertheless, the mathematical and scientific achievements in India and particularly in China occurred largely independently from those of Europe and the confirmed early influences that these two civilizations had on the development of science in Europe in the pre-modern era were indirect, with Mesopotamia and later the Islamic World acting as intermediaries. The arrival of modern science, which grew out of the [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution"), in India and China and the greater Asian region in general can be traced to the scientific activities of Jesuit missionaries who were interested in studying the region's [flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora "Flora") and [fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna "Fauna") during the 16th to 17th century.

### India

#### Mathematics

The earliest traces of mathematical knowledge in the Indian subcontinent appear with the [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation "Indus Valley Civilisation") (c. 3300 – c. 1300 BCE). The people of this civilization made bricks whose dimensions were in the proportion 4:2:1, which is favorable for the stability of a brick structure. They also tried to standardize measurement of length to a high degree of accuracy. They designed a ruler—the *Mohenjo-daro ruler*—whose length of approximately 1.32 in (34 mm) was divided into ten equal parts. Bricks manufactured in ancient Mohenjo-daro often had dimensions that were integral multiples of this unit of length.

The [Bakhshali manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhshali_manuscript "Bakhshali manuscript") contains problems involving [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic"), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") and [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), including [mensuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mensuration_(mathematics) "Mensuration (mathematics)"). The topics covered include fractions, square roots, [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_progression "Arithmetic progression") and [geometric progressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_progression "Geometric progression"), solutions of simple equations, [simultaneous linear equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_linear_equations "Simultaneous linear equations"), [quadratic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_equations "Quadratic equations") and [indeterminate equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_equations "Indeterminate equations") of the second degree. In the 3rd century BCE, [Pingala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingala "Pingala") presents the *Pingala-sutras*, the earliest known treatise on [Sanskrit prosody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_prosody "Sanskrit prosody"). He also presents a numerical system by adding one to the sum of [place values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_value "Place value"). Pingala's work also includes material related to the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_numbers "Fibonacci numbers"), called **mātrāmeru**.

Indian astronomer and mathematician [Aryabhata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata "Aryabhata") (476–550), in his *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya "Aryabhatiya")* (499) introduced the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine "Sine") function in [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry") and the number 0. In 628, [Brahmagupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta "Brahmagupta") suggested that [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity") was a force of attraction. He also lucidly explained the use of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_(number) "0 (number)") as both a placeholder and a [decimal digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_digit "Decimal digit"), along with the [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system "Hindu–Arabic numeral system") now used universally throughout the world. [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") translations of the two astronomers' texts were soon available in the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph "Caliph"), introducing what would become [Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_numerals "Arabic numerals") to the Islamic world by the 9th century.

[Narayana Pandita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narayana_Pandita_(mathematician) "Narayana Pandita (mathematician)") (1340–1400) was an Indian [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"). [Plofker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Plofker "Kim Plofker") writes that his texts were the most significant Sanskrit mathematics treatises after those of [Bhaskara II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_II "Bhaskara II"), other than the [Kerala school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_school_of_astronomy_and_mathematics "Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics"). He wrote the *[Ganita Kaumudi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganita_Kaumudi "Ganita Kaumudi")* (lit. "Moonlight of mathematics") in 1356 about mathematical operations. The work anticipated many developments in [combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics "Combinatorics").

Between the 14th and 16th centuries, the [Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_school_of_astronomy_and_mathematics "Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics") made significant advances in astronomy and especially mathematics, including fields such as trigonometry and analysis. In particular, [Madhava of Sangamagrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhava_of_Sangamagrama "Madhava of Sangamagrama") led advancement in [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis") by providing the infinite and taylor series expansion of some trigonometric functions and pi approximation. [Parameshvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameshvara "Parameshvara") (1380–1460), presents a case of the Mean Value theorem in his commentaries on [Govindasvāmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Govindasv%C4%81mi "Govindasvāmi") and [Bhāskara II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C4%81skara_II "Bhāskara II"). The *[Yuktibhāṣā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuktibh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3%C4%81 "Yuktibhāṣā")* was written by [Jyeshtadeva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyeshtadeva "Jyeshtadeva") in 1530.

#### Astronomy

The first textual mention of astronomical concepts comes from the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veda "Veda"), religious literature of India. According to Sarma (2008): "One finds in the [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda") intelligent speculations about the genesis of the universe from nonexistence, the configuration of the universe, the [spherical self-supporting earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_Earth "Spherical Earth"), and the year of 360 days divided into 12 equal parts of 30 days each with a periodical intercalary month.".

The first 12 chapters of the *[Siddhanta Shiromani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddh%C4%81nta_Shiromani "Siddhānta Shiromani")*, written by [Bhāskara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C4%81skara_II "Bhāskara II") in the 12th century, cover topics such as: mean longitudes of the planets; true longitudes of the planets; the three problems of diurnal rotation; syzygies; lunar eclipses; solar eclipses; latitudes of the planets; risings and settings; the moon's crescent; conjunctions of the planets with each other; conjunctions of the planets with the fixed stars; and the patas of the sun and moon. The 13 chapters of the second part cover the nature of the sphere, as well as significant astronomical and trigonometric calculations based on it.

In the *[Tantrasangraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantrasangraha "Tantrasangraha")* treatise, [Nilakantha Somayaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilakantha_Somayaji "Nilakantha Somayaji")'s updated the Aryabhatan model for the interior planets, Mercury, and Venus and the equation that he specified for the center of these planets was more accurate than the ones in European or Islamic astronomy until the time of [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler "Johannes Kepler") in the 17th century. [Jai Singh II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jai_Singh_II "Jai Singh II") of [Jaipur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Amber "Kingdom of Amber") constructed five [observatories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observatory "Observatory") called [Jantar Mantars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jantar_Mantar "Jantar Mantar") in total, in [New Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jantar_Mantar,_New_Delhi "Jantar Mantar, New Delhi"), [Jaipur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jantar_Mantar_(Jaipur) "Jantar Mantar (Jaipur)"), [Ujjain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jantar_Mantar,_Ujjain "Jantar Mantar, Ujjain"), [Mathura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathura,_Uttar_Pradesh "Mathura, Uttar Pradesh") and [Varanasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jantar_Mantar,_Varanasi "Jantar Mantar, Varanasi"); they were completed between 1724 and 1735.

#### Grammar

Some of the earliest linguistic activities can be found in [Iron Age India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_India "Iron Age India") (1st millennium BCE) with the analysis of [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") for the purpose of the correct recitation and interpretation of [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") texts. The most notable grammarian of Sanskrit was *[Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini "Pāṇini")* (c. 520–460 BCE), whose grammar formulates close to 4,000 rules for Sanskrit. Inherent in his analytic approach are the concepts of the [phoneme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme "Phoneme"), the [morpheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morpheme "Morpheme") and the [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root "Root"). The [Tolkāppiyam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolk%C4%81ppiyam "Tolkāppiyam") text, composed in the early centuries of the common era, is a comprehensive text on Tamil grammar, which includes sutras on orthography, phonology, etymology, morphology, semantics, prosody, sentence structure and the significance of context in language.

#### Medicine

Findings from [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") graveyards in what is now Pakistan show evidence of proto-dentistry among an early farming culture. The ancient text [Suśrutasamhitā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sushruta_Samhita "Sushruta Samhita") of [Suśruta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sushruta "Sushruta") describes procedures on various forms of surgery, including [rhinoplasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinoplasty "Rhinoplasty"), the repair of torn ear lobes, perineal [lithotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithotomy "Lithotomy"), cataract surgery, and several other excisions and other surgical procedures. The *[Charaka Samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charaka_Samhita "Charaka Samhita")* of [Charaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charaka "Charaka") describes ancient theories on human body, [etiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiology "Etiology"), [symptomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symptom "Symptom") and [therapeutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology") for a wide range of diseases. It also includes sections on the importance of diet, hygiene, prevention, medical education, and the teamwork of a physician, nurse and patient necessary for recovery to health.

#### Politics and state

An ancient Indian treatise on [statecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_administration "Public administration"), [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics") policy and [military strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_strategy "Military strategy") by Kautilya and *Viṣhṇugupta*, who are traditionally identified with [*Chāṇakya*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chanakya "Chanakya") (c. 350–283 BCE). In this treatise, the behaviors and relationships of the people, the King, the State, the Government Superintendents, Courtiers, Enemies, Invaders, and Corporations are analyzed and documented. [Roger Boesche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Boesche "Roger Boesche") describes the *[Arthaśāstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthashastra "Arthashastra")* as "a book of political realism, a book analyzing how the political world does work and not very often stating how it ought to work, a book that frequently discloses to a king what calculating and sometimes brutal measures he must carry out to preserve the state and the common good."

#### Logic

The development of Indian logic dates back to the Chandahsutra of Pingala and *[anviksiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anviksiki "Anviksiki")* of Medhatithi Gautama (c. 6th century BCE); the [Sanskrit grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vy%C4%81kara%E1%B9%87a "Vyākaraṇa") rules of [Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini "Pāṇini") (c. 5th century BCE); the [Vaisheshika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaisheshika "Vaisheshika") school's analysis of [atomism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomism "Atomism") (c. 6th century BCE to 2nd century BCE); the analysis of [inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference "Inference") by [Gotama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ny%C4%81ya_S%C5%ABtras "Nyāya Sūtras") (c. 6th century BCE to 2nd century CE), founder of the [Nyaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyaya "Nyaya") school of [Hindu philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_philosophy "Hindu philosophy"); and the [tetralemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetralemma "Tetralemma") of [Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna "Nagarjuna") (c. 2nd century CE).

[Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy "Indian philosophy") logic stands as one of the three original traditions of [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), alongside the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organon "Organon") and the [Chinese logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_logic "Chinese logic"). The Indian tradition continued to develop through early to modern times, in the form of the [Navya-Nyāya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navya-Ny%C4%81ya "Navya-Nyāya") school of logic.

In the 2nd century, the [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy "Buddhist philosophy") philosopher [Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna "Nagarjuna") refined the *Catuskoti* form of logic. The Catuskoti is also often glossed *[Tetralemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetralemma "Tetralemma")* (Greek) which is the name for a largely comparable, but not equatable, 'four corner argument' within the tradition of [Classical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_logic "Classical logic").

Navya-Nyāya developed a sophisticated language and conceptual scheme that allowed it to raise, analyse, and solve problems in logic and epistemology. It systematised all the Nyāya concepts into four main categories: sense or perception (pratyakşa), inference (anumāna), comparison or similarity ([upamāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upam%C4%81na "Upamāna")), and testimony (sound or word; śabda).

### China

#### Chinese mathematics

From the earliest[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] the Chinese used a positional decimal system on counting boards in order to calculate. To express 10, a single rod is placed in the second box from the right. The spoken language uses a similar system to English: e.g. four thousand two hundred and seven. No symbol was used for zero. By the 1st century BCE, negative numbers and decimal fractions were in use and *[The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nine_Chapters_on_the_Mathematical_Art "The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art")* included methods for extracting higher order roots by [Horner's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horner%27s_method "Horner's method") and solving linear equations and by [Pythagoras' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem"). Cubic equations were solved in the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty") and solutions of equations of order higher than 3 appeared in print in 1245 CE by [Ch'in Chiu-shao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%27in_Chiu-shao "Ch'in Chiu-shao"). [Pascal's triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_triangle "Pascal's triangle") for binomial coefficients was described around 1100 by [Jia Xian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jia_Xian "Jia Xian").

Although the first attempts at an axiomatization of geometry appear in the [Mohist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohist "Mohist") canon in 330 BCE, [Liu Hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Hui "Liu Hui") developed algebraic methods in geometry in the 3rd century CE and also calculated [pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi") to 5 significant figures. In 480, [Zu Chongzhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zu_Chongzhi "Zu Chongzhi") improved this by discovering the ratio

#### Astronomical observations

Astronomical observations from China constitute the longest continuous sequence from any civilization and include records of sunspots (112 records from 364 BCE), supernovas (1054), lunar and solar eclipses. By the 12th century, they could reasonably accurately make predictions of eclipses, but the knowledge of this was lost during the Ming dynasty, so that the Jesuit [Matteo Ricci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteo_Ricci "Matteo Ricci") gained much favor in 1601 by his predictions.[*[incomplete short citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:CITESHORT "Wikipedia:CITESHORT")*] By 635 Chinese astronomers had observed that the tails of comets always point away from the sun.

From antiquity, the Chinese used an equatorial system for describing the skies and a star map from 940 was drawn using a cylindrical ([Mercator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercator_projection "Mercator projection")) projection. The use of an [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere "Armillary sphere") is recorded from the 4th century BCE and a sphere permanently mounted in equatorial axis from 52 BCE. In 125 CE [Zhang Heng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhang_Heng "Zhang Heng") used water power to rotate the sphere in real time. This included rings for the meridian and ecliptic. By 1270 they had incorporated the principles of the Arab [torquetum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torquetum "Torquetum").

In the [Song Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_Empire "Song Empire") (960–1279) of [Imperial China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_China "Imperial China"), Chinese [scholar-officials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholar-official "Scholar-official") unearthed, studied, and cataloged ancient artifacts.

#### Inventions

To better prepare for calamities, Zhang Heng invented a [seismometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhang_Heng#Zhang's_seismoscope "Zhang Heng") in 132 CE which provided instant alert to authorities in the capital Luoyang that an earthquake had occurred in a location indicated by a specific [cardinal or ordinal direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction "Cardinal direction"). Although no tremors could be felt in the capital when Zhang told the court that an earthquake had just occurred in the northwest, a message came soon afterwards that an earthquake had indeed struck 400 to 500 km (250 to 310 mi) northwest of Luoyang (in what is now modern [Gansu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gansu "Gansu")). Zhang called his device the 'instrument for measuring the seasonal winds and the movements of the Earth' (Houfeng didong yi 候风地动仪), so-named because he and others thought that earthquakes were most likely caused by the enormous compression of trapped air.

There are many notable contributors to early Chinese disciplines, inventions, and practices throughout the ages. One of the best examples would be the medieval Song Chinese [Shen Kuo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shen_Kuo "Shen Kuo") (1031–1095), a [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath") and statesman who was the first to describe the [magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic "Magnetic")-needle [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass "Compass") used for [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation "Navigation"), discovered the concept of [true north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_north "True north"), improved the design of the astronomical [gnomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomon "Gnomon"), [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere "Armillary sphere"), sight tube, and [clepsydra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_clock "Water clock"), and described the use of [drydocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drydock "Drydock") to repair boats. After observing the natural process of the inundation of [silt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silt "Silt") and the find of [marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_(ocean) "Marine (ocean)") [fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil "Fossil") in the [Taihang Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taihang_Mountains "Taihang Mountains") (hundreds of miles from the Pacific Ocean), Shen Kuo devised a theory of land formation, or [geomorphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomorphology "Geomorphology"). He also adopted a theory of gradual [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change "Climate variability and change") in regions over time, after observing [petrified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrified "Petrified") [bamboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamboo "Bamboo") found underground at [Yan'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan%27an "Yan'an"), Shaanxi. If not for Shen Kuo's writing, the architectural works of [Yu Hao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu_Hao "Yu Hao") would be little known, along with the inventor of [movable type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type "Movable type") [printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing "Printing"), [Bi Sheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi_Sheng "Bi Sheng") (990–1051). Shen's contemporary [Su Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su_Song "Su Song") (1020–1101) was also a brilliant polymath, an astronomer who created a celestial atlas of star maps, wrote a treatise related to [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany "Botany"), [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology "Zoology"), [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy "Mineralogy"), and [metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy "Metallurgy"), and had erected a large [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical "Astronomical") [clocktower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clocktower "Clocktower") in [Kaifeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaifeng "Kaifeng") city in 1088. To operate the crowning [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere "Armillary sphere"), his clocktower featured an [escapement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escapement "Escapement") mechanism and the world's oldest known use of an endless power-transmitting [chain drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_drive "Chain drive").

The [Jesuit China missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit_China_missions "Jesuit China missions") of the 16th and 17th centuries "learned to appreciate the scientific achievements of this ancient culture and made them known in Europe. Through their correspondence European scientists first learned about the Chinese science and culture." Western academic thought on the history of Chinese technology and science was galvanized by the work of [Joseph Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Needham "Joseph Needham") and the Needham Research Institute. Among the technological accomplishments of China were, according to the British scholar Needham, the [water-powered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics "Hydraulics") [celestial globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_globe "Celestial globe") (Zhang Heng), [dry docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graving_dock "Graving dock"), sliding [calipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calipers "Calipers"), the double-action [piston pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piston_pump "Piston pump"), the [blast furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace "Blast furnace"), the multi-tube [seed drill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed_drill "Seed drill"), the [wheelbarrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheelbarrow "Wheelbarrow"), the [suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge "Suspension bridge"), the [winnowing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fengshanche "Fengshanche"), [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder "Gunpowder"), the [raised-relief map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raised-relief_map "Raised-relief map"), toilet paper, the efficient harness, along with contributions in [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"), and other fields.

However, cultural factors prevented these Chinese achievements from developing into "modern science". According to Needham, it may have been the religious and philosophical framework of Chinese intellectuals which made them unable to accept the ideas of laws of nature:

> It was not that there was no order in nature for the Chinese, but rather that it was not an order ordained by a rational personal being, and hence there was no conviction that rational personal beings would be able to spell out in their lesser earthly languages the divine code of laws which he had decreed aforetime. The [Taoists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoists "Taoists"), indeed, would have scorned such an idea as being too naïve for the subtlety and complexity of the universe as they intuited it.

## Pre-Columbian Mesoamerica

During the [Middle Formative Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_chronology "Mesoamerican chronology") (c. 900 BCE – c. 300 BCE) of [Pre-Columbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian "Pre-Columbian") [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"), the [Zapotec civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapotec_civilization "Zapotec civilization"), heavily influenced by the [Olmec civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmec_civilization "Olmec civilization"), established the first known [full writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapotec_script "Zapotec script") of the region (possibly predated by [the Olmec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmec_hieroglyphs "Olmec hieroglyphs") [Cascajal Block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cascajal_Block "Cascajal Block")), as well as the first known astronomical [calendar in Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_calendars "Mesoamerican calendars"). Following a period of initial urban development in the [Preclassical period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preclassic_Maya "Preclassic Maya"), the [Classic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_Maya "Classic Maya") [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") (c. 250 CE – c. 900 CE) built on the shared heritage of the Olmecs by developing the most sophisticated systems of [writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_script "Maya script"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_astronomy "Maya astronomy"), [calendrical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_calendar "Maya calendar"), and [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_numerals "Maya numerals") among Mesoamerican peoples. The Maya developed a [positional numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positional_numeral_system "Positional numeral system") with a [base of 20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigesimal "Vigesimal") that included the use of [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero "Zero") for constructing their calendars. Maya writing, which was developed by 200 BCE, widespread by 100 BCE, and rooted [in Olmec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epi-Olmec_script "Epi-Olmec script") and Zapotec scripts, contains easily discernible calendar dates in the form of [logographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logograph "Logograph") representing numbers, coefficients, and calendar periods amounting to 20 days and even 20 years for tracking social, religious, political, and economic events in 360-day years.

## Classical antiquity and Greco-Roman natural philosophy

The contributions of the Ancient Egyptians and Mesopotamians in the areas of astronomy, mathematics, and medicine had entered and shaped [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy") [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), whereby formal attempts were made to provide explanations of events in the [physical world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") based on natural causes. Inquiries were also aimed at such practical goals such as establishing a reliable calendar or determining how to cure a variety of illnesses. The ancient people who were considered the first *[scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientists "Scientists")* may have thought of themselves as *natural philosophers*, as practitioners of a skilled profession (for example, [physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician")), or as followers of a [religious tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") (for example, [temple healers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asclepeion "Asclepeion")).

### Pre-socratics

The earliest [Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Greek_philosophers "List of Greek philosophers"), known as the [pre-Socratics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratics "Pre-Socratics"), provided competing answers to the question found in the myths of their neighbors: "How did the ordered [cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos "Cosmos") in which we live come to be?" The pre-Socratic philosopher [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales "Thales") (640–546 BCE) of [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus "Miletus"), identified by later authors such as Aristotle as the first of the [Ionian philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_School_(philosophy) "Ionian School (philosophy)"), postulated non-supernatural explanations for natural phenomena. For example, that land floats on water and that earthquakes are caused by the agitation of the water upon which the land floats, rather than the god Poseidon. Thales' student [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras "Pythagoras") of [Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samos "Samos") founded the [Pythagorean school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreanism "Pythagoreanism"), which investigated mathematics for its own sake, and was the first to postulate that the Earth is spherical in shape. [Leucippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leucippus "Leucippus") (5th century BCE) introduced [atomism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomism "Atomism"), the theory that all [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter "Matter") is made of indivisible, imperishable units called [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms "Atoms"). This was greatly expanded on by his pupil [Democritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democritus "Democritus") and later [Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus "Epicurus").

### Plato and Aristotle

[Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") produced the first systematic discussions of natural philosophy, which did much to shape later investigations of nature. Their development of [deductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_reasoning "Deductive reasoning") was of particular importance and usefulness to later scientific inquiry. Plato founded the [Platonic Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy "Platonic Academy") in 387 BCE, whose motto was "Let none unversed in geometry enter here," and also turned out many notable philosophers. Plato's student Aristotle introduced [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism "Empiricism") and the notion that universal truths can be arrived at via observation and induction, thereby laying the foundations of the scientific method. Aristotle also produced [many biological writings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle%27s_biology "Aristotle's biology") that were empirical in nature, focusing on biological causation and the diversity of life. He made countless observations of nature, especially the habits and attributes of plants and animals on [Lesbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbos "Lesbos"), classified more than 540 animal species, and dissected at least 50. Aristotle's writings profoundly influenced subsequent [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Science in the medieval Islamic world") and [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_science_in_the_Middle_Ages "European science in the Middle Ages") scholarship, though they were eventually superseded in the [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution").

Aristotle also contributed to theories of the elements and the cosmos. He believed that the [celestial bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object "Astronomical object") (such as the planets and the Sun) had something called an [unmoved mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmoved_mover "Unmoved mover") that put the celestial bodies in motion. Aristotle tried to explain everything through mathematics and physics, but sometimes explained things such as the motion of celestial bodies through a higher power such as God. Aristotle did not have the technological advancements that would have explained the motion of celestial bodies. In addition, Aristotle had many views on the elements. He believed that everything was derived of the elements earth, water, air, fire, and lastly the [Aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aether_(classical_element) "Aether (classical element)"). The Aether was a celestial element, and therefore made up the matter of the celestial bodies. The elements of earth, water, air and fire were derived of a combination of two of the characteristics of hot, wet, cold, and dry, and all had their inevitable place and motion. The motion of these elements begins with earth being the closest to "the Earth," then water, air, fire, and finally Aether. In addition to the makeup of all things, Aristotle came up with theories as to why things did not return to their natural motion. He understood that water sits above earth, air above water, and fire above air in their natural state. He explained that although all elements must return to their natural state, the human body and other living things have a constraint on the elements – thus not allowing the elements making one who they are to return to their natural state.

The important legacy of this period included substantial advances in factual knowledge, especially in [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy "Anatomy"), [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology "Zoology"), [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany "Botany"), [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy "Mineralogy"), [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography "Geography"), [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"); an awareness of the importance of certain scientific problems, especially those related to the problem of change and its causes; and a recognition of the methodological importance of applying mathematics to natural phenomena and of undertaking empirical research. In the [Hellenistic age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_age "Hellenistic age") scholars frequently employed the principles developed in earlier Greek thought: the application of mathematics and deliberate empirical research, in their scientific investigations. Neither reason nor inquiry began with the Ancient Greeks, but the [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method "Socratic method") did, along with the idea of [Forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substantial_form "Substantial form"), give great advances in geometry, [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), and the natural sciences. According to [Benjamin Farrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Farrington "Benjamin Farrington"), former professor of [Classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics "Classics") at [Swansea University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea_University "Swansea University"):

:   "Men were weighing for thousands of years before [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes") worked out the laws of equilibrium; they must have had practical and intuitional knowledge of the principals involved. What Archimedes did was to sort out the theoretical implications of this practical knowledge and present the resulting body of knowledge as a logically coherent system."

and again:

:   "With astonishment we find ourselves on the threshold of modern science. Nor should it be supposed that by some trick of translation the extracts have been given an air of modernity. Far from it. The vocabulary of these writings and their style are the source from which our own vocabulary and style have been derived."

### Greek astronomy

The astronomer [Aristarchus of Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchus_of_Samos "Aristarchus of Samos") was the first known person to propose a heliocentric model of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System"), while the geographer [Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes "Eratosthenes") accurately calculated the circumference of the Earth. [Hipparchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus "Hipparchus") (c. 190 – c. 120 BCE) produced the first systematic [star catalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_astronomical_maps,_catalogs,_and_surveys "Timeline of astronomical maps, catalogs, and surveys"). The level of achievement in Hellenistic astronomy and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering "Engineering") is impressively shown by the [Antikythera mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_mechanism "Antikythera mechanism") (150–100 BCE), an [analog computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_computer "Analog computer") for calculating the position of planets. Technological artifacts of similar complexity did not reappear until the 14th century, when mechanical [astronomical clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_clock "Astronomical clock") appeared in Europe.

### Hellenistic medicine

There was not a defined societal structure for healthcare during the age of Hippocrates. At that time, society was not organized and knowledgeable as people still relied on pure religious reasoning to explain illnesses. Hippocrates introduced the first healthcare system based on science and clinical protocols. Hippocrates' theories about physics and medicine helped pave the way in creating an organized medical structure for society. In [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"), [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates "Hippocrates") (c. 460–370 BCE) and his followers were the first to describe many diseases and medical conditions and developed the [Hippocratic Oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Oath "Hippocratic Oath") for physicians, still relevant and in use today. Hippocrates' ideas are expressed in [The Hippocratic Corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Corpus "Hippocratic Corpus"). The collection notes descriptions of medical philosophies and how disease and lifestyle choices reflect on the physical body. Hippocrates influenced a Westernized, professional relationship among physician and patient. [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates "Hippocrates") is also known as "the Father of Medicine". [Herophilos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herophilos "Herophilos") (335–280 BCE) was the first to base his conclusions on dissection of the human body and to describe the [nervous system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system "Nervous system"). [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen "Galen") (129 – c. 200 CE) performed many audacious operations—including brain and eye [surgeries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgery "Surgery")— that were not tried again for almost two millennia.

### Greek mathematics

In [Hellenistic Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom "Ptolemaic Kingdom"), the mathematician [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid") laid down the foundations of [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor "Mathematical rigor") and introduced the concepts of definition, axiom, theorem and proof still in use today in his [*Elements*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_elements "Euclid's elements"), considered the most influential textbook ever written. [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes"), considered one of the greatest mathematicians of all time, is credited with using the [method of exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion "Method of exhaustion") to calculate the [area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area "Area") under the arc of a [parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola "Parabola") with the [summation of an infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_(mathematics) "Series (mathematics)"), and gave a remarkably accurate approximation of [pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi"). He is also known in physics for laying the foundations of [hydrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_statics "Fluid statics"), [statics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statics "Statics"), and the explanation of the principle of the [lever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lever "Lever").

### Other developments

[Theophrastus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophrastus "Theophrastus") wrote some of the earliest descriptions of plants and animals, establishing the first [taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy_(biology) "Taxonomy (biology)") and looking at minerals in terms of their properties, such as [hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardness "Hardness"). [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder "Pliny the Elder") produced one of the largest [encyclopedias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia "Encyclopedia") of the natural world in 77 CE, and was a successor to Theophrastus. For example, he accurately describes the [octahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral "Octahedral") shape of the [diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond "Diamond") and noted that diamond dust is used by [engravers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraver "Engraver") to cut and polish other gems owing to its great hardness. His recognition of the importance of [crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal "Crystal") shape is a precursor to modern [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography "Crystallography"), while notes on other minerals presages mineralogy. He recognizes other minerals have characteristic crystal shapes, but in one example, confuses the [crystal habit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_habit "Crystal habit") with the work of [lapidaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapidaries "Lapidaries"). Pliny was the first to show [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber "Amber") was a resin from pine trees, because of trapped insects within them.

The development of archaeology has its roots in history and with those who were interested in the past, such as kings and queens who wanted to show past glories of their respective nations. The 5th-century-BCE [Greek historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_historiography "Greek historiography") [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") was the first scholar to systematically study the past and perhaps the first to examine artifacts.

### Greek scholarship under Roman rule

During the rule of Rome, famous historians such as [Polybius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybius "Polybius"), [Livy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livy "Livy") and [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch "Plutarch") documented the rise of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic"), and the organization and histories of other nations, while statesmen like [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar"), Cicero, and others provided examples of the politics of the republic and Rome's empire and wars. The study of politics during this age was oriented toward understanding history, understanding methods of governing, and describing the operation of governments.

The [Roman conquest of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece_in_the_Roman_era "Greece in the Roman era") did not diminish learning and culture in the Greek provinces. On the contrary, the appreciation of Greek achievements in literature, philosophy, politics, and the arts by Rome's [upper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_class "Upper class") coincided with the increased prosperity of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"). Greek settlements had existed in Italy for centuries and the ability to read and speak Greek was not uncommon in Italian cities such as Rome. Moreover, the settlement of Greek scholars in Rome, whether voluntarily or as slaves, gave Romans access to teachers of Greek literature and philosophy. Conversely, young Roman scholars also studied abroad in Greece and upon their return to Rome, were able to convey Greek achievements to their Latin leadership. And despite the translation of a few Greek texts into Latin, Roman scholars who aspired to the highest level did so using the Greek language. The Roman [statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politician "Politician") and philosopher [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero "Cicero") (106 – 43 BCE) was a prime example. He had studied under Greek teachers in Rome and then in Athens and [Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes "Rhodes"). He mastered considerable portions of Greek philosophy, wrote Latin treatises on several topics, and even wrote Greek commentaries of Plato's *[Timaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue) "Timaeus (dialogue)")* as well as a Latin translation of it, which has not survived.

In the beginning, support for scholarship in Greek knowledge was almost entirely funded by the Roman upper class. There were all sorts of arrangements, ranging from a talented scholar being attached to a wealthy household to owning educated Greek-speaking slaves. In exchange, scholars who succeeded at the highest level had an obligation to provide advice or intellectual companionship to their Roman benefactors, or to even take care of their libraries. The less fortunate or accomplished ones would teach their children or perform menial tasks. The level of detail and sophistication of Greek knowledge was adjusted to suit the interests of their Roman patrons. That meant popularizing Greek knowledge by presenting information that were of practical value such as medicine or logic (for courts and politics) but excluding subtle details of Greek metaphysics and epistemology. Beyond the basics, the Romans did not value natural philosophy and considered it an amusement for leisure time.

Commentaries and [encyclopedias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia "Encyclopedia") were the means by which Greek knowledge was popularized for Roman audiences. The Greek scholar [Posidonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posidonius "Posidonius") (c. 135-c. 51 BCE), a native of Syria, wrote prolifically on history, geography, moral philosophy, and natural philosophy. He greatly influenced Latin writers such as [Marcus Terentius Varro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Terentius_Varro "Marcus Terentius Varro") (116-27 BCE), who wrote the encyclopedia *Nine Books of Disciplines*, which covered nine arts: grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, musical theory, medicine, and architecture. The *Disciplines* became a model for subsequent Roman encyclopedias and Varro's nine liberal arts were considered suitable education for a Roman gentleman. The first seven of Varro's nine arts would later define the [seven liberal arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_arts_education#History "Liberal arts education") of [medieval schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university "Medieval university"). The pinnacle of the popularization movement was the Roman scholar [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder "Pliny the Elder") (23/24–79 CE), a native of northern Italy, who wrote several books on the history of Rome and grammar. His most famous work was his voluminous *[Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_(Pliny) "Natural History (Pliny)")*.

After the death of the Roman Emperor [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius "Marcus Aurelius") in 180 CE, the favorable conditions for scholarship and learning in the Roman Empire were upended by political unrest, civil war, urban decay, and looming economic crisis. In around 250 CE, [barbarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarian#In_classical_Greco-Roman_contexts "Barbarian") began attacking and invading the Roman frontiers. These combined events led to a general decline in political and economic conditions. The living standards of the Roman upper class was severely impacted, and their loss of [leisure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leisure "Leisure") diminished scholarly pursuits. Moreover, during the 3rd and 4th centuries CE, the Roman Empire was administratively divided into two halves: [Greek East and Latin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_East_and_Latin_West "Greek East and Latin West"). These administrative divisions weakened the intellectual contact between the two regions. Eventually, both halves went their separate ways, with the Greek East becoming the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"). [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") was also steadily expanding during this time and soon became a major patron of education in the Latin West. Initially, the Christian church adopted some of the reasoning tools of Greek philosophy in the 2nd and 3rd centuries CE to defend its faith against sophisticated opponents. Nevertheless, Greek philosophy received a mixed reception from leaders and adherents of the Christian faith. Some such as [Tertullian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertullian "Tertullian") (c. 155-c. 230 CE) were vehemently opposed to philosophy, denouncing it as [heretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy "Heresy"). Others such as [Augustine of Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo") (354-430 CE) were ambivalent and defended Greek philosophy and science as the best ways to understand the natural world and therefore treated it as a [handmaiden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handmaiden "Handmaiden") (or servant) of religion. Education in the West began its gradual decline, along with the rest of [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire"), due to invasions by Germanic tribes, civil unrest, and economic collapse. Contact with the classical tradition was lost in specific regions such as [Roman Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain "Roman Britain") and northern [Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Gaul "Roman Gaul") but continued to exist in Rome, northern Italy, southern Gaul, Spain, and [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_(Roman_province) "Africa (Roman province)").

## Middle Ages

In the Middle Ages, the classical learning continued in three major linguistic cultures and civilizations: Greek (the Byzantine Empire), Arabic (the Islamic world), and Latin (Western Europe).

### Byzantine Empire

#### Preservation of Greek heritage

The [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Fall of the Western Roman Empire") led to a deterioration of the classical tradition in the western part (or [Latin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_West "Latin West")) of Europe during the 5th century. In contrast, the Byzantine Empire resisted the barbarian attacks and preserved and improved the learning.

While the Byzantine Empire still held learning centers such as [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople"), Alexandria and Antioch, Western Europe's knowledge was concentrated in [monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastery "Monastery") until the development of [medieval universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university "Medieval university") in the 12th centuries. The curriculum of monastic schools included the study of the few available ancient texts and of new works on practical subjects like medicine and timekeeping.

In the sixth century in the Byzantine Empire, [Isidore of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Miletus "Isidore of Miletus") compiled Archimedes' mathematical works in the [Archimedes Palimpsest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes_Palimpsest "Archimedes Palimpsest"), where all Archimedes' mathematical contributions were collected and studied.

[John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus"), another Byzantine scholar, was the first to question Aristotle's teaching of physics, introducing the [theory of impetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_impetus "Theory of impetus"). The theory of impetus was an auxiliary or secondary theory of Aristotelian dynamics, put forth initially to explain projectile motion against gravity. It is the intellectual precursor to the concepts of inertia, momentum and acceleration in classical mechanics. The works of John Philoponus inspired [Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei") ten centuries later.

#### Collapse

During the [Fall of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople") in 1453, a number of Greek scholars fled to North Italy in which they fueled the era later commonly known as the "[Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance")" as they brought with them a great deal of classical learning including an understanding of botany, medicine, and zoology. Byzantium also gave the West important inputs: John Philoponus' criticism of Aristotelian physics, and the works of Dioscorides.

### Islamic world

This was the period (8th–14th century CE) of the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age") where commerce thrived, and new ideas and technologies emerged such as the importation of [papermaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papermaking "Papermaking") from China, which made the copying of manuscripts inexpensive.

#### Translations and Hellenization

The eastward transmission of Greek heritage to Western Asia was a slow and gradual process that spanned over a thousand years, beginning with the Asian conquests of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") in 335 BCE to the [founding of Islam in the 7th century CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Islamic_history "Timeline of Islamic history"). The birth and expansion of Islam during the 7th century was quickly followed by its [Hellenization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization "Hellenization"). Knowledge of [Greek conceptions of the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_classical_antiquity "Science in classical antiquity") was preserved and absorbed into Islamic theology, law, culture, and commerce, which were aided by the translations of traditional Greek texts and some [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language") intermediary sources into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language") during the 8th–9th century.

#### Education and scholarly pursuits

[Madrasas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa "Madrasa") were centers for many different religious and scientific studies and were the culmination of different institutions such as mosques based around religious studies, housing for out-of-town visitors, and finally educational institutions focused on the natural sciences. Unlike Western universities, students at a madrasa would learn from one specific teacher, who would issue a certificate at the completion of their studies called an [Ijazah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijazah "Ijazah"). An Ijazah differs from a western university degree in many ways one being that it is issued by a single person rather than an institution, and another being that it is not an individual degree declaring adequate knowledge over broad subjects, but rather a license to teach and pass on a very specific set of texts. Women were also allowed to attend madrasas, as both students and teachers, something not seen in high western education until the 1800s. Madrasas were more than just academic centers. The [Suleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque "Süleymaniye Mosque"), for example, was one of the earliest and most well-known madrasas, which was built by [Suleiman the Magnificent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent "Suleiman the Magnificent") in the 16th century. The Suleymaniye Mosque was home to a hospital and medical college, a kitchen, and children's school, as well as serving as a temporary home for travelers.

Higher education at a madrasa (or college) was focused on Islamic law and religious science and students had to engage in self-study for everything else. And despite the occasional theological backlash, many Islamic scholars of science were able to conduct their work in relatively tolerant urban centers (e.g., [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad") and [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo")) and were protected by powerful patrons. They could also travel freely and exchange ideas as there were no political barriers within the unified Islamic state. Islamic science during this time was primarily focused on the correction, extension, articulation, and application of Greek ideas to new problems.

#### Advancements in mathematics

Most of the achievements by Islamic scholars during this period were in mathematics. [Arabic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Mathematics in the medieval Islamic world") was a direct descendant of Greek and Indian mathematics. For instance, what is now known as [Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_numerals "Arabic numerals") originally came from India, but Muslim mathematicians made several key refinements to the number system, such as the introduction of [decimal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_separator "Decimal separator") notation. Mathematicians such as [Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi "Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi") (c. 780–850) gave his name to the concept of the [algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm"), while the term [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") is derived from *al-jabr*, the beginning of the title of one of his publications. Islamic trigonometry continued from the works of Ptolemy's *[Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest "Almagest")* and Indian *[Siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddh%C4%81nta_Shiromani "Siddhānta Shiromani")*, from which they added [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_functions "Trigonometric functions"), drew up tables, and applied trigonometry to spheres and planes. Many of their engineers, instruments makers, and surveyors contributed books in applied mathematics. It was in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_astronomy "Islamic astronomy") where Islamic mathematicians made their greatest contributions. [Al-Battani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Battani "Al-Battani") (c. 858–929) improved the measurements of [Hipparchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus "Hipparchus"), preserved in the translation of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy")'s *Hè Megalè Syntaxis* (*The great treatise*) translated as *[Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest "Almagest")*. Al-Battani also improved the precision of the measurement of the precession of the Earth's axis. Corrections were made to Ptolemy's [geocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_model "Geocentric model") by al-Battani, [Ibn al-Haytham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham"), [Averroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes "Averroes") and the [Maragha astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragheh_observatory "Maragheh observatory") such as [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Din_al-Tusi "Nasir al-Din al-Tusi"), [Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Urdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27ayyad_al-Din_al-Urdi "Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Urdi") and [Ibn al-Shatir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Shatir "Ibn al-Shatir").

Scholars with geometric skills made significant improvements to the earlier classical texts on light and sight by Euclid, Aristotle, and Ptolemy. The earliest surviving Arabic treatises were written in the 9th century by [Abū Ishāq al-Kindī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi "Al-Kindi"), [Qustā ibn Lūqā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qusta_ibn_Luqa "Qusta ibn Luqa"), and (in fragmentary form) Ahmad ibn Isā. Later in the 11th century, [Ibn al-Haytham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham") (known as Alhazen in the West), a mathematician and astronomer, synthesized a new theory of vision based on the works of his predecessors. His new theory included a complete system of geometrical optics, which was set in great detail in his *[Book of Optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Optics "Book of Optics")*. His book was translated into Latin and was relied upon as a principal source on the science of optics in Europe until the 17th century.

#### Institutionalization of medicine

The medical sciences were prominently cultivated in the Islamic world. The works of Greek medical theories, especially those of Galen, were translated into Arabic and there was an outpouring of medical texts by Islamic physicians, which were aimed at organizing, elaborating, and disseminating classical medical knowledge. [Medical specialties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_specialty "Medical specialty") started to emerge, such as those involved in the treatment of eye diseases such as [cataracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract "Cataract"). Ibn Sina (known as [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna") in the West, c. 980–1037) was a prolific Persian medical encyclopedist wrote extensively on medicine, with his two most notable works in medicine being the *Kitāb al-shifāʾ* ("Book of Healing") and [The Canon of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canon_of_Medicine "The Canon of Medicine"), both of which were used as standard medicinal texts in both the Muslim world and in Europe well into the 17th century. Amongst his many contributions are the discovery of the contagious nature of infectious diseases, and the introduction of clinical pharmacology. Institutionalization of medicine was another important achievement in the Islamic world. Although hospitals as an institution for the sick emerged in the Byzantium empire, the model of institutionalized medicine for all social classes was extensive in the Islamic empire and was scattered throughout. In addition to treating patients, physicians could teach apprentice physicians, as well write and do research. The discovery of the pulmonary transit of blood in the human body by [Ibn al-Nafis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Nafis "Ibn al-Nafis") occurred in a hospital setting.

#### Decline

Islamic science began its decline in the 12th–13th century, before the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") in Europe, due in part to the [Christian reconquest of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista") and the [Mongol conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquests "Mongol conquests") in the East in the 11th–13th century. The Mongols [sacked Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_(1258) "Siege of Baghdad (1258)"), capital of the [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate"), in 1258, which ended the [Abbasid empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate"). Nevertheless, many of the conquerors became patrons of the sciences. [Hulagu Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulagu_Khan "Hulagu Khan"), for example, who led the siege of Baghdad, became a patron of the [Maragheh observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragheh_observatory "Maragheh observatory"). Islamic astronomy continued to flourish into the 16th century.

### Western Europe

By the eleventh century, most of Europe had become Christian; stronger monarchies emerged; borders were restored; technological developments and agricultural innovations were made, increasing the food supply and population. Classical Greek texts were translated from Arabic and Greek into Latin, stimulating scientific discussion in Western Europe.

In [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), Greek and Roman taboos had meant that dissection was usually banned, but in the Middle Ages medical teachers and students at Bologna began to open human bodies, and [Mondino de Luzzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondino_de_Luzzi "Mondino de Luzzi") (c. 1275–1326) produced the first known anatomy textbook based on human dissection.

As a result of the [Pax Mongolica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Mongolica "Pax Mongolica"), Europeans, such as [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo "Marco Polo"), began to venture further and further east. The written accounts of Polo and his fellow travelers inspired other Western European maritime explorers to search for a direct sea route to Asia, ultimately leading to the [Age of Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery "Age of Discovery").

Technological advances were also made, such as the early flight of [Eilmer of Malmesbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilmer_of_Malmesbury "Eilmer of Malmesbury") (who had studied mathematics in 11th-century England), and the metallurgical achievements of the [Cistercian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercians "Cistercians") [blast furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace "Blast furnace") at [Laskill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laskill "Laskill").

#### Medieval universities

An intellectual revitalization of Western Europe started with the birth of [medieval universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university "Medieval university") in the 12th century. These urban institutions grew from the informal scholarly activities of learned [friars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar "Friar") who visited [monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastery "Monastery"), consulted [libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library "Library"), and conversed with other fellow scholars. A friar who became well-known would attract a following of disciples, giving rise to a brotherhood of scholars (or *collegium* in Latin). A *collegium* might travel to a town or request a monastery to host them. However, if the number of scholars within a *collegium* grew too large, they would opt to settle in a town instead. As the number of *collegia* within a town grew, the *collegia* might request that their king grant them a [charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter "Charter") that would convert them into a *universitas*. Many universities were chartered during this period, with the first in [Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bologna "Bologna") in 1088, followed by [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") in 1150, [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford "Oxford") in 1167, and [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge") in 1231. The granting of a charter meant that the medieval universities were partially sovereign and independent from local authorities. Their independence allowed them to conduct themselves and judge their own members based on their own rules. Furthermore, as initially religious institutions, their faculties and students were protected from capital punishment (e.g., [gallows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallows "Gallows")). Such independence was a matter of custom, which could, in principle, be revoked by their respective rulers if they felt threatened. Discussions of various subjects or claims at these medieval institutions, no matter how controversial, were done in a formalized way so as to declare such discussions as being within the bounds of a university and therefore protected by the privileges of that institution's sovereignty. A claim could be described as *[ex cathedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_infallibility#Ex_cathedra "Papal infallibility")* (literally "from the chair", used within the context of teaching) or *[ex hypothesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases_(E)#ex_hypothesi "List of Latin phrases (E)")* (by hypothesis). This meant that the discussions were presented as purely an intellectual exercise that did not require those involved to commit themselves to the truth of a claim or to proselytize. Modern academic concepts and practices such as [academic freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_freedom "Academic freedom") or freedom of inquiry are remnants of these medieval privileges that were tolerated in the past.

The curriculum of these medieval institutions centered on the [seven liberal arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_arts_education#History "Liberal arts education"), which were aimed at providing beginning students with the skills for reasoning and scholarly language. Students would begin their studies starting with the first three liberal arts or *[Trivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivium "Trivium")* (grammar, rhetoric, and logic) followed by the next four liberal arts or *[Quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium "Quadrivium")* (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music). Those who completed these requirements and received their *[baccalaureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor%27s_degree "Bachelor's degree")* (or [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts")) had the option to join the higher faculty (law, medicine, or theology), which would confer an [LLD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legum_Doctor "Legum Doctor") for a lawyer, an [MD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine "Doctor of Medicine") for a physician, or [ThD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Theology "Doctor of Theology") for a theologian. Students who chose to remain in the lower faculty (arts) could work towards a *[Magister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magister_degree "Magister degree")* (or [Master's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master%27s_degree "Master's degree")) degree and would study three philosophies: metaphysics, ethics, and natural philosophy. [Latin translations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century "Latin translations of the 12th century") of Aristotle's works such as *[De Anima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Anima "De Anima")* (*On the Soul*) and the commentaries on them were required readings. As time passed, the lower faculty was allowed to confer its own doctoral degree called the [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy"). Many of the Masters were drawn to encyclopedias and had used them as textbooks. But these scholars yearned for the complete original texts of the Ancient Greek philosophers, mathematicians, and physicians such as [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"), [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid"), and [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen "Galen"), which were not available to them at the time. These Ancient Greek texts were to be found in the Byzantine Empire and the Islamic World.

#### Translations of Greek and Arabic sources

Contact with the Byzantine Empire, and with the Islamic world during the [Reconquista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista") and the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades"), allowed Latin Europe access to scientific [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language") texts, including the works of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"), [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy"), [Isidore of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Miletus "Isidore of Miletus"), [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus"), [Jābir ibn Hayyān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C4%81bir_ibn_Hayy%C4%81n "Jābir ibn Hayyān"), [al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_M%C5%ABs%C4%81_al-Khw%C4%81rizm%C4%AB "Muhammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī"), [Alhazen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham"), [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna"), and [Averroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes "Averroes"). European scholars had access to the translation programs of [Raymond of Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_of_Toledo "Raymond of Toledo"), who sponsored the 12th century [Toledo School of Translators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo_School_of_Translators "Toledo School of Translators") from Arabic to Latin. Later translators like [Michael Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Scotus "Michael Scotus") would learn Arabic in order to study these texts directly. The European universities aided materially in the [translation and propagation of these texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century "Latin translations of the 12th century") and started a new infrastructure which was needed for scientific communities. In fact, European university put many works about the natural world and the study of nature at the center of its curriculum, with the result that the "medieval university laid far greater emphasis on science than does its modern counterpart and descendent."

At the beginning of the 13th century, there were reasonably accurate Latin translations of the main works of almost all the intellectually crucial ancient authors, allowing a sound transfer of scientific ideas via both the universities and the monasteries. By then, the natural philosophy in these texts began to be extended by [scholastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism") such as [Robert Grosseteste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Grosseteste "Robert Grosseteste"), [Roger Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Bacon "Roger Bacon"), [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus "Albertus Magnus") and [Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus "Duns Scotus"). Precursors of the modern scientific method, influenced by earlier contributions of the Islamic world, can be seen already in Grosseteste's emphasis on mathematics as a way to understand nature, and in the empirical approach admired by Bacon, particularly in his *[Opus Majus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Majus "Opus Majus")*.

In the 13th century [Stephen Tempier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Tempier "Stephen Tempier") – the Bishop of Paris – issued strict religious restrictions related to the study of Aristotle (the [Condemnation of 1277](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condemnation_of_1277 "Condemnation of 1277")). While the physicist [Pierre Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Duhem "Pierre Duhem")'s thesis that this, paradoxically, led to the study of medieval science as a serious discipline, "no one in the field any longer endorses his view that modern science started in 1277". However, many scholars agree with Duhem's view that the mid-late Middle Ages saw important scientific developments and continue to argue that the 13th-century Condemnations were beneficial for medieval natural philosophy because they loosened the hold of Aristotelian dogma on natural philosophers in the following century. By condemning any proposition of natural philosophy that subordinated [divine omnipotence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipotence#Scholasticism "Omnipotence") - "God's absolute power" - to Aristotelian impossibilities, the Condemnations, it is argued, may have inadvertently freed scholars to explore "what-if" scenarios that Aristotle had declared impossible, such as [the existence of a vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horror_vacui_(philosophy)#Origin "Horror vacui (philosophy)"), the plurality of worlds, or [the rotation of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_rotation#History "Earth's rotation"). This encouraged a shift in natural philosophy from an Aristotelian-style search for logical necessity toward the possibility of contingency (e.g. the idea that the *actual* order of Nature is one of many God *could* have chosen).

#### Natural philosophy

The natural philosophy of the first half of the 14th century consisted largely of [scholastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism") commentaries on, and arguments about, Latin translations of the work of Aristotle. [William of Ockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham "William of Ockham") emphasized the principle of [parsimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor "Occam's razor"): natural philosophers should not postulate unnecessary entities, so that motion is not a distinct thing but is only the moving object and an intermediary "sensible species" is not needed to transmit an image of an object to the eye. Scholars such as [Jean Buridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Buridan "Jean Buridan") and [Nicole Oresme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_Oresme "Nicole Oresme") started to reinterpret elements of Aristotle's mechanics. In particular, Buridan developed the theory that impetus was the cause of the motion of projectiles, which was a first step towards the modern concept of [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia "Inertia"). The [Oxford Calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Calculators "Oxford Calculators") began to mathematically analyze the [kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematics "Kinematics") of motion, making this analysis without considering the causes of motion. In 1348, the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death") and other disasters abruptly interrupted this scholarship.

## Renaissance

### Printing and discovery

The introduction and rapid spread of the [movable type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type "Movable type") [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press "Printing press") during the 2nd half of the 15th century ended the [manuscript culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript_culture "Manuscript culture") of the Middle Ages, where [facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact "Fact") were few and far between, and replaced it with a [printing culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_culture "Printing culture") where reliable and documented facts rapidly proliferated and became the secure foundation for scientific knowledge.

The [Fall of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople") in 1453 caused many [Byzantine scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_scholars_in_the_Renaissance "Greek scholars in the Renaissance") to seek refuge in the West.

In the mid-15th century, Venetian glassmakers developed the exceptionally clear colourless glass, *[cristallo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cristallo "Cristallo")*, made from high-purity quartz pebbles (instead of sand) and using manganese oxide as a "decolorizer" to neutralize the greenish tint caused by iron impurities. This was the "specialty" glass of the era, a luxury product used for windows, mirrors, ships' lanterns, and lenses. When the first telescope was later invented during the Scientific Revolution, the first historical record of the invention did not appear in a work of natural philosophy but rather in a patent filed by a [spectacle maker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Lipperhey "Hans Lipperhey").

The encounter with the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"), continents that were completely unknown to the [ancients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), profoundly impacted European intellectual life in the 16th century and specifically undermined the authority of [Claudius Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Ptolemy "Claudius Ptolemy"), the 2nd-century scholar whose [geographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_(Ptolemy) "Geography (Ptolemy)") and [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism#Ptolemaic_model "Geocentrism") models had previously been considered infallible.

[Vesalius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesalius "Vesalius")'s work on human cadavers found problems with the Galenic view of anatomy.

The [Northern Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Renaissance "Northern Renaissance") showed a decisive shift in focus from Aristotelian natural philosophy to chemistry and the biological sciences (botany, anatomy, and medicine).

### Copernican heliocentrism

**Copernican heliocentrism** is the astronomical [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_modeling "Scientific modeling") developed by [Nicolaus Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus "Nicolaus Copernicus") and published in 1543. This model positioned the Sun near the center of the Universe, motionless, with Earth and the other planets orbiting around it in circular motions, modified by [epicycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicycle "Epicycle"), and at uniform speeds. The Copernican model challenged the dominant [geocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism#Ptolemaic_model "Geocentrism") of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy"), which had placed Earth at the center of the Universe. 16th-century astronomers believed that Copernicus' elimination of the [equant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equant "Equant") was his chief achievement but his model never displaced Ptolemy's, which only fell out of favor 70 years later after [Galileo's telescopic observations of 1610](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei#Phases_of_Venus "Galileo Galilei").

## Scientific Revolution and birth of New Science

The [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution") of the 16th and 17th centuries in Europe marked a sharp break with the [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") that had preceded it. The New Science that emerged departed from previous Greek conceptions and traditions, was more mechanistic in its worldview and more integrated with mathematics, and was obsessed with the acquisition and interpretation of new evidence. The Scientific Revolution is a convenient boundary between ancient thought and modern science. While the [period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodization "Periodization") is frequently said to have begun in 1543 with the printings of *[De humani corporis fabrica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_humani_corporis_fabrica "De humani corporis fabrica")* (*On the Workings of the Human Body*) by [Andreas Vesalius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Vesalius "Andreas Vesalius") and *[De Revolutionibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Revolutionibus_Orbium_Coelestium "De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium")* (*On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres*) by [Nicolaus Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus "Nicolaus Copernicus"), the [SN 1572](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN_1572 "SN 1572") supernova has also been suggested as its beginning. The period culminated with the publication of the *[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica "Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica")* in 1687 by [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), representative of the unprecedented growth of [scientific publications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquarian_science_book "Antiquarian science book") throughout Europe.

### Modern astronomy

[Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_Brahe "Tycho Brahe")'s unprecedentedly accurate [astronomical observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_observation "Astronomical observation") in the late 16th century and [Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei")'s [early 17th-century telescopic observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei#Phases_of_Venus "Galileo Galilei") combined to turn astronomy into the first modern science. Galileo's observations ended a [millennium of pre-modern astronomical orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism#Ptolemaic_model "Geocentrism") while [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler "Johannes Kepler") used Brahe's data to discover that planets have [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_laws_of_planetary_motion#First_law "Kepler's laws of planetary motion"), not circular, orbits and develop the [laws of planetary motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_laws_of_planetary_motion "Kepler's laws of planetary motion"). Because of Kepler, astronomical phenomena came to be seen as being governed by physical laws.

### Calculus and Newtonian mechanics

In 1687, Isaac Newton published the *[Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica "Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica")*, detailing two comprehensive and successful physical theories: [Newton's laws of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion "Newton's laws of motion"), which led to classical mechanics; and [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_law_of_universal_gravitation "Newton's law of universal gravitation"), which describes the fundamental force of gravity.

### Emergence of chemistry

A decisive moment came when "chemistry" was distinguished from [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy "Alchemy") by [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle") in his work *[The Sceptical Chymist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sceptical_Chymist "The Sceptical Chymist")*, in 1661; although the alchemical tradition continued for some time after his work. Other important steps included the gravimetric experimental practices of medical chemists like [William Cullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cullen "William Cullen"), [Joseph Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Black "Joseph Black"), [Torbern Bergman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torbern_Bergman "Torbern Bergman") and [Pierre Macquer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Macquer "Pierre Macquer") and through the work of [Antoine Lavoisier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier "Antoine Lavoisier") ("[father of modern chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_considered_father_or_mother_of_a_scientific_field "List of people considered father or mother of a scientific field")") on [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen") and the law of [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass "Conservation of mass"), which refuted [phlogiston theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlogiston_theory "Phlogiston theory"). Modern chemistry emerged from the sixteenth through the eighteenth centuries through the material practices and theories promoted by alchemy, medicine, manufacturing and mining.

### Circulatory system

[William Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harvey "William Harvey") published *[De Motu Cordis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercitatio_Anatomica_de_Motu_Cordis_et_Sanguinis_in_Animalibus "Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus")* in 1628, which revealed his conclusions based on his extensive studies of [vertebrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate "Vertebrate") [circulatory systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system "Circulatory system"). He identified the central role of the [heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart "Heart"), [arteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artery "Artery"), and [veins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vein "Vein") in producing blood movement in a circuit, and failed to find any confirmation of [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen "Galen")'s pre-existing notions of heating and cooling functions. The history of early modern biology and medicine is often told through the search for the seat of the soul. Galen in his descriptions of his foundational work in medicine presents the distinctions between arteries, veins, and nerves using the vocabulary of the soul.

### Scientific societies and journals

A critical innovation was the creation of permanent scientific societies and their scholarly journals, which dramatically sped the diffusion of new ideas. Typical was the founding of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in London in 1660 and its journal in 1665 the [Philosophical Transaction of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions_of_the_Royal_Society "Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society"), the first scientific journal in English. 1665 also saw the first journal in French, the [Journal des *sçavans*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_des_s%C3%A7avans "Journal des sçavans"). Science drawing on the works of [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes "Descartes"), [Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Pascal "Blaise Pascal") and [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz"), science was on a path to modern [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology "Technology") by the time of the generation of [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin "Benjamin Franklin") (1706–1790), [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler") (1707–1783), [Mikhail Lomonosov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Lomonosov "Mikhail Lomonosov") (1711–1765) and [Jean le Rond d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d%27Alembert "Jean le Rond d'Alembert") (1717–1783). [Denis Diderot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Diderot "Denis Diderot")'s *[Encyclopédie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die "Encyclopédie")*, published between 1751 and 1772 brought this new understanding to a wider audience. The impact of this process was not limited to science and technology, but affected [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy "History of philosophy") ([Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant"), [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume "David Hume")), [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_religion "History of religion") (the increasingly significant impact of [science upon religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relationship_between_religion_and_science "Relationship between religion and science")), and society and politics in general ([Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith "Adam Smith"), [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire "Voltaire")).

### Developments in geology

Geology did not undergo systematic restructuring during the [Scientific Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution "Scientific Revolution") but instead existed as a cloud of isolated, disconnected ideas about rocks, minerals, and landforms long before it became a coherent science. [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke "Robert Hooke") formulated a theory of earthquakes, and [Nicholas Steno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Steno "Nicholas Steno") developed the theory of [superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_superposition "Law of superposition") and argued that [fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossils "Fossils") were the remains of once-living creatures. Beginning with [Thomas Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Burnet_(theologian) "Thomas Burnet (theologian)")'s *Sacred Theory of the Earth* in 1681, natural philosophers began to explore the idea that the Earth had changed over time. Burnet and his contemporaries interpreted Earth's past in terms of events described in the Bible, but their work laid the intellectual foundations for secular interpretations of Earth history.

## Romanticism and Post-Scientific Revolution

### Bioelectricity

During the late 18th century, researchers such as [Hugh Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Williamson "Hugh Williamson") and [John Walsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Walsh_(British_scientist) "John Walsh (British scientist)") experimented on the effects of electricity on the human body. Further studies by [Luigi Galvani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Galvani "Luigi Galvani") and [Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta "Alessandro Volta") established the electrical nature of what Volta called [galvanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanism "Galvanism").

Electricity thus became in [Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") a multifaceted symbol representing both revolutionary fervor and the creative force of nature, as well as a metaphor for the pervasive power of the [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind "Mind") and its spiritual connection. Its presence, both literal and figurative, in both scientific experiments and literature, such as Galvani's study of electrical effects on bodies, fueled the Romantic imagination, serving as a [vital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vital_force "Vital force") concept bridging the animate and the inanimate, the rational and the spiritual.

### Developments in geology

Modern geology, like modern chemistry, gradually evolved during the 18th and early 19th centuries. [Benoît de Maillet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beno%C3%AEt_de_Maillet "Benoît de Maillet") and the [Comte de Buffon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges-Louis_Leclerc,_Comte_de_Buffon "Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon") saw the Earth as much older than the 6,000 years envisioned by biblical scholars. [Jean-Étienne Guettard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-%C3%89tienne_Guettard "Jean-Étienne Guettard") and [Nicolas Desmarest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Desmarest "Nicolas Desmarest") hiked central France and recorded their observations on some of the first geological maps. Aided by chemical experimentation, naturalists such as Scotland's [John Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Walker_(natural_historian) "John Walker (natural historian)"), Sweden's Torbern Bergman, and Germany's [Abraham Werner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Werner "Abraham Werner") created comprehensive classification systems for rocks and minerals—a collective achievement that transformed geology into a cutting edge field by the end of the eighteenth century. These early geologists also proposed a generalized interpretations of Earth history that led [James Hutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hutton "James Hutton"), [Georges Cuvier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Cuvier "Georges Cuvier") and [Alexandre Brongniart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Brongniart "Alexandre Brongniart"), following in the steps of [Steno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Steno "Nicolas Steno"), to argue that layers of rock could be dated by the fossils they contained: a principle first applied to the geology of the Paris Basin. The use of [index fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_fossil "Index fossil") became a powerful tool for making geological maps, because it allowed geologists to correlate the rocks in one locality with those of similar age in other, distant localities.

### Birth of modern economics

The basis for [classical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_economics "Classical economics") forms [Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith "Adam Smith")'s *[An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations "The Wealth of Nations")*, published in 1776. Smith criticized [mercantilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercantilism "Mercantilism"), advocating a system of free trade with [division of labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labour "Division of labour"). He postulated an "[invisible hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invisible_hand "Invisible hand")" that regulated economic systems made up of actors guided only by self-interest. The "invisible hand" mentioned in a lost page in the middle of a chapter in the middle of the "[Wealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wealth_of_Nations "Wealth of Nations")", 1776, advances as Smith's central message.

### Social science

Anthropology can best be understood as an outgrowth of the Age of Enlightenment. It was during this period that Europeans attempted systematically to study human behavior. Traditions of jurisprudence, history, philology and sociology developed during this time and informed the development of the social sciences of which anthropology was a part.

## 19th century

The 19th century saw the birth of science as a profession. [William Whewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Whewell "William Whewell") had coined the term *scientist* in 1833, which soon replaced the older term *natural philosopher*.

### Developments in physics

In physics, the behavior of electricity and magnetism was studied by [Giovanni Aldini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Aldini "Giovanni Aldini"), [Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta "Alessandro Volta"), [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday "Michael Faraday"), [Georg Ohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Ohm "Georg Ohm"), and others. The experiments, theories and discoveries of [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday "Michael Faraday"), [André-Marie Ampère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9-Marie_Amp%C3%A8re "André-Marie Ampère"), [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell"), and their contemporaries led to the unification of the two phenomena into a single theory of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism "Electromagnetism") as described by [Maxwell's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_equations "Maxwell's equations"). [Thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") led to an understanding of heat and the notion of energy being defined.

### Discovery of Neptune

In astronomy, the planet Neptune was discovered. Advances in astronomy and in optical systems in the 19th century resulted in the first observation of an [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid") ([1 Ceres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceres_(dwarf_planet) "Ceres (dwarf planet)")) in 1801, and the discovery of [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune "Neptune") in 1846.

### Developments in mathematics

In mathematics, the notion of complex numbers finally matured and led to a subsequent analytical theory; they also began the use of [hypercomplex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_number "Hypercomplex number"). [Karl Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Weierstrass "Karl Weierstrass") and others carried out the [arithmetization of analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetization_of_analysis "Arithmetization of analysis") for functions of [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_of_a_real_variable "Function of a real variable") and [complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable "Complex variable"). It also saw rise to [new progress in geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry "Non-Euclidean geometry") beyond those classical theories of Euclid, after a period of nearly two thousand years. The mathematical science of logic likewise had revolutionary breakthroughs after a similarly long period of stagnation. But the most important step in science at this time were the ideas formulated by the creators of electrical science. Their work changed the face of physics and made possible for new technology to come about such as electric power, electrical telegraphy, the telephone, and radio.

### Developments in chemistry

In chemistry, [Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev "Dmitri Mendeleev"), following the [atomic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory "Atomic theory") of [John Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton "John Dalton"), created the first [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table") of [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element"). Other highlights include the discoveries unveiling the nature of atomic structure and matter, simultaneously with chemistry – and of new kinds of radiation. The theory that all matter is made of atoms, which are the smallest constituents of matter that cannot be broken down without losing the basic chemical and physical properties of that matter, was provided by [John Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton "John Dalton") in 1803, although the question took a hundred years to settle as proven. Dalton also formulated the law of mass relationships. In 1869, [Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev "Dmitri Mendeleev") composed his [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table") of elements on the basis of Dalton's discoveries. The synthesis of [urea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urea "Urea") by [Friedrich Wöhler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_W%C3%B6hler "Friedrich Wöhler") opened a new research field, [organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry"), and by the end of the 19th century, scientists were able to synthesize hundreds of organic compounds. The later part of the 19th century saw the exploitation of the Earth's petrochemicals, after the exhaustion of the oil supply from [whaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaling "Whaling"). By the 20th century, systematic production of refined materials provided a ready supply of products which provided not only energy, but also synthetic materials for clothing, medicine, and everyday disposable resources. Application of the techniques of organic chemistry to living organisms resulted in [physiological chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiological_chemistry "Physiological chemistry"), the precursor to [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry").

### Age of the Earth

Over the first half of the 19th century, geologists such as [Charles Lyell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Lyell "Charles Lyell"), [Adam Sedgwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Sedgwick "Adam Sedgwick"), and [Roderick Murchison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roderick_Murchison "Roderick Murchison") applied the new technique to rocks throughout Europe and eastern North America, setting the stage for more detailed, government-funded mapping projects in later decades. Midway through the 19th century, the focus of geology shifted from description and classification to attempts to understand *how* the surface of the Earth had changed. The first comprehensive theories of mountain building were proposed during this period, as were the first modern theories of earthquakes and volcanoes. [Louis Agassiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Agassiz "Louis Agassiz") and others established the reality of continent-covering [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age "Ice age"), and "fluvialists" like [Andrew Crombie Ramsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Crombie_Ramsay "Andrew Crombie Ramsay") argued that river valleys were formed, over millions of years by the rivers that flow through them. After the discovery of [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity "Radioactivity"), [radiometric dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometric_dating "Radiometric dating") methods were developed, starting in the 20th century. [Alfred Wegener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener "Alfred Wegener")'s theory of "continental drift" was widely dismissed when he proposed it in the 1910s, but new data gathered in the 1950s and 1960s led to the theory of [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics"), which provided a plausible mechanism for it. Plate tectonics also provided a unified explanation for a wide range of seemingly unrelated geological phenomena. Since the 1960s it has served as the unifying principle in geology.

### Evolution and inheritance

Perhaps the most prominent, controversial, and far-reaching theory in all of science has been the theory of [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution") by [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection"), which was independently formulated by [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin "Charles Darwin") and [Alfred Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Russel_Wallace "Alfred Russel Wallace"). It was described in detail in Darwin's book *[The Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_Species "The Origin of Species")*, which was published in 1859. In it, Darwin proposed that the features of all living things, including humans, were shaped by natural processes over long periods of time. The theory of evolution in its current form affects almost all areas of biology. Implications of evolution on fields outside of pure science have led to both [opposition and support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_effect_of_evolutionary_theory "Social effect of evolutionary theory") from different parts of society, and profoundly influenced the popular understanding of "man's place in the universe". Separately, [Gregor Mendel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel "Gregor Mendel") formulated the principles of inheritance in 1866, which became the basis of modern [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics").

### Germ theory

Another important landmark in medicine and biology were the successful efforts to prove the [germ theory of disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory_of_disease "Germ theory of disease"). Following this, [Louis Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pasteur "Louis Pasteur") made the first [vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine "Vaccine") against [rabies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabies "Rabies"), and also made many discoveries in the field of chemistry, including the [asymmetry of crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_isomerism "Optical isomerism"). In 1847, Hungarian physician [Ignác Fülöp Semmelweis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis "Ignaz Semmelweis") dramatically reduced the occurrence of [puerperal fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerperal_fever "Puerperal fever") by simply requiring physicians to wash their hands before attending to women in childbirth. This discovery predated the [germ theory of disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory_of_disease "Germ theory of disease"). However, Semmelweis' findings were not appreciated by his contemporaries and handwashing came into use only with discoveries by British surgeon [Joseph Lister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lister,_1st_Baron_Lister "Joseph Lister, 1st Baron Lister"), who in 1865 proved the principles of [antisepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisepsis "Antisepsis"). Lister's work was based on the important findings by French biologist [Louis Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pasteur "Louis Pasteur"). Pasteur was able to link microorganisms with disease, revolutionizing medicine. He also devised one of the most important methods in [preventive medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_medicine "Preventive medicine"), when in 1880 he produced a [vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine "Vaccine") against [rabies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabies "Rabies"). Pasteur invented the process of [pasteurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasteurization "Pasteurization"), to help prevent the spread of disease through milk and other foods.

### Schools of economics

[Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx") developed an alternative economic theory, called [Marxian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxian_economics "Marxian economics"). Marxian economics is based on the [labor theory of value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_theory_of_value "Labor theory of value") and assumes the value of good to be based on the amount of labor required to produce it. Under this axiom, [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism "Capitalism") was based on employers not paying the full value of workers labor to create profit. The [Austrian School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_School "Austrian School") responded to Marxian economics by viewing [entrepreneurship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrepreneurship "Entrepreneurship") as driving force of economic development. This replaced the labor theory of value by a system of [supply and demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_and_demand "Supply and demand").

### Founding of psychology

Psychology as a scientific enterprise that was independent from philosophy began in 1879 when [Wilhelm Wundt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Wundt "Wilhelm Wundt") founded the first laboratory dedicated exclusively to psychological research (in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig "Leipzig")). Other important early contributors to the field include [Hermann Ebbinghaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Ebbinghaus "Hermann Ebbinghaus") (a pioneer in memory studies), [Ivan Pavlov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Pavlov "Ivan Pavlov") (who discovered [classical conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_conditioning "Classical conditioning")), [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James "William James"), and [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud"). Freud's influence has been enormous, though more as cultural icon than a force in scientific psychology.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Modern sociology

Modern sociology emerged in the early 19th century as the academic response to the modernization of the world. Among many early sociologists (e.g., [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim")), the aim of sociology was in [structuralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_functionalism "Structural functionalism"), understanding the cohesion of social groups, and developing an "antidote" to social disintegration. [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber") was concerned with the modernization of society through the concept of [rationalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalization_(sociology) "Rationalization (sociology)"), which he believed would trap individuals in an "iron cage" of rational thought. Some sociologists, including [Georg Simmel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Simmel "Georg Simmel") and [W. E. B. Du Bois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._E._B._Du_Bois "W. E. B. Du Bois"), used more [microsociological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsociology "Microsociology"), qualitative analyses. This microlevel approach played an important role in American sociology, with the theories of [George Herbert Mead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Herbert_Mead "George Herbert Mead") and his student [Herbert Blumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Blumer "Herbert Blumer") resulting in the creation of the [symbolic interactionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism "Symbolic interactionism") approach to sociology. In particular, just [Auguste Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte "Auguste Comte"), illustrated with his work the transition from a theological to a metaphysical stage and, from this, to a positive stage. Comte took care of the classification of the sciences as well as a transit of humanity towards a situation of progress attributable to a re-examination of nature according to the affirmation of 'sociality' as the basis of the scientifically interpreted society.

### Romanticism

The [Romantic Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism_in_science "Romanticism in science") of the early 19th century reshaped science by opening up new pursuits unexpected in the classical approaches of the Enlightenment. The decline of Romanticism occurred because a new movement, [Positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivism "Positivism"), began to take hold of the ideals of the intellectuals after 1840 and lasted until about 1880. At the same time, the romantic reaction to the Enlightenment produced thinkers such as [Johann Gottfried Herder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Gottfried_Herder "Johann Gottfried Herder") and later [Wilhelm Dilthey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Dilthey "Wilhelm Dilthey") whose work formed the basis for the [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture "Culture") concept which is central to the discipline. Traditionally, much of the history of the subject was based on [colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonialism "Colonialism") encounters between Western Europe and the rest of the world, and much of 18th- and 19th-century anthropology is now classed as [scientific racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism "Scientific racism"). During the late 19th century, battles over the "study of man" took place between those of an "anthropological" persuasion (relying on [anthropometrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropometry "Anthropometry") techniques) and those of an "[ethnological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnology "Ethnology")" persuasion (looking at cultures and traditions), and these distinctions became part of the later divide between [physical anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_anthropology "Physical anthropology") and [cultural anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_anthropology "Cultural anthropology"), the latter ushered in by the students of [Franz Boas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas "Franz Boas").

## 20th century

Science advanced dramatically during the 20th century. There were new and radical developments in the [physical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_science "Physical science") and [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_sciences "Life sciences") sciences, building on the progress from the 19th century.

### Theory of relativity and quantum mechanics

The beginning of the 20th century brought the start of a revolution in physics. The long-held theories of Newton were shown not to be correct in all circumstances. Beginning in 1900, [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck"), [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr") and others developed quantum theories to explain various anomalous experimental results, by introducing discrete energy levels. Not only did [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") show that the laws of motion did not hold on small scales, but the theory of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"), proposed by Einstein in 1915, showed that the fixed background of [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime "Spacetime"), on which both [Newtonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics "Newtonian mechanics") and [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity") depended, could not exist. In 1925, [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") and [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schr%C3%B6dinger "Erwin Schrödinger") formulated [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), which explained the preceding quantum theories. Currently, general relativity and quantum mechanics are inconsistent with each other, and efforts are underway to unify the two.

### Big Bang

The observation by [Edwin Hubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble "Edwin Hubble") in 1929 that the speed at which galaxies recede positively correlates with their distance, led to the understanding that the universe is expanding, and the formulation of the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang") theory by [Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lema%C3%AEtre "Georges Lemaître"). [George Gamow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gamow "George Gamow"), [Ralph Alpher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Alpher "Ralph Alpher"), and [Robert Herman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Herman "Robert Herman") had calculated that there should be evidence for a [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang") in the background temperature of the universe. In 1964, [Arno Penzias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arno_Penzias "Arno Penzias") and [Robert Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Woodrow_Wilson "Robert Woodrow Wilson") discovered a 3 Kelvin background hiss in their [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs") [radiotelescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiotelescope "Radiotelescope") (the [Holmdel Horn Antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holmdel_Horn_Antenna "Holmdel Horn Antenna")), which was evidence for this hypothesis, and formed the basis for a number of results that helped determine the [age of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_universe "Age of the universe").

### Big science

In 1938 [Otto Hahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Hahn "Otto Hahn") and [Fritz Strassmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Strassmann "Fritz Strassmann") [discovered nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission "Discovery of nuclear fission") with radiochemical methods, and in 1939 [Lise Meitner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner "Lise Meitner") and [Otto Robert Frisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Robert_Frisch "Otto Robert Frisch") wrote the first theoretical interpretation of the fission process, which was later improved by [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr") and [John A. Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_A._Wheeler "John A. Wheeler"). Further developments took place during World War II, which led to the practical application of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar") and the development and use of the [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb"). Around this time, [Chien-Shiung Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu "Chien-Shiung Wu") was recruited by the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project") to help develop a process for separating uranium metal into U-235 and U-238 isotopes by [Gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion "Gaseous diffusion"). She was an expert experimentalist in beta decay and weak interaction physics. Wu designed an experiment (see [Wu experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_experiment "Wu experiment")) that enabled theoretical physicists [Tsung-Dao Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsung-Dao_Lee "Tsung-Dao Lee") and [Chen-Ning Yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen-Ning_Yang "Chen-Ning Yang") to disprove the law of parity experimentally, winning them a Nobel Prize in 1957.

Though the process had begun with the invention of the [cyclotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotron "Cyclotron") by [Ernest O. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_O._Lawrence "Ernest O. Lawrence") in the 1930s, physics in the postwar period entered into a phase of what historians have called "[Big Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Science "Big Science")", requiring massive machines, budgets, and laboratories in order to test their theories and move into new frontiers. The primary patron of physics became state governments, who recognized that the support of "basic" research could often lead to technologies useful to both military and industrial applications.

### Advances in genetics

In the early 20th century, the study of heredity became a major investigation after the rediscovery in 1900 of the laws of inheritance developed by [Mendel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel "Gregor Mendel"). The 20th century also saw the integration of physics and chemistry, with chemical properties explained as the result of the electronic structure of the atom. [Linus Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling "Linus Pauling")'s book on *The Nature of the Chemical Bond* used the principles of quantum mechanics to deduce [bond angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_angle "Bond angle") in ever-more complicated molecules. Pauling's work culminated in the physical modelling of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA"), *the secret of life* (in the words of [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick"), 1953). In the same year, the [Miller–Urey experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller%E2%80%93Urey_experiment "Miller–Urey experiment") demonstrated in a simulation of primordial processes, that basic constituents of proteins, simple [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid"), could themselves be built up from simpler molecules, kickstarting decades of research into the [chemical origins of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiogenesis "Abiogenesis"). By 1953, [James D. Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson "James D. Watson") and [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") clarified the basic structure of DNA, the [genetic material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_material "Genetic material") for expressing life in all its forms, building on the work of [Maurice Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkins "Maurice Wilkins") and [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin "Rosalind Franklin"), suggested that the structure of DNA was a double helix. In their famous paper "[Molecular structure of Nucleic Acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_structure_of_Nucleic_Acids "Molecular structure of Nucleic Acids")" In the late 20th century, the possibilities of [genetic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_engineering "Genetic engineering") became practical for the first time, and a massive international effort began in 1990 to map out an entire human [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome "Genome") (the [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project "Human Genome Project")). The discipline of [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology "Ecology") typically traces its origin to the synthesis of [Darwinian evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution") and [Humboldtian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldtian_science "Humboldtian science") [biogeography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogeography "Biogeography"), in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Equally important in the rise of ecology, however, were [microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbiology "Microbiology") and [soil science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_science "Soil science")—particularly the [cycle of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogeochemical_cycle "Biogeochemical cycle") concept, prominent in the work of [Louis Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pasteur "Louis Pasteur") and [Ferdinand Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Cohn "Ferdinand Cohn"). The word *ecology* was coined by [Ernst Haeckel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Haeckel "Ernst Haeckel"), whose particularly holistic view of nature in general (and Darwin's theory in particular) was important in the spread of ecological thinking. The field of [ecosystem ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_ecology "Ecosystem ecology") emerged in the Atomic Age with the use of radioisotopes to visualize food webs and by the 1970s ecosystem ecology deeply influenced global environmental management.

### Space exploration

In 1925, [Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin "Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin") determined that stars were composed mostly of hydrogen and helium. She was dissuaded by astronomer [Henry Norris Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Norris_Russell "Henry Norris Russell") from publishing this finding in her PhD thesis because of the widely held belief that stars had the same composition as the Earth. However, four years later, in 1929, [Henry Norris Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Norris_Russell "Henry Norris Russell") came to the same conclusion through different reasoning and the discovery was eventually accepted.

In 1987, supernova [SN 1987A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN_1987A "SN 1987A") was observed by astronomers on Earth both visually, and in a triumph for [neutrino astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_astronomy "Neutrino astronomy"), by the solar neutrino detectors at [Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamiokande "Kamiokande"). But the solar neutrino flux was [a fraction of its theoretically expected value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_neutrino_problem "Solar neutrino problem"). This discrepancy forced a change in some values in the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") for [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics").

### Neuroscience as a distinct discipline

The understanding of neurons and the nervous system became increasingly precise and molecular during the 20th century. For example, in 1952, [Alan Lloyd Hodgkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Lloyd_Hodgkin "Alan Lloyd Hodgkin") and [Andrew Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Huxley "Andrew Huxley") presented a mathematical model for transmission of electrical signals in neurons of the giant axon of a squid, which they called "[action potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_potentials "Action potentials")", and how they are initiated and propagated, known as the [Hodgkin–Huxley model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodgkin%E2%80%93Huxley_model "Hodgkin–Huxley model"). In 1961–1962, Richard FitzHugh and J. Nagumo simplified Hodgkin–Huxley, in what is called the [FitzHugh–Nagumo model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FitzHugh%E2%80%93Nagumo_model "FitzHugh–Nagumo model"). In 1962, [Bernard Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Katz "Bernard Katz") modeled [neurotransmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotransmission "Neurotransmission") across the space between neurons known as [synapses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapses "Synapses"). Beginning in 1966, Eric Kandel and collaborators examined biochemical changes in neurons associated with learning and memory storage in *[Aplysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aplysia "Aplysia")*. In 1981 Catherine Morris and Harold Lecar combined these models in the [Morris–Lecar model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris%E2%80%93Lecar_model "Morris–Lecar model"). Such increasingly quantitative work gave rise to numerous [biological neuron models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_neuron_model "Biological neuron model") and [models of neural computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Models_of_neural_computation "Models of neural computation"). [Neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience") began to be recognized as a distinct academic discipline in its own right. [Eric Kandel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Kandel "Eric Kandel") and collaborators have cited [David Rioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rioch "David Rioch"), [Francis O. Schmitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_O._Schmitt "Francis O. Schmitt"), and [Stephen Kuffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Kuffler "Stephen Kuffler") as having played critical roles in establishing the field.

### Plate tectonics

Geologists' embrace of [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics") became part of a broadening of the field from a study of rocks into a study of the Earth as a planet. Other elements of this transformation include: [geophysical studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics "Geophysics") of the interior of the Earth, the grouping of geology with [meteorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology "Meteorology") and [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography "Oceanography") as one of the "[earth sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_science "Earth science")", and comparisons of Earth and the solar system's other rocky planets.

### Applications

In terms of applications, a massive number of new technologies were developed in the 20th century. Technologies such as [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity "Electricity"), the [incandescent light bulb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescent_light_bulb "Incandescent light bulb"), the [automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile "Automobile") and the [phonograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph "Phonograph"), first developed at the end of the 19th century, were perfected and universally deployed. The first car was introduced by Karl Benz in 1885. The first [airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplane "Airplane") flight occurred in 1903, and by the end of the century [airliners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airliner "Airliner") flew thousands of miles in a matter of hours. The development of the [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio "Radio"), [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television "Television") and [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computers "Computers") caused massive changes in the dissemination of information. Advances in biology also led to large increases in food production, as well as the elimination of diseases such as [polio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polio "Polio") by [Dr. Jonas Salk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonas_Salk "Jonas Salk"). Gene mapping and gene sequencing, invented by Drs. Mark Skolnik and Walter Gilbert, respectively, are the two technologies that made the [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project "Human Genome Project") feasible. Computer science, built upon a foundation of [theoretical linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_linguistics "Theoretical linguistics"), [discrete mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics "Discrete mathematics"), and [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering"), studies the nature and limits of computation. Subfields include [computability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability_theory_(computer_science) "Computability theory (computer science)"), [computational complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory "Computational complexity theory"), [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database "Database") design, [computer networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_networking "Computer networking"), artificial intelligence, and the design of [computer hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware "Computer hardware"). One area in which advances in computing have contributed to more general scientific development is by facilitating large-scale [archiving of scientific data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_data_archiving "Scientific data archiving"). Contemporary computer science typically distinguishes itself by emphasizing mathematical 'theory' in contrast to the practical emphasis of [software engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_engineering "Software engineering").

Einstein's paper "On the Quantum Theory of Radiation" outlined the principles of the stimulated emission of photons. This led to the invention of the [Laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser "Laser") (light amplification by the stimulated emission of radiation) and the [optical amplifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_amplifier "Optical amplifier") which ushered in the [Information Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Age "Information Age"). It is optical amplification that allows [fiber optic networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber-optic_network "Fiber-optic network") to transmit the massive capacity of the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet "Internet").

Based on wireless transmission of electromagnetic radiation and global networks of cellular operation, the mobile phone became a primary means to access the internet.

### Developments in political science and economics

In political science during the 20th century, the study of ideology, behaviouralism and international relations led to a multitude of 'pol-sci' subdisciplines including [rational choice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_choice_theory "Rational choice theory"), [voting theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_theory "Voting theory"), [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory "Game theory") (also used in economics), [psephology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psephology "Psephology"), [political geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_geography "Political geography")/[geopolitics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopolitics "Geopolitics"), [political anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_anthropology "Political anthropology")/[political psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_psychology "Political psychology")/[political sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_sociology "Political sociology"), political economy, [policy analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_analysis "Policy analysis"), public administration, comparative political analysis and [peace studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_studies "Peace studies")/conflict analysis. In economics, [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes "John Maynard Keynes") prompted a division between [microeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microeconomics "Microeconomics") and [macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomics "Macroeconomics") in the 1920s. Under [Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics "Keynesian economics") macroeconomic trends can overwhelm economic choices made by individuals. Governments should promote [aggregate demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregate_demand "Aggregate demand") for goods as a means to encourage economic expansion. Following World War II, [Milton Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman "Milton Friedman") created the concept of [monetarism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetarism "Monetarism"). Monetarism focuses on using the supply and demand of money as a method for controlling economic activity. In the 1970s, monetarism has adapted into [supply-side economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply-side_economics "Supply-side economics") which advocates reducing taxes as a means to increase the amount of money available for economic expansion. Other modern schools of economic thought are [New Classical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Classical_economics "New Classical economics") and [New Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Keynesian_economics "New Keynesian economics"). New Classical economics was developed in the 1970s, emphasizing solid microeconomics as the basis for macroeconomic growth. New Keynesian economics was created partially in response to New Classical economics. It shows how imperfect competition and market rigidities, means monetary policy has real effects, and enables analysis of different policies.

### Developments in psychology, sociology, and anthropology

Psychology in the 20th century saw a rejection of Freud's theories as being too unscientific, and a reaction against [Edward Titchener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Titchener "Edward Titchener")'s atomistic approach of the mind. This led to the formulation of [behaviorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behaviorism "Behaviorism") by [John B. Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Watson "John B. Watson"), which was popularized by [B.F. Skinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.F._Skinner "B.F. Skinner"). Behaviorism proposed [epistemologically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology") limiting psychological study to overt behavior, since that could be reliably measured. Scientific knowledge of the "mind" was considered too metaphysical, hence impossible to achieve. The final decades of the 20th century have seen the rise of [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science "Cognitive science"), which considers the mind as once again a subject for investigation, using the tools of psychology, [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics"), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science "Computer science"), philosophy, and [neurobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurobiology "Neurobiology"). New methods of visualizing the activity of the brain, such as [PET scans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PET_scan "PET scan") and [CAT scans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAT_scan "CAT scan"), began to exert their influence as well, leading some researchers to investigate the mind by investigating the brain, rather than cognition. These new forms of investigation assume that a wide understanding of the human mind is possible, and that such an understanding may be applied to other research domains, such as [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"). Evolutionary theory was applied to behavior and introduced to anthropology and psychology, through the works of [cultural anthropologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_anthropologist "Cultural anthropologist") [Napoleon Chagnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_Chagnon "Napoleon Chagnon"). Physical anthropology would become [biological anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_anthropology "Biological anthropology"), incorporating elements of evolutionary biology.

American sociology in the 1940s and 1950s was dominated largely by [Talcott Parsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talcott_Parsons "Talcott Parsons"), who argued that aspects of society that promoted structural integration were therefore "functional". This structural functionalism approach was questioned in the 1960s, when sociologists came to see this approach as merely a justification for inequalities present in the status quo. In reaction, [conflict theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_theory "Conflict theory") was developed, which was based in part on the philosophies of Karl Marx. Conflict theorists saw society as an arena in which different groups compete for control over resources. Symbolic interactionism also came to be regarded as central to sociological thinking. [Erving Goffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erving_Goffman "Erving Goffman") saw social interactions as a stage performance, with individuals preparing "backstage" and attempting to control their audience through [impression management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_management "Impression management"). While these theories are currently prominent in sociological thought, other approaches exist, including [feminist theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist_theory "Feminist theory"), [post-structuralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-structuralism "Post-structuralism"), rational choice theory, and [postmodernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernism "Postmodernism").

In the mid-20th century, much of the methodologies of earlier anthropological and ethnographical study were reevaluated with an eye towards research ethics, while at the same time the scope of investigation has broadened far beyond the traditional study of "primitive cultures".

## 21st century

In the early 21st century, some concepts that originated in 20th century physics were proven. On 4 July 2012, physicists working at CERN's [Large Hadron Collider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Hadron_Collider "Large Hadron Collider") announced that they had discovered a new subatomic particle greatly resembling the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson "Higgs boson"), confirmed as such by the following March. [Gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave") were [first detected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves "First observation of gravitational waves") by astronomers at the [Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO "LIGO") on 14 September 2015. One of the first direct images of a [black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole "Black hole"), taken by the [Event Horizon Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_Horizon_Telescope "Event Horizon Telescope") in April 2017, was released to the public on April 10, 2019.

The Human Genome Project was declared complete in 2003. The [CRISPR gene editing technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing "CRISPR gene editing") developed in 2012 allowed scientists to precisely and easily modify DNA in living organisms and led to the development of new medicine. Advances in [synthetic biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_biology "Synthetic biology") with computer assistance led to the creation of artificial microbial life, such as [JCVI-syn3.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycoplasma_laboratorium "Mycoplasma laboratorium") in 2016 and [xenobots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenobot "Xenobot") in 2020.

[Positive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_psychology "Positive psychology") is a branch of psychology founded in 1998 by [Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Seligman "Martin Seligman") that is concerned with the study of happiness, mental well-being, and positive human functioning, and is a reaction to 20th century psychology's emphasis on mental illness and dysfunction. Starting around 2011, a [replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis "Replication crisis") affected some branches of the social sciences.
