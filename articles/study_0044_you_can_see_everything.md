# You Can See Everything

***You Can See Everything*** is a 2026 American [documentary film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documentary_film "Documentary film") directed by [Nathan Fielder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Fielder "Nathan Fielder") and [Lance Oppenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Oppenheim "Lance Oppenheim"). It follows [Elizabeth Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Holmes "Elizabeth Holmes"), the founder of the defunct health technology company [Theranos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theranos "Theranos"), and her partner Billy Evans, who invite a film crew to document them beginning in the weeks before Holmes began serving a [United States federal prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_federal_prisons "List of United States federal prisons") sentence for [defrauding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraud "Fraud") investors.

The film premiered as a secret screening at the [53rd Telluride Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53rd_Telluride_Film_Festival "53rd Telluride Film Festival") on September 6, 2026, and was theatrically released by [A24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A24 "A24") on October 16. It received widespread praise from critics.

## Synopsis

In 2023, thirty-four days before [Elizabeth Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Holmes "Elizabeth Holmes") is scheduled to begin an eleven-year prison sentence for defrauding investors of [Theranos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theranos "Theranos"), she and her partner Billy Evans invite filmmakers [Nathan Fielder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Fielder "Nathan Fielder") and [Lance Oppenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Oppenheim "Lance Oppenheim") to document their lives at a rented [San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Diego "San Diego") beach house. Although Fielder is given a great deal of access to their personal lives, even being allowed to film Billy shower, he comes to believe they are not being entirely forthcoming. Elizabeth denies that she intentionally misled anybody about Theranos' supposed blood-testing device, going so far as to say that she has never told a lie in her life; she and Billy continue to make calls to prospective investors about "Theranos 2.0". Billy exhibits controlling behavior towards both Elizabeth and the filmmakers, frequently speaking for his partner and interrupting interviews.

Fielder shows Elizabeth and Billy scenes from *[The Dropout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dropout "The Dropout")*, a miniseries starring [Amanda Seyfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanda_Seyfried "Amanda Seyfried") as Holmes, but Elizabeth becomes distressed and asks to stop watching. Before she reports to prison, Elizabeth has her [body scanned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_body_scanning "3D body scanning"), and expresses distaste for the famous image of her holding a tiny vial of blood in her fingers. Finally, she reports to [Federal Prison Camp, Bryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Prison_Camp,_Bryan "Federal Prison Camp, Bryan") to start her sentence, saying goodbye to Fielder before she does so, with Billy and her two children moving to [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas "Texas") to be closer to her.

Two years later, Fielder meets with Amanda Seyfried, who reprises her performance as Holmes to recreate moments Fielder could not film. He shows her the footage he filmed earlier, then details a meeting with Elizabeth and Billy before they began filming. After Elizabeth left the room, Billy asked for a deal to receive money for the documentary; despite concerns about a [conflict of interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_of_interest "Conflict of interest"), a deal was negotiated. However, Elizabeth later stated to Fielder that she would not receive any money for the documentary. This leads Fielder and Seyfried to believe that Billy is exploiting Elizabeth, although Fielder expresses uncertainty over whether Elizabeth was being truthful.

Fielder's suspicions are further raised by Billy's activities after Elizabeth's imprisonment: among other things, he sets up billboards using the blood vial image Elizabeth disliked, impersonates her on [X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_(social_network) "X (social network)"), and allows an [AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") model of her likeness to be released to the public. Fielder and Seyfried visit Billy, and Seyfried briefly speaks to Elizabeth over the phone. Billy claims that he posts as Elizabeth based on emails she sends him from prison and claims that she knows "some" of the details of the deal he made. Seyfried directly accuses him of exploitation, while Billy defends himself by claiming Elizabeth places absolute trust in him.

Due to the difficulty of communicating over the phone, Fielder and Elizabeth arrange to meet in person, a meeting Fielder details to Seyfried before re-enacting it. Elizabeth expresses her anger towards Seyfried for her portrayal, claiming that she gets accused of things she did not do by other prisoners because they watched *The Dropout*. She says she's aware of Billy's activities after her imprisonment; while she doesn't say she was aware of the deal he made, she says she leaves financial matters up to him due to the hundreds of millions of dollars in [restitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restitution "Restitution") she owes. She tearfully professes her love for Billy, who she greets with what Fielder believes to be genuine affection when he comes to visit her. Ultimately, Fielder and Seyfried are both left uncertain of Elizabeth's true feelings and intentions.

On-screen text at the film’s conclusion states that Billy Evans relinquished his profit-sharing agreement before its release. It further notes that *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* stands by its reporter’s recollection of a remark Elizabeth Holmes made about *The Dropout*: “They’re not playing me. They’re playing a character I created.”

## Production

### Development

Oppenheim and Fielder first came into contact after Oppenheim learned Fielder had seen his 2020 documentary *[Some Kind of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Some_Kind_of_Heaven "Some Kind of Heaven")*; Oppenheim, already a fan of Fielder's, emailed him about it and the two became friends afterward. Meaning to work with one another on a future project, Oppenheim approached Fielder in 2023 about working on a project centered around [Elizabeth Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Holmes "Elizabeth Holmes"), after hearing through a friend that she was looking for someone to document her life. Fielder had read [John Carreyrou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carreyrou "John Carreyrou")'s 2018 book *[Bad Blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Blood:_Secrets_and_Lies_in_a_Silicon_Valley_Startup "Bad Blood: Secrets and Lies in a Silicon Valley Startup")* and thought that the story had already been thoroughly covered in the media, but he became curious about Holmes's perspective following her retreat from the public eye.

Holmes had already reached out to A24 about making a documentary about her prior to Oppenheim and Fielder agreeing to the project. According to *[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_(magazine) "Vanity Fair (magazine)")*, Holmes was "[convinced] of her own innocence" after having been charged and sentenced for defrauding investors in 2022 and was "utterly certain that everyone else will believe her too, if only she has an opportunity to speak her truth".

### Filming

In April 2023, Holmes and her partner Billy Evans invited the film crew to live at home with them and their children during the 34 days prior to Holmes beginning her prison sentence. A24 gave the filmmakers permission to pursue the project without a deadline or obligation to turn the footage into a finished film, with the studio saying that if nothing came of the project it could just be scrapped. According to Fielder, they knew they had a compelling story when crew members disagreed about what was happening in the house, saying: "And that's when we really realized, 'Oh, this is really interesting to follow this. We're all smart and we're really working at the top of our intelligence. Why can't we make sense of what's happening in this house?'"

The original plan was for Fielder to remain behind the camera, but the interactions Fielder had with Holmes and Evans were determined to be so strange that it was decided to include them in the film. According to Fielder, the filmmakers' relationship with Evans had deteriorated by the end of filming; Fielder recounted that "[n]ear the end of filming, Billy [Evans] was trying to exert a level of control over the project that made us uncomfortable, and communication with him sort of broke down because of that".

Fielder and Oppenheim also reached out to several people who knew Holmes or had worked with her, but were unable to get them to participate in the film. Fielder claimed "people did not want to appear in this, or people from the past were just sort of done with her." Actress [Amanda Seyfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanda_Seyfried "Amanda Seyfried"), who previously portrayed Holmes in the miniseries *[The Dropout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dropout "The Dropout")*, appears in the documentary both as herself and in her role as Holmes. Actor [Christopher Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Abbott "Christopher Abbott") portrays Evans in recreations of private conversations with Fielder.

### Music

Ari Balouzian, a frequent collaborator of Oppenheim's, composed the soundtrack for *You Can See Everything*. The soundtrack features 25 tracks which "help [to] build the film's uneasy atmosphere" and borrows from [Bernard Hermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Hermann "Bernard Hermann")'s [score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertigo_(soundtrack) "Vertigo (soundtrack)") from [Alfred Hitchcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Hitchcock "Alfred Hitchcock")'s *[Vertigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertigo_(film) "Vertigo (film)")*. It was released alongside the film on October 16, 2026.

## Release

On September 6, 2026, the film premiered at the [53rd Telluride Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53rd_Telluride_Film_Festival "53rd Telluride Film Festival") as a secret screening. Attendees were unaware of what film they would be seeing and were required to surrender their mobile devices to prevent leaking information; that same day, A24 released the film's teaser trailer. The film was quickly added to the lineup of the [2026 New York Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_New_York_Film_Festival "2026 New York Film Festival") following its premiere at Telluride, screening on September 30. It will close the [2026 Stockholm International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Stockholm_International_Film_Festival "2026 Stockholm International Film Festival") on November 26.

*You Can See Everything* released on October 16 in both the US and Canada. It is scheduled for release in United Kingdom and Ireland on November 20.

## Reception

### Box office

In the United States and Canada, *You Can See Everything* will be released alongside *[Whalefall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whalefall_(film) "Whalefall (film)")*, *[Street Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Fighter_(2026_film) "Street Fighter (2026 film)")*, and *[Sense and Sensibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_and_Sensibility_(2026_film) "Sense and Sensibility (2026 film)")*, and is projected to gross $7–8 million from 800 theaters in its opening weekend.

### Critical response

On [review aggregator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Review_aggregator "Review aggregator") [Rotten Tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes "Rotten Tomatoes"), the film holds an approval rating of 99% based on 101 reviews, with an average rating of 9.3/10. The website’s consensus reads: "Nathan Fielder's ironclad commitment to the bit is pushed to its limit in *You Can See Everything*, an inscrutable Rorschach Test of Elizabeth Holmes' psyche that's as absorbing as it is haunting." [Metacritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacritic "Metacritic"), which uses a [weighted average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_arithmetic_mean "Weighted arithmetic mean"), assigned the film a score of 99 out of 100, based on 37 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".

The film's Telluride premiere drew positive early reviews, highlighting the filmmakers' close access to Holmes and Fielder's deadpan role as an on-camera interviewer.

[Manohla Dargis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manohla_Dargis "Manohla Dargis") of *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* praised the film, writing "few movies speak as uncannily to the pathos and destabilizing confusions of the present moment". Dargis further posited it to be the inverse of [Orson Welles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orson_Welles "Orson Welles")'s 1938 radio broadcast of "[The War of the Worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds_(1938_radio_drama) "The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama)")", suggesting that, in a way similar to how Welles exposed the "'extent of [the] American lunatic fringe'", *You Can See Everything* exposes "the legitimacy conferred on Holmes by her fame and the hundreds of millions of dollars that went into Theranos ... [s]erious people took her seriously".

Writing for *[Variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(magazine) "Variety (magazine)")*, [Owen Gleiberman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Gleiberman "Owen Gleiberman") called the film "a one-of-a-kind reality-TV-as-hangout-movie psychodrama circus" and said that it "overlaps reality, performance, deception, and mental illness until it brings you into the enigmatic space where all those things meet". [Richard Brody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brody "Richard Brody") of *[The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")* noted how Fielder's role in *You Can See Everything* differed heavily from his past performances in programs such as *[Nathan for You](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_for_You "Nathan for You")*, writing that "[n]othing Fielder discovers can top what's already widely known, so his observations inevitably temper [Holmes's] public image. Fielder's portrait of Holmes and his display of his methods turn his questioning to self-questioning, forcing him to assess, and even revise, basic assumptions about her and about life in general ... [f]or all the unpleasantness ... the film is more than fascinating and ingenious; it's strangely, poetically, poignantly beautiful."

Adrian Horton, from *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")*, wrote that the film "was somehow, more shocking, twistier, thornier and revealing than billed", describing it as a "stupefying and gloriously destabilizing hall of psychological mirrors, a thrilling once-in-a-lifetime collision of director and subject." *[The Daily Beast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Beast "The Daily Beast")*'s Nick Schager called *You Can See Everything* "not just the film of the year—it's a documentary that stands shoulder-to-shoulder with the form's all-time greats." Alison Willmore of *[Vulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulture_(website) "Vulture (website)")* concurred in a review titled "Nathan Fielder Has Made the Movie of the Year", calling it "a surreal, agonizingly intimate window ... [into] how we decide whether we really know what’s going on in the head and the heart of another person, where manipulation ends and investment begins".
