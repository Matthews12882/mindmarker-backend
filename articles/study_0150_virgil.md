# Virgil

**Publius Vergilius Maro** ([Classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin_language "Classical Latin language"): [[ˈpuːbliʊs wɛrˈɡɪliʊs ˈmaroː]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin "Help:IPA/Latin"); 15 October 70 BC – 21 September 19 BC), usually called **Virgil** or **Vergil** ([/ˈvɜːrdʒɪl/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*VUR-jil*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key")) in English, was an [ancient Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") poet of the [Augustan period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustan_literature_(ancient_Rome) "Augustan literature (ancient Rome)"). He composed three of the most famous poems in [Latin literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature "Latin literature"): the *[Eclogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogues "Eclogues")* (or *Bucolics*), the *[Georgics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgics "Georgics")*, and the [epic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry "Epic poetry") *[Aeneid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid "Aeneid")*. Some minor poems, collected in the *[Appendix Vergiliana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appendix_Vergiliana "Appendix Vergiliana")*, were attributed to him in ancient times, but modern scholars regard these as spurious, with the possible exception of some short pieces.

Already acclaimed in his lifetime as a classic author, Virgil rapidly replaced [Ennius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ennius "Ennius") and other earlier authors as a standard school text, and stood as the most popular Latin poet through late antiquity, the Middle Ages, and early modernity, exerting major influence on [Western literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_literature "Western literature"). [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer "Geoffrey Chaucer") assigned Virgil a uniquely prominent position in history in *[The House of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_House_of_Fame "The House of Fame")* (1374–85), describing him as standing *on a pilere / that was of tinned yren clere* ("on a pillar that was of bright tin-plated iron"), and in the *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy "Divine Comedy")*, in which Virgil appears as the author's guide through [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell "Hell") and [Purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory "Purgatory"), [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri "Dante Alighieri") pays tribute to Virgil with the words *tu se' solo colui da cu'io tolsi / lo bello stile che m'ha fatto onore* (*Inf.* I.86–7) ("thou art alone the one from whom I took the beautiful style that has done honour to me"). In the 20th century, [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot") famously began a lecture on the subject "What Is a Classic?" by asserting as self-evidently true that "whatever the definition we arrive at, it cannot be one which excludes Virgil – we may say confidently that it must be one which will expressly reckon with him".

## Traditional biography

### Biographical sources

Biographical information about Virgil is transmitted chiefly in *vitae* ("lives") of the poet, prefixed to commentaries on his work by [Probus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Valerius_Probus "Marcus Valerius Probus"), [Donatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aelius_Donatus "Aelius Donatus"), and [Servius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servius_the_Grammarian "Servius the Grammarian"). The life given by Donatus is considered to closely reproduce the life of Virgil from a lost work of [Suetonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius "Suetonius") on the lives of famous authors, just as Donatus used it for the poet's life in his commentary on [Terence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence "Terence"), where Suetonius is explicitly credited. The far shorter life given by Servius likewise seems to be an abridgement of Suetonius except for one or two statements. [Varius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Varius_Rufus "Lucius Varius Rufus") is said to have written a memoir of his friend Virgil, and Suetonius likely drew on this lost work and other sources contemporary with the poet. A life written in verse by the grammarian Phocas (probably active in the 4th to 5th centuries AD) differs in some details from Donatus and Servius. [Henry Nettleship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Nettleship "Henry Nettleship") believed the life attributed to Probus may have drawn independently from the same sources as Suetonius, but it is attributed by other authorities to an anonymous author of the 5th or 6th century AD who drew on Donatus, Servius, and Phocas. The Servian life was the principal source of Virgil's biography for medieval readers, while the Donatian life enjoyed a more limited circulation, and the lives of Phocas and Probus remained largely unknown.

Although the commentaries record much factual information about Virgil, some of their evidence can be shown to rely on allegorizing and on inferences drawn from his poetry. For this reason, details regarding Virgil's life story are considered somewhat problematic.

### Family and birth

According to the ancient *vitae*, Publius Vergilius Maro was born on the [Ides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ides_(calendar) "Ides (calendar)") of October during the [consulship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consulship "Consulship") of [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey "Pompey") and [Crassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Licinius_Crassus "Marcus Licinius Crassus") (15 October 70 BC) in the village of Andes, near [Mantua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantua "Mantua") in [Cisalpine Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisalpine_Gaul "Cisalpine Gaul") ([northern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Italy "Northern Italy"), added to [Italy proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Italy "Roman Italy") during his lifetime). His ancestry is uncertain, with scholars variously proposing [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscans "Etruscans"), [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts "Celts"), [Venetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneti_(Gaul) "Veneti (Gaul)"), [Ligurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligurians "Ligurians") or [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks "Greeks") origins.

The Donatian life reports that some say Virgil's father was a potter, but most say he was an employee of an [apparitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparitor "Apparitor") named Magius, whose daughter he married. According to Phocas and Probus, the name of Virgil's mother was [Magia Polla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magia_Polla "Magia Polla"). The [gentilicium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentilicium "Gentilicium") of Virgil's maternal family, *Magius*, and failure to distinguish the genitive form of this name (*Magi*) in Servius' life, from the genitive *magi* of the noun *magus* ("magician"), probably contributed to the rise of the medieval legend that Virgil's father was employed by a certain itinerant magician, and that Virgil was a magician.

#### Site of Andes

A tradition of obscure origin, which was accepted by Dante, identifies Andes with modern [Pietole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietole "Pietole"), two or three miles southeast of Mantua. The ancient biography attributed to [Probus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Valerius_Probus "Marcus Valerius Probus") records that Andes was thirty [Roman miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mile#Roman "Mile") (about 45 kilometres or 28 miles) from Mantua. There are eight or nine references to the *[gens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gens "Gens")* to which Vergil belonged, [*gens Vergilia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vergilia_gens "Vergilia gens"), in inscriptions from Northern Italy. Out of these, four are from townships remote from Mantua, three appear in inscriptions from [Verona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verona "Verona"), and one in an inscription from [Calvisano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvisano "Calvisano"), a [votive offering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Votive_offering "Votive offering") to the [Matronae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matres_and_Matronae "Matres and Matronae") (a group of deities) by a woman called Vergilia, asking the goddesses to deliver from danger another woman, called Munatia. A tomb erected by a member of the [*gens Magia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magia_gens "Magia gens"), to which Virgil's mother belonged, is found at [Casalpoglio](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Casalpoglio&action=edit&redlink=1 "Casalpoglio (page does not exist)"), just 12 kilometres (7.5 mi) from Calvisano. In 1915, G. E. K. Braunholtz drew attention to the proximity of these inscriptions to each other, and the fact that Calvisano is exactly 30 Roman miles from Mantua, which led [Robert Seymour Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Seymour_Conway "Robert Seymour Conway") to theorize that these inscriptions have to do with relatives of Virgil, and Calvisano or [Carpenedolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpenedolo "Carpenedolo"), not Pietole, is the site of Andes. [E. K. Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Kennard_Rand "Edward Kennard Rand") defended the traditional site at Pietole, noting that [Egnazio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egnazio "Egnazio")'s 1507 edition of Probus's commentary, supposedly based on a "very ancient codex" from [Bobbio Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobbio_Abbey "Bobbio Abbey") which can no longer be found, says that Andes was three miles from Mantua, and arguing this is the correct reading. Conway replied that Egnazio's manuscript cannot be trusted to have been as ancient as Egnazio claimed it was, nor can we be sure that the reading "three" is not Egnazio's conjectural correction of his manuscript to harmonize it with the Pietole tradition, and all other evidence strongly favours the unanimous reading of the other witnesses of "thirty miles". Other studies claim that today's consideration for ancient *Andes* should be sought in the Casalpoglio area of [Castel Goffredo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castel_Goffredo "Castel Goffredo").

#### Spelling of name

By the 4th or 5th century AD the original spelling *Vergilius* had been changed to *Virgilius*, and the latter spelling spread to modern European languages. This latter spelling persisted even though, as early as the 15th century, the classical scholar [Poliziano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poliziano "Poliziano") had shown *Vergilius* to be the original spelling. Today, the [anglicisations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicisation "Anglicisation") *Vergil* and *Virgil* are both considered acceptable.

There is speculation that the spelling *Virgilius* might have arisen due to a pun, since *virg-* carries an echo of the Latin word for "wand" (*uirga*), Virgil being particularly associated with magic in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"). There is also a possibility that *virg-* is meant to evoke the Latin *virgo* ("virgin"); this would be a reference to the [fourth *Eclogue*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogue_4 "Eclogue 4"), which has a history of Christian, and specifically [Messianic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messianism "Messianism"), [interpretations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_interpretations_of_Virgil%27s_Eclogue_4 "Christian interpretations of Virgil's Eclogue 4").

### Childhood and education

Virgil spent his boyhood in [Cremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremona "Cremona") until his 15th year (55 BC), when he is said to have received the *[toga virilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toga_virilis "Toga virilis")* on the very day [Lucretius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius "Lucretius") died. From Cremona, he moved to Milan, and shortly afterwards to Rome. After briefly considering a career in [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric") and law, Virgil turned his talents to poetry. Despite the biographers' statements that Virgil's family was of modest means, these accounts of his education, as well as of his ceremonial assumption of the *toga virilis*, suggest his father was a wealthy [equestrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equites "Equites") landowner.

He is said to have been tall and stout, with a swarthy complexion and a rustic appearance. Virgil seems to have suffered bad health throughout his life and in some ways lived the life of an invalid. Schoolmates considered Virgil shy and reserved, and he was nicknamed "Parthenias" ("virgin") because of his aloofness.

### Poetic career

The biographical tradition asserts that Virgil began the hexameter *[Eclogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogues "Eclogues")* (or *Bucolics*) in 42 BC and it is thought the collection was published around 39–38 BC, although this is controversial. After defeating the army led by the [assassins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Julius_Caesar "Assassination of Julius Caesar") of [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") in the [Battle of Philippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Philippi "Battle of Philippi") (42 BC), [Octavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavian "Octavian") tried to pay off his veterans with land expropriated from towns in northern Italy, which—according to tradition—included an estate near Mantua belonging to Virgil. The loss of Virgil's family farm and the attempt through poetic petitions to regain his property, were seen as his motives in the composition of the *Eclogues*. This is now thought to be an unsupported inference from interpretations of the *Eclogues*. In *Eclogues* 1 and 9, Virgil indeed dramatizes the contrasting feelings caused by the brutality of the land expropriations through pastoral idiom, but offers no indisputable evidence of the supposed biographic incident.

Sometime after the publication of the *Eclogues*, probably before 37 BC, Virgil became part of the circle of [Gaius Maecenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Maecenas "Gaius Maecenas"), Octavian's capable political adviser, who sought to counter sympathy for Antony among the leading families by rallying Roman literary figures to Octavian's side. Virgil came to know many other leading literary figures of the time, including [Horace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace "Horace"), in whose poetry he is often mentioned, and [Varius Rufus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varius_Rufus "Varius Rufus"), who later helped finish the *Aeneid*. At Maecenas's insistence, according to the tradition, Virgil spent the ensuing years (perhaps 37–29 BC) on the long [dactylic hexameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dactylic_hexameter "Dactylic hexameter") poem called the *[Georgics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgics "Georgics")* (from Greek, "On Working the Earth"), which he dedicated to Maecenas.

Virgil worked on the *Aeneid* during the last eleven years of his life (29–19 BC), commissioned, according to [Propertius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propertius "Propertius"), by [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus "Augustus"). According to the tradition, Virgil travelled to the [senatorial province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senatorial_province "Senatorial province") of [Achaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaea_(Roman_province) "Achaea (Roman province)") in Greece, in about 19 BC, to revise the *Aeneid*. After meeting Augustus in Athens and deciding to return home, Virgil caught a fever while visiting a town near [Megara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megara "Megara"). After crossing to Italy by ship, weakened with disease, Virgil died in [Apulia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apulia "Apulia") on 21 September 19 BC. Augustus ordered Virgil's literary executors, [Lucius Varius Rufus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Varius_Rufus "Lucius Varius Rufus") and [Plotius Tucca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotius_Tucca "Plotius Tucca"), to disregard Virgil's wish [that the poem be burned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_burning#Posthumous_destruction_of_works "Book burning"), instead ordering it to be published with as few editorial changes as possible.

#### Burial and tomb

After his death at [Brundisium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brundisium "Brundisium") according to Donatus, or [Taranto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranto "Taranto") according to late manuscripts of Servius, Virgil's remains were transported to [Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naples "Naples"), where his tomb was engraved with an epitaph he had composed: **Mantua me genuit; Calabri rapuere; tenet nunc Parthenope. Cecini pascua, rura, duces**; "[Mantua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantua "Mantua") gave me life, the [Calabrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salento "Salento") took it away, Naples holds me now; I sang of pastures, farms, and commanders." (transl. [Bernard Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Knox "Bernard Knox")) [Martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial "Martial") reports that [Silius Italicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silius_Italicus "Silius Italicus") annexed the site to his estate (11.48, 11.50), and [Pliny the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Younger "Pliny the Younger") says that Silius "would visit Virgil's tomb as if it were a temple" (*Epistulae* 3.7.8).

The structure known as Virgil's tomb is found at the entrance of an ancient Roman tunnel (*grotta vecchia*) in [Piedigrotta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedigrotta "Piedigrotta"), a district 1.9 mi (3 km) from the centre of Naples, near the [Mergellina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mergellina "Mergellina") harbour, on the road heading north along the coast to [Pozzuoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozzuoli "Pozzuoli"). While Virgil was already the object of literary admiration and veneration before his death, in the Middle Ages his name became associated with miraculous powers, and for a couple of centuries his tomb was the destination of [pilgrimages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrimage "Pilgrimage") and veneration. A famous medieval legend that [Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle "Paul the Apostle") had visited Virgil's tomb and wept that so great a poet had died without the Christian faith is referenced in a liturgical hymn said to have been used on Paul's feast day at Mantua:

> Ad Maronis mausoleum
> Ductus, fudit super eum
> Piæ rorem lacrymæ;
> Quem te, inquit, reddidissem,
> Si te vivum invenissem,
> Poetarum maxime!

Translation:
> When to Maro's tomb they brought him,
> Tender grief and pity wrought him
> To bedew the stone with tears;
> "What a saint I might have crowned thee
> Had I only living found thee,
> Poet first and without peers!"

However, [Johann Friedrich Heinrich Schlosser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Friedrich_Heinrich_Schlosser "Johann Friedrich Heinrich Schlosser") was unable to find a manuscript of this hymn, and reported that he had only heard these verses recited from memory by a brother who had lived at Mantua.

Through the 19th century, the supposed tomb attracted travellers on the [Grand Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Tour "Grand Tour"), and still draws visitors.

## Works

### Early works

According to the commentators, Virgil received his first education when he was five and later went to [Cremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremona "Cremona"), [Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan "Milan"), and finally [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome "Rome") to study [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric"), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"), and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), which he would abandon for philosophy. From Virgil's admiring references to the [neoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoteric "Neoteric") writers [Asinius Pollio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Asinius_Pollio_(consul_40_BC) "Gaius Asinius Pollio (consul 40 BC)") and [Cinna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvius_Cinna "Helvius Cinna"), it has been inferred that he was, for a time, associated with [Catullus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catullus "Catullus")'s neoteric circle. According to the *[Catalepton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appendix_Vergiliana#Catalepton_("Trifles") "Appendix Vergiliana")*, he began to write poetry while in the [Epicurean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurean "Epicurean") school of [Siro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siro_the_Epicurean "Siro the Epicurean") in Naples. A group of small works attributed to the youthful Virgil by the commentators survive collected under the title *[Appendix Vergiliana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appendix_Vergiliana "Appendix Vergiliana")*, but are considered spurious by scholars. One, the *Catalepton*, consists of fourteen short poems, some of which may be Virgil's, and a short narrative poem *[Culex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appendix_Vergiliana#Culex_("The_Gnat") "Appendix Vergiliana")* ("The Gnat"), was attributed to Virgil as early as the 1st century AD.

### *Eclogues*

The *Eclogues* (from the Greek for "selections") are a group of ten poems roughly modelled on the [bucolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucolic "Bucolic") ("pastoral" or "rural") poetry of the Hellenistic poet [Theocritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theocritus "Theocritus"), which were written in [dactylic hexameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dactylic_hexameter "Dactylic hexameter"). While some readers have identified Virgil with various characters and their vicissitudes, whether gratitude by an old rustic to a new god (*Ecl*. 1), frustrated love by a rustic singer for a distant boy (his master's pet, *Ecl*. 2), or a master singer's claim to have composed several eclogues (*Ecl*. 5), modern scholars largely reject such efforts to garner biographical details from fiction, preferring to interpret an author's characters and themes as illustrations of contemporary life and thought.

The ten *Eclogues* present traditional pastoral themes with a fresh perspective. Eclogues 1 and 9 address the land confiscations and their effects on the Italian countryside. 2 and 3 are pastoral and erotic, discussing homosexual love (*Ecl*. 2) and attraction toward people of any gender (*Ecl*. 3). [*Eclogue* 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogue_4 "Eclogue 4"), addressed to [Asinius Pollio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asinius_Pollio "Asinius Pollio"), the so-called "Messianic Eclogue", uses the imagery of the golden age in connection with the birth of a child (the child's identity has been debated). 5 and 8 describe the myth of [Daphnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphnis "Daphnis") in a song contest, 6, the cosmic and mythological song of [Silenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silenus "Silenus"); 7, a heated poetic contest, and 10 the sufferings of the contemporary elegiac poet [Cornelius Gallus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Gallus "Cornelius Gallus"). Virgil in his *Eclogues* is credited with establishing [Arcadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcadia_(utopia) "Arcadia (utopia)") as a poetic ideal that still resonates in literature and visual arts and with setting the stage for the development of Latin pastoral by [Calpurnius Siculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calpurnius_Siculus "Calpurnius Siculus"), [Nemesianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemesianus "Nemesianus") and later writers.

### *Georgics*

The ostensible theme of the *Georgics* is instruction in the methods of running a farm. In handling this, Virgil follows in the [didactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didactic "Didactic") ("how to") tradition of the Greek poet [Hesiod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesiod "Hesiod")'s *[Works and Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Works_and_Days "Works and Days")* and works of the later Hellenistic poets. The four books of the *Georgics* focus respectively on:

1. raising crops;
2. raising trees;
3. livestock and horses;
4. beekeeping and the qualities of bees.

Well-known passages include the beloved *Laus Italiae* of Book 2, the prologue description of the temple in Book 3, and the description of the plague at the end of Book 3. Book 4 concludes with a long mythological narrative, in the form of an *[epyllion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epyllion "Epyllion")*, which describes vividly the discovery of beekeeping by [Aristaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristaeus "Aristaeus"), and the story of [Orpheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orpheus "Orpheus")' journey to the underworld. Ancient scholars, such as Servius, conjectured that the Aristaeus episode replaced, at the emperor's request, a long section in praise of Virgil's friend, the poet Gallus, who was disgraced by Augustus, and committed suicide in 26 BC.

The tone of the *Georgics* wavers between optimism and pessimism, sparking critical debate on the poet's intentions, but the work lays the foundations for later didactic poetry. Virgil and Maecenas are said to have taken turns reading the *Georgics* to Octavian upon his return from defeating Antony and [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra") at the [Battle of Actium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Actium "Battle of Actium") in 31 BC.

### *Aeneid*

The *Aeneid* is widely considered Virgil's finest work, and one of the most important poems in the history of literature ([T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot") referred to it as "the classic of all Europe"). The work, modelled after [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer")'s *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad "Iliad")* and *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey "Odyssey")*, chronicles the journey of a warrior and refugee of the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War "Trojan War"), named [Aeneas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneas "Aeneas"), as he struggles to fulfill his destiny. After fleeing the sack of Troy, he travels to Italy, where he battles with [Turnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnus "Turnus"), and his descendants [Romulus and Remus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus_and_Remus "Romulus and Remus") found the city of Rome.

The epic poem consists of 12 books in [dactylic hexameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dactylic_hexameter "Dactylic hexameter") verse. The *Aeneid*'s first six books describe the journey of Aeneas from Troy to Rome. Virgil made use of several models in the composition of his epic; Homer, the pre-eminent author of classical epic, is everywhere present, but Virgil also makes special use of the Latin poet [Ennius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ennius "Ennius") and the Hellenistic poet [Apollonius of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonius_of_Rhodes "Apollonius of Rhodes"), among other writers to whom he alludes. Although the *Aeneid* casts itself firmly into the epic mode, it often expands the genre by including elements of other genres, such as tragedy and aetiological poetry. Ancient commentators noted that Virgil seems to divide the *Aeneid* into two sections based on the poetry of Homer; the first six books were viewed as employing the *Odyssey* as a model while the last six were connected to the *Iliad*.

Book 1, at the head of the Odyssean section, opens with a storm which [Juno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_(mythology) "Juno (mythology)"), Aeneas's enemy throughout the poem, stirs up against the fleet. The storm drives the hero to the coast of [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage "Carthage"), which was Rome's deadliest foe. The queen, [Dido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dido_(Queen_of_Carthage) "Dido (Queen of Carthage)"), welcomes the ancestor of the Romans, and under the influence of the gods falls deeply in love with him. At a banquet in Book 2, Aeneas tells the story of the sack of [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy "Troy"), the death of his wife, and his escape, to the enthralled Carthaginians, while in Book 3 he recounts to them his wanderings over the Mediterranean in search of a suitable new home. [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_(god) "Jupiter (god)") in Book 4 recalls the lingering Aeneas to his duty to found a new city, and he slips away from Carthage, leaving Dido to commit suicide, cursing Aeneas and calling down revenge in symbolic anticipation of the fierce wars between Carthage and Rome. In Book 5, funeral games are celebrated for Aeneas's father [Anchises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchises "Anchises"), who had died a year before. On reaching [Cumae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumae "Cumae"), in Italy in Book 6, Aeneas consults the [Cumaean Sibyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumaean_Sibyl "Cumaean Sibyl"), who conducts him through the [Underworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underworld "Underworld") where Aeneas meets the dead Anchises who reveals Rome's destiny to his son.

Book 7, beginning the Iliadic half, opens with an address to the muse and recounts Aeneas's arrival in Italy and betrothal to [Lavinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavinia "Lavinia"), daughter of King [Latinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinus "Latinus"). Lavinia had already been promised to [Turnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnus "Turnus"), the king of the [Rutulians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutuli "Rutuli"), who is roused to war by the [Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erinyes "Erinyes") [Allecto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allecto "Allecto") and [Amata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amata "Amata"), Lavinia's mother. In Book 8, Aeneas allies with [King Evander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evander_of_Pallene "Evander of Pallene"), who occupies the future site of Rome, and is given new armour and a shield depicting Roman history. Book 9 records an assault by [Nisus and Euryalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nisus_and_Euryalus "Nisus and Euryalus") on the Rutulians; Book 10, the death of Evander's young son [Pallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallas_(son_of_Evander) "Pallas (son of Evander)"); and 11 the death of the Volscian warrior princess [Camilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camilla_(mythology) "Camilla (mythology)") and the decision to settle the war with a duel between Aeneas and Turnus. The *Aeneid* ends in Book 12 with the taking of Latinus's city, the death of Amata, and Aeneas's defeat and killing of Turnus, whose pleas for mercy are spurned. The final book ends with the image of Turnus's soul lamenting as it flees to the underworld.

### Reception of the *Aeneid*

Critics of the *Aeneid* focus on a variety of issues. The tone as a whole is a particular matter of debate; some see the poem as ultimately pessimistic and politically subversive to the Augustan regime, while others view it as a celebration of the new imperial dynasty. Virgil makes use of the symbolism of the regime, and some scholars see strong associations between Augustus and Aeneas, the one as founder and the other as re-founder of Rome. A strong [teleology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleology "Teleology"), or drive towards a climax, has been detected. The *Aeneid* is full of prophecies about the future of Rome, the deeds of Augustus, his ancestors, and famous Romans, and the [Carthaginian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthaginian_Wars "Carthaginian Wars"); the shield of Aeneas even depicts Augustus's victory at [Actium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Actium "Battle of Actium") against [Mark Antony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Antony "Mark Antony") and [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra") in 31 BC. A further focus of study is the character of Aeneas. As the protagonist, Aeneas seems to waver constantly between his emotions and commitment to his prophetic duty to found Rome; critics note the breakdown of Aeneas's emotional control in the last sections of the poem where the "pious" and "righteous" Aeneas mercilessly slaughters Turnus.

The *Aeneid* appears to have been a great success. Virgil is said to have recited Books 2, 4, and 6 to Augustus; and Book 6 apparently caused the emperor's sister [Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavia_the_Younger "Octavia the Younger") to faint. Although the truth of this claim is subject to scholarly scepticism, it has served as a basis for art, such as [Jean-Baptiste Wicar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Wicar "Jean-Baptiste Wicar")'s *Virgil Reading the Aeneid*.

Some lines of the poem were left unfinished, and the whole was unedited, at Virgil's death in 19 BC. As a result, the text of the *Aeneid* that exists may contain faults which Virgil was planning to correct before publication. However, the only obvious imperfections are a few lines of verse that are metrically unfinished, i.e. not a complete line of [dactylic hexameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dactylic_hexameter "Dactylic hexameter"). Some scholars have argued that Virgil deliberately left these incomplete for dramatic effect. Other alleged imperfections are subject to debate.

## Legacy and reception

### Antiquity

The works of Virgil, almost from the moment of their publication, revolutionized [Latin poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_poetry "Latin poetry"). The *Eclogues*, *Georgics*, and above all the *Aeneid* became standard texts in school curricula with which all educated Romans were familiar. Poets following Virgil often refer intertextually to his works to generate meaning in their poetry. The Augustan poet [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid "Ovid") parodies the opening lines of the *Aeneid* in *[Amores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amores_(Ovid) "Amores (Ovid)")* 1.1.1–2, and his summary of the Aeneas story in Book 14 of the *[Metamorphoses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphoses "Metamorphoses")*, the so-called "mini-Aeneid", has been viewed as an important example of post-Virgilian response to the epic genre. [Lucan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucan "Lucan")'s epic, the *[Bellum Civile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharsalia "Pharsalia")*, has been considered an anti-Virgilian epic, disposing of the divine mechanism, treating historical events, and diverging from Virgilian epic practice. The Flavian-era poet [Statius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statius "Statius") in his 12-book epic *Thebaid* engages closely with the poetry of Virgil; in his epilogue he advises his poem not to "rival the divine *Aeneid*, but follow afar and ever venerate its footsteps". Virgil finds one of his most ardent admirers in [Silius Italicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silius_Italicus "Silius Italicus"). With almost every line of his epic *[Punica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punica_(poem) "Punica (poem)")*, Silius references Virgil.

Virgil also found commentators in antiquity. [Servius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurus_Servius_Honoratus "Maurus Servius Honoratus"), a commentator of the 4th century AD, based his work on the commentary of [Donatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aelius_Donatus "Aelius Donatus"). Servius's commentary provides us with a great deal of information about Virgil's life, sources, and references; however, many modern scholars find the variable quality of his work and the often simplistic interpretations frustrating.

### Late antiquity

Even as the Western Roman Empire collapsed, literate men acknowledged that Virgil was a master poet; [Augustine of Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo") confessed how he had wept at reading the death of Dido. The best-known surviving manuscripts of Virgil's works include manuscripts from late antiquity, such as the *[Vergilius Augusteus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vergilius_Augusteus "Vergilius Augusteus")*, the *[Vergilius Vaticanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vergilius_Vaticanus "Vergilius Vaticanus")* and the *[Vergilius Romanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vergilius_Romanus "Vergilius Romanus")*.

### Middle Ages

[Gregory of Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours "Gregory of Tours") read Virgil, whom he quotes in several places, along with other Latin poets, though he cautions that "we ought not to relate their lying fables, lest we fall under sentence of eternal death". In the [Renaissance of the 12th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_of_the_12th_century "Renaissance of the 12th century"), [Alexander Neckham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Neckham "Alexander Neckham") placed the "divine" *Aeneid* on his standard arts curriculum, and Dido became the romantic heroine of the age. Monks like [Maiolus of Cluny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiolus_of_Cluny "Maiolus of Cluny") might repudiate what they called "the luxurious eloquence of Virgil", but they could not deny the power of his appeal.

[Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri "Dante Alighieri") presents Virgil as his guide through [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferno_(Dante) "Inferno (Dante)") and the greater part of [Purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatorio "Purgatorio") in the *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy "Divine Comedy")*. He also mentions Virgil in *[De vulgari eloquentia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_vulgari_eloquentia "De vulgari eloquentia")*, as one of the four *regulati poetae* along with [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid "Ovid"), [Lucan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Annaeus_Lucanus "Marcus Annaeus Lucanus") and [Statius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statius "Statius") (ii, vi, 7).

### Renaissance and early modernity

Several epics arrived in Virgil's wake, notably [Edmund Spenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser "Edmund Spenser")'s *[The Faerie Queene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Faerie_Queene "The Faerie Queene")* and [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton "John Milton")'s *[Paradise Lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost "Paradise Lost")*. He inspired works in other fields, including [Ingres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Auguste-Dominique_Ingres "Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres")'s painting *[Virgil reading The Aeneid before Augustus, Livia and Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil_reading_The_Aeneid_before_Augustus,_Livia_and_Octavia "Virgil reading The Aeneid before Augustus, Livia and Octavia")*, [Berlioz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Berlioz "Hector Berlioz")'s opera *[Les Troyens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Troyens "Les Troyens")* and [Hermann Broch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Broch "Hermann Broch")'s novel *[The Death of Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_of_Virgil "The Death of Virgil")*.

In the early modern period until the middle of the 18th century, Virgil was often regarded as the preeminent poet that European poets should try to emulate. A shift began in Germany when classical Greek culture rose in prestige at the expense of Roman, notably through the influence of [Johann Joachim Winckelmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Joachim_Winckelmann "Johann Joachim Winckelmann"). In spite of a loss in prestige, Virgil continued to be widely read and studied, and had significant influence also on German-language writers from the second half of the 18th century, such as [Salomon Gessner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salomon_Gessner "Salomon Gessner"), [Maler Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maler_M%C3%BCller "Maler Müller"), [Johann Heinrich Voß](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Heinrich_Voss "Johann Heinrich Voss"), [Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe") and [Novalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novalis "Novalis").

[Alfred, Lord Tennyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred,_Lord_Tennyson "Alfred, Lord Tennyson") wrote:

> I salute thee, Mantovano,
> I that loved thee since my day began,
> Wielder of the stateliest measure
> ever moulded by the lips of man.

— Alfred, Lord Tennyson, "To Virgil, Written at the Request of the Mantuans for the Nineteenth Centenary of Virgil's Death"

### Legends

The legend of "Virgil in his basket" arose in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), and is often seen in art and mentioned in literature as part of the [Power of Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_Women "Power of Women") [literary topos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_topos "Literary topos"), demonstrating the disruptive force of female attractiveness on men. In this story Virgil became enamoured of a beautiful woman, sometimes described as the emperor's daughter or mistress and called Lucretia. She played him along and agreed to an assignation at her house, which he was to sneak into at night, by climbing into a large basket let down from a window. When he did so he was hoisted only halfway up the wall and left trapped there into the next day, exposed to public ridicule. The story paralleled that of [Phyllis riding Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllis_and_Aristotle "Phyllis and Aristotle"). Among other artists depicting the scene, [Lucas van Leyden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_van_Leyden "Lucas van Leyden") made a [woodcut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut "Woodcut") and later an [engraving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraving "Engraving").

Partially as a result of his so-called "Messianic" [Eclogue 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogue_4 "Eclogue 4") – interpreted from the 3rd century by Christian thinkers to have predicted the [birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_of_Jesus "Nativity of Jesus") – Virgil was in later antiquity imputed to have the magical abilities of a seer. Eclogue 4 describes the birth of a boy ushering in a golden age. In consequence, Virgil came to be seen on a similar level to the Hebrew [prophets of the Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_prophecy "Bible prophecy") as one who had heralded Christianity. *[The Jewish Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jewish_Encyclopedia "The Jewish Encyclopedia")* argues that medieval legends about the [golem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golem "Golem") may have been inspired by Virgilian legends about the poet's apocryphal power to bring inanimate objects to life.

Possibly as early as the 2nd century AD, and into the Middle Ages, Virgil's works were seen as having magical properties and used for [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination "Divination"). In what became known as the *[Sortes Vergilianae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortes_Vergilianae "Sortes Vergilianae")* ("Virgilian Lots"), passages would be selected at random and interpreted to answer questions. In a similar vein, Macrobius in the *[Saturnalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrobius#Saturnalia "Macrobius")* credits the work of Virgil as the embodiment of human knowledge and experience, mirroring the Greek conception of Homer. In the 12th century, starting around [Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naples "Naples") but eventually spreading throughout Europe, a tradition developed in which Virgil was regarded as a great [magician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magician_(paranormal) "Magician (paranormal)"). Legends about Virgil and his magical powers remained popular for over two hundred years, arguably becoming as prominent as his writings. In medieval [Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales "Wales"), the Welsh version of his name, *Fferyllt* or *Pheryllt*, became a generic term for magic-worker, and survives in its word for pharmacist, *fferyllydd*.
