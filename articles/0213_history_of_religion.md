# History of religion

The **history of religion** is the written record of human [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious "Religious") feelings, thoughts, and ideas. This period of religious history begins with the [invention of writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invention_of_writing "Invention of writing") about 5,200 years ago (3200 BCE). The [prehistory of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_religion "Prehistoric religion") involves the study of religious beliefs that existed prior to the advent of written records. One can also study comparative religious chronology through a [timeline of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_religion "Timeline of religion"), or the interrelationships and historical diversification of religious ideologies through the use of evolutionary philosophy and broad comparativism. Writing played a major role in standardizing religious texts regardless of time or location and making easier the memorization of prayers and divine rules.

The concept of "religion" was formed in the 16th and 17th centuries. Sacred texts like the Bible, the Quran, and others did not have a word or even a concept of religion in the original languages and neither did the people or the cultures in which these sacred texts were written. Many cultures around the world today and in the past lack concepts or reject distinctions between the natural and supernatural.

The word *religion* as used in the 21st century does not have an obvious pre-colonial translation into non-European languages. The anthropologist Daniel Dubuisson writes that "what the West and the history of religions in its wake have objectified under the name 'religion' is ... something quite unique, which could be appropriate only to itself and its own history".

## History of study

The school of religious history called the *[Religionsgeschichtliche Schule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_religions_school "History of religions school")*, a late 19th-century [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") school of thought, originated the systematic study of [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") as a [socio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society "Society")-[cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture "Culture") phenomenon. It depicted religion as evolving with human culture, from [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism") to [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism").

The *Religionsgeschichtliche Schule* emerged at a time when scholarly study of the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible") and of [church history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_history "Church history") flourished in Germany and elsewhere (see [higher criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_criticism "Higher criticism"), also called the *historical-critical method*). The study of religion is important: religion and similar concepts have often shaped civilizations' law and moral codes, social structure, art and music.

In order to better understand the origin and current diversity of religious belief systems throughout the world, recent studies have attempted to focus on historical interrelationships and diversification of all major organized religions, applying modern evolutionary philosophy to the comparative analysis of putative ideological groups. These studies take an agnostic, pluralistic approach in the hope of moving beyond chauvinistic cultural tribalism, which is increasingly interfering with our ability to understand "other" cultures and address growing global challenges.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Origin

The earliest archeological evidence interpreted by some as suggestive of the emergence of religious ideas dates back several hundred thousand years, to the [Middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Paleolithic "Middle Paleolithic") and [Lower Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Paleolithic "Lower Paleolithic") periods: some archaeologists conclude that the apparently intentional burial of [archaic humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Homo_sapiens "Early Homo sapiens"), [Neanderthals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neanderthal "Neanderthal") and even *[Homo naledi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_naledi "Homo naledi")* as early as 300,000 years ago is proof that religious ideas already existed, but such a connection is entirely conjectural. Other evidence that some infer as indicative of religious ideas includes symbolic artifacts from [Middle Stone Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Stone_Age "Middle Stone Age") sites in Africa. However, the interpretation of early Paleolithic artifacts, with regard to how they relate to religious ideas, remains controversial.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Archeological evidence from more recent periods is less controversial. Scientists[*[which?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words "Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words")*] generally interpret a number of artifacts from the [Upper Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic "Upper Paleolithic") (50,000–13,000 BCE) as representing religious ideas. Examples of Upper Paleolithic remains that some associate with religious beliefs include the [lion man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%B6wenmensch_figurine "Löwenmensch figurine"), the [Venus figurines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_figurines "Venus figurines"), and the elaborate ritual burial from [Sungir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sungir "Sungir").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In the 19th century, researchers proposed various theories regarding the origin of religion, challenging earlier claims of a Christianity-like [urreligion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urreligion "Urreligion"). Early theorists, such as [Edward Burnett Tylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burnett_Tylor "Edward Burnett Tylor") (1832–1917) and [Herbert Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Spencer "Herbert Spencer") (1820–1903), emphasized the concept of [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism "Animism"), while archaeologist [John Lubbock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lubbock,_1st_Baron_Avebury "John Lubbock, 1st Baron Avebury") (1834–1913) used the term "[fetishism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetishism "Fetishism")". Meanwhile, the religious scholar [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") (1823–1900) theorized that religion began in [hedonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonism "Hedonism") and the folklorist [Wilhelm Mannhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Mannhardt "Wilhelm Mannhardt") (1831–1880) suggested that religion began in "naturalism" – by which he meant mythological explanations for natural events.[*[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] All of these theories have been widely criticized since then; there is no broad consensus regarding the origin of religion.

[Pre-pottery Neolithic A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-pottery_Neolithic_A "Pre-pottery Neolithic A") (PPNA) [Göbekli Tepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6bekli_Tepe "Göbekli Tepe"), the oldest potentially religious site yet discovered anywhere includes circles of erected massive T-shaped stone pillars, the world's oldest known [megaliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalith "Megalith") decorated with [abstract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_art "Abstract art"), enigmatic [pictograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictogram "Pictogram") and [carved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture "Sculpture")-animal reliefs. The site, near the home place of original wild wheat, was built before the so-called [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution"), i.e., the beginning of [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture") and [animal husbandry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_husbandry "Animal husbandry") around 9000 BCE. But the construction of Göbekli Tepe implies organization of an advanced order not hitherto associated with [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic "Paleolithic"), [PPNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_A "Pre-Pottery Neolithic A"), or [PPNB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_B "Pre-Pottery Neolithic B") societies. The site, abandoned around the time the first agricultural societies started, is still being excavated and analyzed, and thus might shed light on the significance it had, if any, for the religions of older, foraging communities, as well as for the general history of religions.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The [Pyramid Texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_Texts "Pyramid Texts") from ancient Egypt, the oldest known [religious texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_texts "Religious texts") in the world, date to between 2400 and 2300 BCE.

The earliest records of [Indian religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religion "Indian religion") are the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"), composed c. 1500–1200 BCE during the [Vedic Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Period "Vedic Period").

Surviving early copies of religious texts include:

* The [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads"), some of which date to the mid-first millennium BCE.
* The [Dead Sea Scrolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea_Scrolls "Dead Sea Scrolls"), representing fragmentary texts of the Hebrew [Tanakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh").
* Complete Hebrew texts, also of the [Tanakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh"), but translated into the [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek "Koine Greek") ([Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint "Septuagint") 300–200 BCE), were in wide use by the early 1st century CE.
* The Zoroastrian [Avesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avesta "Avesta"), from a [Sassanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanian "Sassanian")-era master copy.

## Axial age

Some historians have labelled the period from 900 to 200 BCE as the "axial age", a term coined by [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany")-Swiss [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher "Philosopher") [Karl Jaspers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Jaspers "Karl Jaspers") (1883–1969). According to Jaspers, in this era of history "the [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") foundations of humanity were laid simultaneously and independently... And these are the foundations upon which humanity still subsists today." Intellectual historian [Peter Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Watson_(intellectual_historian) "Peter Watson (intellectual historian)") has summarized this period as the foundation time of many of humanity's most influential philosophical traditions, including monotheism in [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia "Persia") and [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan "Canaan"), [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism "Platonism") in Greece, [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") and [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism "Jainism") in India, and [Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism "Confucianism") and [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism") in China. These ideas would become institutionalized in time – note for example [Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka "Ashoka")'s role in the spread of Buddhism, or the role of [Neoplatonic philosophy in Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism_and_Christianity "Neoplatonism and Christianity") at its foundation.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The historical roots of Jainism in India date back to the 9th century BCE with the rise of [Parshvanatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parshvanatha "Parshvanatha") and his non-violent philosophy.[*[need quotation to verify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

## Middle Ages

[World religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_religion "World religion") of the present day established themselves throughout [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia "Eurasia") during the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") by:

* [Christianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization "Christianization") of the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world")
* [Buddhist missions to East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road_transmission_of_Buddhism "Silk Road transmission of Buddhism")
* the [decline of Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_of_Buddhism_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "Decline of Buddhism in the Indian subcontinent") in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent")
* the [spread of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam "Spread of Islam") throughout the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"), [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"), [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa") and parts of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") and India

During the Middle Ages, [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim "Muslim") came into conflict with [Zoroastrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrian "Zoroastrian") during the [Muslim conquest of Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia "Muslim conquest of Persia") (633–654); [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians "Christians") fought against Muslims during the [Arab–Byzantine wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Byzantine_wars "Arab–Byzantine wars") (7th to 11th centuries), the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades") (1095 onward), the [Reconquista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista") (718–1492), the [Ottoman wars in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_wars_in_Europe "Ottoman wars in Europe") (13th century onwards) and the [Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inquisition "Inquisition"); [Shamanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism "Shamanism") was in conflict with [Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism"), [Taoists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism"), Muslims and Christians during the [Mongol invasions and conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_and_conquests "Mongol invasions and conquests") (1206–1337); and Muslims clashed with [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus "Hindus") and [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhs "Sikhs") during the [Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquests_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent") (8th to 16th centuries).

Many medieval religious movements continued to emphasize [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism"), such as the [Cathars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathars "Cathars") and related movements in the West, the Jews in Spain (see [Zohar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohar "Zohar")), the [Bhakti movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti_movement "Bhakti movement") in India and [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") in Islam. [Monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") and related mysticisms reached definite forms in Christian [Christology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christology "Christology") and in Islamic [Tawhid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawhid "Tawhid"). [Hindu monotheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_views_on_monotheism "Hindu views on monotheism") notions of [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") likewise reached their classical form with the teaching of [Adi Shankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankara "Adi Shankara") (788–820).

## Modern Ages

From the 15th century to the 19th century, European [colonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonisation "Colonisation") resulted in the spread of Christianity to [Sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa"), and the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"), [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia "Australia") and the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines "Philippines"). The invention of the [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press "Printing press") in the 15th century played a major role in the rapid spread of the [Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation "Protestant Reformation") under leaders such as [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther "Martin Luther") (1483–1546) and [John Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin "John Calvin") (1509–1564). [Wars of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_wars_of_religion "European wars of religion") broke out, culminating in the [Thirty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years%27_War "Thirty Years' War") which ravaged [Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe "Central Europe") between 1618 and 1648. The 18th century saw the beginning of [secularisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularisation "Secularisation") in Europe, a trend which gained momentum after the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution") broke out in 1789. By the late 20th century, religion had declined in most of Europe.
