# Neolithic Revolution

The **Neolithic Revolution**, also known as the  **First Agricultural Revolution**, was the wide-scale transition of many [human cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_culture "Human culture") during the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") period in [Afro-Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia "Afro-Eurasia") from a lifestyle of [hunting and gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") to one of [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture") and [settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedentism "Sedentism"), making an increasingly large population possible. These settled communities permitted humans to observe and experiment with plants, learning how they grew and developed. This new knowledge led to the domestication of plants into [crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop "Crop").

Archaeological data indicate that the [domestication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication "Domestication") of various types of [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant "Plant") and animals happened in separate locations worldwide, starting in the [geological epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale "Geologic time scale") of the [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene "Holocene") 11,700 years ago, after the end of the last Ice Age. It was humankind's first historically verifiable transition to agriculture. The Neolithic Revolution greatly narrowed the diversity of foods available, resulting in a decrease in the quality of human nutrition compared with that obtained previously from hunting and [foraging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foraging "Foraging"). However, because food production became more efficient, it released humans to invest their efforts in other activities and was thus "ultimately necessary to the rise of modern civilization by creating the foundation for the later process of industrialization and sustained economic growth".

The Neolithic Revolution involved much more than the adoption of a limited set of food-producing techniques. During the next millennia, it transformed the small and mobile groups of hunter-gatherers that had hitherto dominated [human prehistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history "Human history") into [sedentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedentism "Sedentism") (non-[nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad")) [societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society "Society") based in built-up villages and towns. These societies radically modified their [natural environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_environment "Natural environment") by means of specialized food-crop cultivation, with activities such as [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation") and [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation "Deforestation") which allowed the production of surplus food. Other developments that are found very widely during this era are the [domestication of animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_animals "Domestication of animals"), [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery "Pottery"), polished stone tools, and rectangular houses. In many regions, the adoption of agriculture by prehistoric societies caused episodes of rapid population growth, a phenomenon known as the [Neolithic demographic transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_demographic_transition "Neolithic demographic transition").

These developments, sometimes called the **Neolithic package**, provided the basis for centralized administrations and political structures, hierarchical [ideologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology "Ideology"), depersonalized systems of knowledge (e.g. [writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_writing "History of writing")), [densely populated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density "Population density") settlements, specialization and [division of labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labour "Division of labour"), more [trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade "Trade"), the development of non-portable [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art "Art") and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"), and greater [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property "Property") ownership. The [earliest known civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization "Cradle of civilization") developed in [Sumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") in southern [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") (c. 6,500 [BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present "Before Present")); its emergence also heralded the beginning of the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age").

The relationship of the aforementioned Neolithic characteristics to the onset of agriculture, their sequence of emergence, and their empirical relation to each other at various Neolithic sites remains the subject of academic debate. It is usually understood to vary from place to place, rather than being the outcome of universal laws of [social evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociocultural_evolution "Sociocultural evolution").

## Background

Prehistoric [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") had different [subsistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence "Subsistence") requirements and lifestyles from agriculturalists. Hunter-gatherers were often highly mobile and migratory, living in temporary shelters and in small tribal groups, and having limited contact with outsiders. Their diet was well-balanced[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] though heavily dependent on what the environment could provide each season. In contrast, because the [surplus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surplus_product "Surplus product") and [plannable supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_security "Food security") of food provided by agriculture made it possible to support larger population groups, agriculturalists lived in more permanent dwellings in more densely populated [settlements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement "Human settlement") than what could be supported by a hunter-gatherer lifestyle. The agricultural communities' seasonal need to plan and coordinate [resource](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_management "Resource management") and [manpower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manpower "Manpower") encouraged [division of labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labor "Division of labor"), which gradually led to [specialization of labourers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skilled_worker "Skilled worker") and [complex societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_societies "Complex societies"). The subsequent development of [trading networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_network "Trading network") to exchange surplus commodities and services brought agriculturalists into contact with outside groups, which promoted [cultural exchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_globalization "Cultural globalization") that led to the rise of [civilizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization "Civilization") and [technological evolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_evolution "Technological evolution").[*[full citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#What_information_to_include "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*]

However, higher population and food abundance did not necessarily correlate with improved health. Reliance on a very limited variety of [staple crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_crop "Staple crop") can adversely affect health even while making it possible to feed more people. As an example from the Americas, [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") is deficient in certain essential [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acids "Amino acids") ([lysine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysine "Lysine") and [tryptophan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryptophan "Tryptophan")) and is a poor source of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron "Iron"). The [phytic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytic_acid "Phytic acid") it contains may inhibit [nutrient absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestion "Digestion"). Other factors that likely affected the health of early agriculturalists and their domesticated [livestock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livestock "Livestock") would have been increased numbers of [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasites "Parasites") and disease-bearing pests associated with human waste and contaminated food and water supplies. [Fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer "Fertilizer") and [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation") may have increased crop yields but also would have promoted proliferation of [insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect "Insect") and [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria "Bacteria") in the local environment while grain storage attracted additional insects and [rodents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodents "Rodents").

## Agricultural transition

The term 'neolithic revolution' was invented by [V. Gordon Childe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._Gordon_Childe "V. Gordon Childe") in his book *Man Makes Himself* (1936). Childe introduced it as the first in a series of agricultural revolutions in [Middle Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") history, calling it a "revolution" to denote its significance, the degree of change to communities adopting and refining agricultural practices.

The beginning of this process in different regions has been dated from 10,000 to 8,000 BCE in the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent"), and perhaps 8000 BCE in the [Kuk Early Agricultural Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuk_Swamp "Kuk Swamp") of Papua New Guinea in [Melanesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanesia "Melanesia"). Everywhere, this transition is associated with a change from a largely [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic "Nomadic") [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") way of life to a more [settled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler "Settler"), agrarian one, with the [domestication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication "Domestication") of various plant and animal species – depending on the species locally available, and influenced by local culture. Archaeological research in 2003 suggests that in some regions, such as the Southeast Asian peninsula, the transition from hunter-gatherer to agriculturalist was not linear, but region-specific.

## Domestication

### Crops

Once agriculture started gaining momentum, around 9000 BP, human activity resulted in the [selective breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_breeding "Selective breeding") of cereal grasses (beginning with [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer"), [einkorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einkorn "Einkorn") and [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley")), and not simply of those that favoured greater caloric returns through larger seeds. Plants with traits such as small seeds or bitter taste were seen as undesirable. Plants that rapidly shed their seeds on maturity tended not to be gathered at harvest, therefore not stored and not seeded the following season; successive years of harvesting spontaneously selected for strains that retained their edible seeds longer.

[Daniel Zohary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Zohary "Daniel Zohary") identified several plant species as "pioneer crops" or [Neolithic founder crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_founder_crops "Neolithic founder crops"). He highlighted the importance of wheat, barley and rye, and suggested that domestication of [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax "Flax"), [peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea "Pea"), [chickpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickpea "Chickpea"), [bitter vetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicia_ervilia "Vicia ervilia") and [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil "Lentil") came a little later. Based on analysis of the [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") of domesticated plants, he preferred theories of a single, or at most a very small number of domestication events for each [taxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxon "Taxon") that spread in an arc from the [Levantine corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_corridor "Levantine corridor") around the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent") and later into Europe. [Gordon Hillman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Hillman "Gordon Hillman") and Stuart Davies carried out experiments with varieties of wild wheat to show that the process of domestication would have occurred over a relatively short period of between 20 and 200 years.

Some of the pioneering attempts failed at first and crops were abandoned, sometimes to be taken up again and successfully domesticated thousands of years later: [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye "Rye"), tried and abandoned in Neolithic [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia"), made its way to Europe as weed seeds and was successfully domesticated in Europe, thousands of years after the earliest agriculture. Wild lentils presented a different problem: most of the wild seeds do not germinate in the first year; the first evidence of lentil domestication, breaking dormancy in their first year, appears in the early Neolithic at [Jerf el Ahmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerf_el_Ahmar "Jerf el Ahmar") (in modern Syria), and lentils quickly spread south to the [Netiv HaGdud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netiv_HaGdud "Netiv HaGdud") site in the [Jordan Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_Valley_(Middle_East) "Jordan Valley (Middle East)"). The process of domestication allowed the founder crops to adapt and eventually become larger, more easily harvested, more dependable[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] in storage and more useful to the human population.

Selectively propagated [figs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ficus "Ficus"), wild barley and wild oats were cultivated at the early Neolithic site of [Gilgal I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgal_I "Gilgal I"), where in 2006 archaeologists found caches of seeds of each in quantities too large to be accounted for even by [intensive gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_gathering "Intensive gathering"), at strata datable to c. 11,000 years ago. Some of the plants tried and then abandoned during the Neolithic period in the Ancient Near East, at sites like Gilgal, were later successfully domesticated in other parts of the world.

Once early farmers perfected their agricultural techniques like [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation") (traced as far back as the 6th millennium BCE in [Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestan_province "Khuzestan province")), their crops [yielded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yield "Crop yield") surpluses that needed storage. Most hunter-gatherers could not easily store food for long due to their migratory lifestyle, whereas those with a sedentary dwelling could store their surplus grain. Eventually [granaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granary "Granary") were developed that allowed villages to store their seeds longer. So with more food, the population expanded and communities developed specialized workers and more advanced tools.

The process was not as linear as was once thought, but a more complicated effort, which was undertaken by different human populations in different regions in many different ways.

One of the world's most important crops, [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley"), was domesticated in the Near East around 11,000 years ago (c. 9,000 BCE). Barley is a highly resilient crop, able to grow in varied and marginal environments, such as in regions of high altitude and latitude. Archaeobotanical evidence shows that barley had spread throughout Eurasia by 2,000 BCE. To further elucidate the routes by which barley cultivation was spread through Eurasia, genetic analysis was used to determine genetic diversity and population structure in extant barley taxa. Genetic analysis shows that cultivated barley spread through Eurasia via several different routes, which were most likely separated in both time and space.

### Livestock

When hunter-gathering began to be replaced by sedentary food production it became more efficient to keep animals close at hand. Therefore, it became necessary to bring animals permanently to their settlements, although in many cases there was a distinction between relatively sedentary farmers and nomadic herders.[*[original research?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research "Wikipedia:No original research")*] The animals' size, temperament, diet, mating patterns, and life span were factors in the desire and success in domesticating animals. Animals that provided milk, such as cows and goats, offered a source of protein that was renewable and therefore quite valuable. The animal's ability as a worker (for example ploughing or towing), as well as a food source, also had to be taken into account. Besides being a direct source of food, certain animals could provide leather, wool, hides, and fertilizer. Some of the earliest domesticated animals included [dogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_the_domestic_dog "Origin of the domestic dog") ([East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia"), about 15,000 years ago), sheep, goats, cows, and pigs.

West Asia was the source for many animals that could be domesticated, such as sheep, goats and pigs. This area was also the first region to [domesticate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesticate "Domesticate") the [dromedary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dromedary "Dromedary"). [Henri Fleisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Fleisch "Henri Fleisch") discovered and termed the [Shepherd Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd_Neolithic "Shepherd Neolithic") [flint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint "Flint") industry from the [Bekaa Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekaa_Valley "Bekaa Valley") in [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon "Lebanon") and suggested that it could have been used by the earliest [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad") [shepherds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd "Shepherd"). He dated this industry to the [Epipaleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipaleolithic "Epipaleolithic") or [Pre-Pottery Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic "Pre-Pottery Neolithic") as it is evidently not [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic "Paleolithic"), [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic "Mesolithic") or even [Pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery "Pottery") [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic").

The presence of these animals gave the region a large advantage in cultural and economic development. As the climate in the Middle East changed and became drier, many of the farmers were forced to leave, taking their domesticated animals with them. It was this massive emigration from the Middle East that later helped distribute these animals to the rest of [Afroeurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroeurasia "Afroeurasia"). This emigration was mainly on an east–west axis of similar climates, as crops usually have a narrow optimal climatic range outside of which they cannot grow for reasons of light or rain changes. For instance, wheat does not normally grow in tropical climates, just like tropical crops such as bananas do not grow in colder climates. Some authors, like [Jared Diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jared_Diamond "Jared Diamond"), have postulated that this east–west axis is the main reason why plant and animal domestication spread so quickly from the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent") to the rest of Eurasia and North Africa, while it did not reach through the north–south axis of [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") to reach the Mediterranean climates of [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa "South Africa"), where temperate crops were successfully imported by ships in the last 500 years. Similarly, the African [Zebu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebu "Zebu") of central Africa and the domesticated bovines of the fertile-crescent — separated by the dry sahara desert — were not introduced into each other's region.

## Centers of agricultural origin

### West Asia

[Use-wear analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use-wear_analysis "Use-wear analysis") of five [glossed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle-gloss "Sickle-gloss") flint blades found at [Ohalo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohalo_II "Ohalo II"), a 23,000-years-old fisher-hunter-gatherers' camp on the shore of the [Sea of Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Galilee "Sea of Galilee"), Northern [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel"), provides the earliest evidence for the use of composite cereal harvesting tools. The Ohalo site is at the junction of the [Upper Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic "Upper Paleolithic") and the Early [Epipaleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipaleolithic "Epipaleolithic"), and has been attributed to both periods.

The wear traces indicate that tools were used for harvesting near-ripe semi-green wild cereals, shortly before grains are ripe and disperse naturally. The studied tools were not used intensively, and they reflect two harvesting modes: flint knives held by hand and inserts hafted in a handle. The finds shed new light on cereal harvesting techniques some 8,000 years before the [Natufian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natufian "Natufian") and 12,000 years before the establishment of sedentary farming communities in the Near East. Furthermore, the new finds accord well with evidence for the earliest ever cereal cultivation at the site and the use of stone-made grinding implements.

Agriculture appeared first in [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia "West Asia") about 10,000–9,000 years ago. The region was the centre of domestication for three cereals (einkorn wheat, emmer wheat and barley), four legumes (lentil, pea, bitter vetch and chickpea), and flax. Domestication was a slow process that unfolded across multiple regions, and was preceded by centuries if not [millennia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennia "Millennia") of pre-domestication cultivation.

Other sites in the [Levantine corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_corridor "Levantine corridor") that show early evidence of agriculture include [Wadi Faynan 16](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wadi_Faynan_16&action=edit&redlink=1 "Wadi Faynan 16 (page does not exist)") and [Netiv Hagdud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netiv_Hagdud "Netiv Hagdud"). [Jacques Cauvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Cauvin "Jacques Cauvin") noted that the settlers of Aswad did not domesticate on site, but *"arrived, perhaps from the neighbouring [Anti-Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Lebanon "Anti-Lebanon"), already equipped with the seed for planting"*. In the Eastern Fertile Crescent, evidence of cultivation of wild plants has been found in [Choga Gholan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chogha_Golan "Chogha Golan") in [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran") dated to 12,000 BP, with domesticated emmer wheat appearing in 9,800 BP, suggesting there may have been multiple regions in the Fertile Crescent where cereal domestication evolved roughly contemporaneously. The [Heavy Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_Neolithic "Heavy Neolithic") [Qaraoun culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaraoun_culture "Qaraoun culture") has been identified at around fifty sites in Lebanon around the source springs of the [River Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Jordan "River Jordan"), but never reliably dated.

### East Asia

Agriculture in [Neolithic China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_China "Neolithic China") can be separated into two broad regions, Northern China and Southern China.

The agricultural centre in northern China is believed to be the homelands of the early [Sino-Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan "Sino-Tibetan")-speakers, associated with the [Houli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houli_culture "Houli culture"), [Peiligang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peiligang_culture "Peiligang culture"), [Cishan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cishan_culture "Cishan culture"), and [Xinglongwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinglongwa "Xinglongwa") [cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_culture "Archaeological culture"), clustered around the [Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River "Yellow River") basin. It was the domestication centre for [foxtail millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxtail_millet "Foxtail millet") (*Setaria italica*) and [broomcorn millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broomcorn_millet "Broomcorn millet") (*Panicum miliaceum*), with early evidence of domestication approximately 8,000 years ago, and widespread cultivation 7,500 years ago. ([Soybean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean "Soybean") was also domesticated in northern China 4,500 years ago. [Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_(fruit) "Orange (fruit)") and [peach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peach "Peach") also originated in China, being cultivated c. 2500 BCE.)

The agricultural centres in southern China are clustered around the [Yangtze River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze_River "Yangtze River") basin. Rice was domesticated in this region, together with the development of [paddy field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddy_field "Paddy field") cultivation, between 13,500 and 8,200 years ago.

There are two possible centres of domestication for rice. The first is in the lower [Yangtze River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze_River "Yangtze River"), believed to be the homelands of [pre-Austronesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_peoples "Austronesian peoples") and associated with the [Kauhuqiao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauhuqiao_culture "Kauhuqiao culture"), [Hemudu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemudu_culture "Hemudu culture"), [Majiabang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majiabang_culture "Majiabang culture"), and [Songze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songze_culture "Songze culture") [cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_culture "Archaeological culture"). It is characterized by typical pre-Austronesian features, including stilt houses, jade carving, and boat technologies. Their diet were also supplemented by [acorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acorn "Acorn"), [water chestnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleocharis_dulcis "Eleocharis dulcis"), [foxnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxnut "Foxnut"), and [pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesticated_pig "Domesticated pig") domestication. The second is in the middle Yangtze River, believed to be the homelands of the early [Hmong–Mien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong%E2%80%93Mien "Hmong–Mien") speakers and associated with the [Pengtoushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pengtoushan_culture "Pengtoushan culture") and [Daxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daxi_culture "Daxi culture") [cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_culture "Archaeological culture"). Both of these regions were heavily populated and had regular trade contacts with each other, as well as with early [Austroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic "Austroasiatic") speakers to the west, and early [Kra-Dai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kra-Dai "Kra-Dai") speakers to the south, facilitating the spread of rice cultivation throughout southern China.

The millet and rice-farming cultures also first came into contact with each other at around 9,000 to 7,000 BP, resulting in a corridor between the millet and rice cultivation centres where both rice and millet were cultivated. At around 5,500 to 4,000 BP, there was increasing migration into [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan") from the early Austronesian [Dapenkeng culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dapenkeng_culture "Dapenkeng culture"), bringing rice and millet cultivation technology with them. During this period, there is evidence of large settlements and intensive rice cultivation in Taiwan and the [Penghu Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penghu_Islands "Penghu Islands"), which may have resulted in [overexploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overexploitation "Overexploitation"). Bellwood (2011) proposes that this may have been the impetus of the [Austronesian expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_expansion "Austronesian expansion") which started with the migration of the Austronesian-speakers from Taiwan to the Philippines at around 5,000 BP.

Austronesians carried rice cultivation technology to [Island Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_Southeast_Asia "Island Southeast Asia") along with other domesticated species. The new tropical island environments also had new food plants that they exploited. They carried useful [plants and animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesticated_plants_and_animals_of_Austronesia "Domesticated plants and animals of Austronesia") during each colonization voyage, resulting in the rapid introduction of domesticated and semi-domesticated species throughout [Oceania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceania "Oceania"). They also came into contact with the early agricultural centres of [Papuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papuan_languages "Papuan languages")-speaking populations of [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea") as well as the [Dravidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravidian_languages "Dravidian languages")-speaking regions of [South India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India") and [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") by around 3,500 BP. They acquired further cultivated food plants like bananas and pepper from them, and in turn introduced Austronesian technologies like wetland cultivation and [outrigger canoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outrigger_canoe "Outrigger canoe"). During the 1st millennium CE, they also colonized [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar "Madagascar") and the [Comoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoros "Comoros"), bringing Southeast Asian food plants, including rice, to [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa").

### Africa

On the African continent, three areas have been identified as having independently developed agriculture: the [Ethiopian highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Highlands "Ethiopian Highlands"), the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel "Sahel") and [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"). By contrast, [agriculture in the Nile River Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_agriculture "Ancient Egyptian agriculture") is thought to be related to migration of populations and to have developed from the original Neolithic Revolution in the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent").
Many grinding stones are found with the early Egyptian [Sebilian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebilian "Sebilian") and [Mechian](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mechian&action=edit&redlink=1 "Mechian (page does not exist)") cultures and evidence has been found of a Neolithic domesticated crop-based economy dating around 7,000 BP.
Unlike the Middle East, this evidence appears as a "false dawn" to agriculture, as the sites were later abandoned, and permanent farming then was delayed until 6,500 BP with the [Tasian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasian_culture "Tasian culture") and [Badarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badarian "Badarian") culture and the arrival of crops and animals from the Near East.

[Bananas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bananas "Bananas") and [plantains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantain_(cooking) "Plantain (cooking)"), which were first domesticated in [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), most likely [Papua New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea "Papua New Guinea"), were re-domesticated in Africa possibly as early as 5,000 years ago. Asian yams and [taro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taro "Taro") were also cultivated in Africa.

The most famous crop domesticated in the Ethiopian highlands is [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee "Coffee"). In addition, [khat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khat "Khat"), [ensete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensete "Ensete"), [noog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noog "Noog"), [teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff "Teff") and [finger millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finger_millet "Finger millet") were also domesticated in the Ethiopian highlands. Crops domesticated in the Sahel region include [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum") and [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet "Pearl millet"). The [kola nut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_nut "Kola nut") was first domesticated in West Africa. Other crops domesticated in West Africa include [African rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_rice "African rice"), [yams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_(vegetable) "Yam (vegetable)") and the [oil palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_palm "Oil palm").

Agriculture spread to Central and Southern Africa in the [Bantu expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion "Bantu expansion") during the 1st millennium BCE to 1st millennium CE.

### Americas

The term "Neolithic" is not customarily used in describing cultures in the Americas. However, a broad similarity exists between Eastern Hemisphere cultures of the Neolithic and cultures in the Americas. [Maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") (corn), [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beans "Beans") and [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_(plant) "Squash (plant)") were among the earliest crops domesticated in [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"): squash as early as 6000 BCE, beans no later than 4000 BCE, and maize beginning about 7000 BCE. [Potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potatoes "Potatoes") and [manioc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manioc "Manioc") were domesticated in [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America "South America"). In what is now the eastern United States, Native Americans domesticated [sunflower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower "Sunflower"), [sumpweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumpweed "Sumpweed") and [goosefoot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenopodium_berlandieri "Chenopodium berlandieri") c. 2500 BCE. In the highlands of central Mexico, sedentary village life based on farming did not develop until the "formative period" in the second millennium BCE.

### New Guinea

Evidence of drainage ditches at [Kuk Swamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuk_Swamp "Kuk Swamp") on the borders of the [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Highlands_Province "Western Highlands Province") and [Southern Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Highlands_Province "Southern Highlands Province") of [Papua New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea "Papua New Guinea") indicates cultivation of [taro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taro "Taro") and a variety of other crops, dating back to 11,000 BP. Two potentially significant economic species, taro (*[Colocasia esculenta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colocasia_esculenta "Colocasia esculenta")*) and [yam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_(vegetable) "Yam (vegetable)") (*[Dioscorea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioscorea "Dioscorea")* sp.), have been identified dating at least to 10,200 calibrated years before present (cal BP). Further evidence of [bananas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana "Banana") and [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane "Sugarcane") dates to 6,950 to 6,440 BCE. This was at the altitudinal limits of these crops, and it has been suggested that cultivation in more favourable ranges in the lowlands may have been even earlier. [CSIRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_Organisation "Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation") has found evidence that taro was introduced into the [Solomon Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Islands_(archipelago) "Solomon Islands (archipelago)") for human use, from 28,000 years ago, making taro the earliest cultivated crop in the world.
It seems to have resulted in the spread of the [Trans–New Guinea languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans%E2%80%93New_Guinea_languages "Trans–New Guinea languages") from New Guinea east into the Solomon Islands and west into [Timor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timor "Timor") and adjacent areas of [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia"). This seems to confirm the theories of [Carl Sauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_O._Sauer "Carl O. Sauer") who, in "Agricultural Origins and Dispersals", suggested as early as 1952 that this region was a centre of early agriculture.

## Spread of agriculture

### Europe

Archaeologists trace the emergence of food-producing societies in the [Levantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") region of southwest Asia at the close of the last glacial period around 12,000 BCE, and developed into a number of regionally distinctive cultures by the eighth millennium BCE. Remains of food-producing societies in the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_civilization "Aegean civilization") have been carbon-dated to c. 6500 BCE at [Knossos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knossos "Knossos"), [Franchthi Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franchthi_Cave "Franchthi Cave"), and a number of mainland sites in [Thessaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaly "Thessaly"). Neolithic groups appear soon afterwards in the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") and south-central Europe. The Neolithic cultures of [southeastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Europe "Southeastern Europe") (the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") and the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_civilization "Aegean civilization")) show some continuity with groups in southwest Asia and [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") (e.g., [Çatalhöyük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk "Çatalhöyük")).

Current evidence suggests that Neolithic material culture was introduced to Europe via western Anatolia. All Neolithic sites in Europe contain [ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery "Pottery"), and contain the plants and animals domesticated in Southwest Asia: [einkorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einkorn "Einkorn"), [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer"), [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley"), [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil "Lentil"), [pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig "Pig"), [goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat "Goat"), [sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep "Sheep"), and [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle "Cattle"). Genetic data suggest that no independent domestication of animals took place in Neolithic Europe, and that all domesticated animals were originally domesticated in Southwest Asia. The only domesticate not from Southwest Asia was [broomcorn millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broomcorn_millet "Broomcorn millet"), domesticated in East Asia.The earliest evidence of [cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheese "Cheese")-making dates to 5500 BCE in [Kujawy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kujawy "Kujawy"), [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland").

The diffusion across Europe, from the Aegean to Britain, took about 2,500 years (8500–6000 BP). The Baltic region was penetrated a bit later, around 5500 BP, and there was also a delay in settling the [Pannonian plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_plain "Pannonian plain"). In general, colonization shows a "saltatory" pattern, as the Neolithic advanced from one patch of fertile alluvial soil to another, bypassing mountainous areas. Analysis of [radiocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon "Radiocarbon") dates show clearly that Mesolithic and Neolithic populations lived side by side for as much as a millennium in many parts of Europe, especially in the [Iberian peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_peninsula "Iberian peninsula") and along the Atlantic coast.

#### Carbon 14 evidence

The spread of the Neolithic from the [Near East Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic "Pre-Pottery Neolithic") to Europe was first studied quantitatively in the 1970s, when a sufficient number of [Carbon 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_14 "Carbon 14") age determinations for early Neolithic sites had become available. In 1973, Ammerman and [Cavalli-Sforza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalli-Sforza "Cavalli-Sforza") discovered a linear relationship between the age of an Early Neolithic site and its distance from the conventional source in the Near East ([Jericho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jericho "Jericho")), demonstrating that the Neolithic spread at an average speed of about 1 km/yr. More recent studies (2005) confirm these results and yield the speed of 0.6–1.3 km/yr (at 95% confidence level).

#### Analysis of mitochondrial DNA

Since the original human [expansions out of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recent_African_origin_of_modern_humans "Recent African origin of modern humans") 200,000 years ago, different prehistoric and historic migration events have taken place in Europe. Considering that the movement of the people implies a consequent movement of their genes, it is possible to estimate the impact of these migrations through the genetic analysis of human populations. Agricultural and husbandry practices originated 10,000 years ago in a region of the Near East known as the Fertile Crescent. According to the archaeological record this phenomenon, known as "Neolithic", rapidly expanded from these territories into Europe.

However, whether this diffusion was accompanied or not by human migrations is greatly debated. [Mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA "Mitochondrial DNA") – a type of maternally inherited DNA located in the cell cytoplasm – was recovered from the remains of [Pre-Pottery Neolithic B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_B "Pre-Pottery Neolithic B") (PPNB) farmers in the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East "Near East") and then compared to available data from other Neolithic populations in Europe and also to modern populations from South Eastern Europe and the Near East. The obtained results show that substantial human migrations were involved in the Neolithic spread and suggest that the first Neolithic farmers entered Europe following a maritime route through [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") and the [Aegean Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Islands "Aegean Islands").

### South Asia

The earliest Neolithic site in South Asia is [Mehrgarh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrgarh "Mehrgarh"), dated to between 6500 and 5500 BCE, in the [Kachi plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kacchi_Plain "Kacchi Plain") of [Balochistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochistan,_Pakistan "Balochistan, Pakistan"), Pakistan; the site has evidence of farming (wheat and barley) and herding (cattle, sheep and goats).

There is strong evidence for causal connections between the Near-Eastern Neolithic and that further east, up to the Indus Valley. There are several lines of evidence that support the idea of connection between the Neolithic in the Near East and in the Indian subcontinent. The prehistoric site of Mehrgarh in Baluchistan (modern Pakistan) is the earliest Neolithic site in the north-west Indian subcontinent, dated as early as 8500 BCE.

Neolithic domesticated crops in Mehrgarh include more than 90% barley and a small amount of wheat. There is good evidence for the local domestication of barley and the zebu cattle at Mehrgarh, but the wheat varieties are suggested to be of Near-Eastern origin, as the modern distribution of wild varieties of wheat is limited to Northern Levant and Southern Turkey.

A detailed satellite map study of a few archaeological sites in the Baluchistan and Khybar Pakhtunkhwa regions also suggests similarities in early phases of farming with sites in Western Asia. Pottery prepared by sequential slab construction, circular fire pits filled with burnt pebbles, and large granaries are common to both Mehrgarh and many Mesopotamian sites.

The postures of the skeletal remains in graves at Mehrgarh bear strong resemblance to those at Ali Kosh in the Zagros Mountains of southern Iran. Despite their scarcity, the Carbon-14 and archaeological age determinations for early Neolithic sites in Southern Asia exhibit remarkable continuity across the vast region from the Near East to the Indian Subcontinent, consistent with a systematic eastward spread at a speed of about 0.65 km/yr.

## Causes

The most prominent of several theories (not mutually exclusive) as to factors that caused populations to develop agriculture include:

* The Oasis Theory, originally proposed by [Raphael Pumpelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Pumpelly "Raphael Pumpelly") in 1908, popularized by [V. Gordon Childe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._Gordon_Childe "V. Gordon Childe") in 1928 and summarised in Childe's book *Man Makes Himself*. This theory maintains that as the climate got drier due to the Atlantic depressions shifting northward, communities contracted to [oases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oases "Oases") where they were forced into close association with animals, which were then domesticated together with planting of seeds. However, this theory now has little support amongst archaeologists because subsequent climate data suggests that the region was getting wetter rather than drier.
* The [Hilly Flanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilly_Flanks "Hilly Flanks") hypothesis, proposed by [Robert John Braidwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_John_Braidwood "Robert John Braidwood") in 1948, suggests that agriculture began in the hilly flanks of the [Taurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taurus_Mountains "Taurus Mountains") and [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains "Zagros Mountains"), where the climate was not drier as Childe had believed, and fertile land supported a variety of plants and animals amenable to domestication.
* The Feasting model by Brian Hayden suggests that agriculture was driven by ostentatious displays of power, such as giving feasts, to exert dominance. This required assembling large quantities of food, which drove agricultural technology.
* The Demographic theories proposed by [Carl Sauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sauer "Carl Sauer") and adapted by [Lewis Binford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Binford "Lewis Binford") and [Kent Flannery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent_Flannery "Kent Flannery") posit an increasingly sedentary population that expanded up to the [carrying capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrying_capacity "Carrying capacity") of the local environment and required more food than could be gathered. Various social and economic factors helped drive the need for food.
* The evolutionary/intentionality theory, developed by [David Rindos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rindos "David Rindos") and others, considers agriculture as an evolutionary adaptation of plants and humans. Starting with domestication by protection of wild plants, it resulted specialization of location and then complete domestication.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
* [Peter Richerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Richerson "Peter Richerson"), [Robert Boyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyd_(anthropologist) "Robert Boyd (anthropologist)"), and [Robert Bettinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Robert_Bettinger&action=edit&redlink=1 "Robert Bettinger (page does not exist)") make a case for the development of agriculture coinciding with an increasingly stable climate at the beginning of the [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene "Holocene"). [Ronald Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Wright "Ronald Wright")'s book and Massey Lecture Series *[A Short History of Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Short_History_of_Progress "A Short History of Progress")* popularized this hypothesis.
* [Leonid Grinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Grinin "Leonid Grinin") argues that whatever plants were cultivated, the independent invention of agriculture always occurred in special natural environments (e.g., South-East Asia). It is supposed that the cultivation of cereals started somewhere in the Near East: in the hills of Israel or Egypt. So Grinin dates the beginning of the agricultural revolution within the interval 12,000 to 9,000 BP, though in some cases the first cultivated plants or domesticated animals' bones are even of a more ancient age of 14–15 thousand years ago.
* [Andrew Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_M._T._Moore "Andrew M. T. Moore") suggested that the Neolithic Revolution originated over long periods of development in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"), possibly beginning during the [Epipaleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipaleolithic "Epipaleolithic"). In *"A Reassessment of the Neolithic Revolution"*, [Frank Hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Hole "Frank Hole") further expanded the relationship between plant and [animal domestication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_domestication "Animal domestication"). He suggested the events could have occurred independently during different periods of time, in as yet unexplored locations. He noted that no transition site had been found documenting the shift from what he termed immediate and delayed return social systems.[*[further explanation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] He noted that the full range of domesticated animals ([goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat "Goat"), [sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep "Sheep"), [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle "Cattle") and [pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigs "Pigs")) were not found until the sixth millennium BCE at [Tell Ramad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Ramad "Tell Ramad"). Hole concluded that "close attention should be paid in future investigations to the western margins of the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates") basin, perhaps as far south as the [Arabian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula "Arabian Peninsula"), especially where [wadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadi "Wadi") carrying Pleistocene rainfall runoff flowed."

## Consequences

### Social change

Despite the significant technological advance and advancements in knowledge, arts and trade, the Neolithic revolution did not lead immediately to a rapid growth of population. Its benefits appear to have been offset by various adverse effects, mostly diseases and warfare.

The introduction of agriculture has not necessarily led to unequivocal progress. The nutritional standards of the growing Neolithic populations were inferior to that of hunter-gatherers. Several ethnological and archaeological studies conclude that the transition to cereal-based diets caused a reduction in life expectancy and stature, an increase in infant mortality and infectious diseases, the development of chronic, inflammatory or degenerative diseases (such as obesity, [type 2 diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_2_diabetes "Type 2 diabetes") and cardiovascular diseases) and multiple nutritional deficiencies, including vitamin deficiencies, [iron deficiency anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_deficiency_anemia "Iron deficiency anemia") and mineral disorders affecting bones (such as [osteoporosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteoporosis "Osteoporosis") and [rickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickets "Rickets")) and teeth. Average height for Europeans went down from 178 centimetres (5 ft 10 in) for men and 168 centimetres (5 ft 6 in) for women to 165 and 155 centimetres (5 ft 5 in and 5 ft 1 in) respectively, and it took until the twentieth century for average height for Europeans to return to the pre-Neolithic Revolution levels.

The traditional view is that agricultural food production supported a denser population, which in turn supported larger sedentary communities, the accumulation of goods and tools, and specialization in diverse forms of new labor. Food surpluses made possible the development of a social elite who were not otherwise engaged in agriculture, industry or commerce, but dominated their communities by other means and monopolized decision-making. Nonetheless, larger societies made it more feasible for people to adopt diverse decision making and governance models. Jared Diamond (in *[The World Until Yesterday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Until_Yesterday "The World Until Yesterday")*) identifies the availability of milk and cereal grains as permitting mothers to raise both an older (e.g. 3 or 4 year old) and a younger child concurrently. The result is that a population can increase more rapidly. Diamond, in agreement with feminist scholars such as [V. Spike Peterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._Spike_Peterson "V. Spike Peterson"), points out that agriculture brought about deep social divisions and encouraged [gender inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_inequality "Gender inequality"). This social reshuffle is traced by historical theorists, like Veronica Strang, through developments in theological depictions. Strang supports her theory through a comparison of aquatic deities before and after the Neolithic Agricultural Revolution, most notably the [Venus of Lespugue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_of_Lespugue "Venus of Lespugue") and the Greco-Roman deities such as [Circe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circe "Circe") or [Charybdis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charybdis "Charybdis"): the former venerated and respected, the latter dominated and conquered. The theory, supplemented by the widely accepted assumption from Parsons that "society is always the object of religious veneration", argues that with the centralization of government and the dawn of the Anthropocene, roles within society became more restrictive and were rationalized through the conditioning effect of religion; a process that is crystallized in the progression from polytheism to monotheism.

### Subsequent revolutions

[Andrew Sherratt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Sherratt "Andrew Sherratt") has argued that following upon the Neolithic Revolution was a second phase of discovery that he refers to as the [secondary products revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_products_revolution "Secondary products revolution"). Animals, it appears, were first domesticated purely as a source of meat. The Secondary Products Revolution occurred when it was recognised that animals also provided a number of other useful products. These included:

* [hides and skins (from undomesticated animals)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_hide_materials "History of hide materials")
* [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure "Manure") for soil conditioning (from all domesticated animals)
* [wool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool "Wool") (from sheep, llamas, alpacas, and [Angora goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angora_goat "Angora goat"))
* [milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk "Milk") (from goats, cattle, yaks, sheep, horses, and camels)
* [traction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_animal "Working animal") (from oxen, [onagers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onagers "Onagers"), donkeys, horses, camels, and dogs)
* guarding and herding assistance (dogs)

Sherratt argued that this phase in agricultural development enabled humans to make use of the energy possibilities of their animals in new ways, and permitted permanent intensive subsistence farming and crop production, and the opening up of heavier soils for farming. It also made possible [nomadic pastoralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_pastoralism "Nomadic pastoralism") in semi arid areas, along the margins of deserts, and eventually led to the domestication of both the [dromedary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dromedary "Dromedary") and [Bactrian camel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactrian_camel "Bactrian camel"). Overgrazing of these areas, particularly by herds of goats, greatly extended the areal extent of deserts.

### Diet and health

Compared to foragers, Neolithic farmers' diets were higher in [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate "Carbohydrate") but lower in [fibre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_fiber "Dietary fiber"), [micronutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronutrient "Micronutrient"), and [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"). This led to an increase in the frequency of [carious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tooth_decay "Tooth decay") teeth and slower growth in childhood and increased [body fat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_fat "Body fat")[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*], and studies have consistently found that populations around the world became shorter after the transition to agriculture. This trend may have been exacerbated by the greater seasonality of farming diets and with it the increased risk of [famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine "Famine") due to crop failure.

Throughout the development of sedentary societies, disease spread more rapidly than it had during the time in which hunter-gatherer societies existed. Inadequate sanitary practices and the domestication of animals may explain the rise in deaths and sickness following the Neolithic Revolution, as diseases jumped from the animal to the human population. Some examples of [infectious diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_diseases "Infectious diseases") spread from animals to humans are [influenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influenza "Influenza"), [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox "Smallpox"), and [measles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles "Measles"). Ancient microbial genomics has shown that progenitors to human-adapted strains of *[Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica "Salmonella enterica")* infected up to 5,500 year old agro-pastoralists throughout Western Eurasia, providing molecular evidence for the hypothesis that the Neolithization process facilitated the emergence of Salmonella entericia.

In concordance with a process of [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection"), the humans who first domesticated the big [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammals "Mammals") quickly built up immunities to the diseases as within each generation the individuals with better immunities had better chances of survival. In their approximately 10,000 years of shared proximity with animals, such as cows, Eurasians and Africans became more resistant to those diseases compared with the indigenous populations encountered outside [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia "Eurasia") and [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"). For instance, the population of most [Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean "Caribbean") and several [Pacific Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Islands "Pacific Islands") have been completely wiped out by diseases. 90% or more of many populations of the Americas were [wiped out by European and African diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas "Population history of indigenous peoples of the Americas") before recorded contact with European explorers or colonists. Some cultures like the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire "Inca Empire") did have a large domestic mammal, the [llama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llama "Llama"), but llama milk was not drunk, nor did llamas live in a closed space with humans, so the risk of contagion was limited. According to bioarchaeological research, the effects of agriculture on dental health in Southeast Asian rice farming societies from 4000 to 1500 BP was not detrimental to the same extent as in other world regions.

[Jonathan C. K. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jonathan_C._K._Wells&action=edit&redlink=1 "Jonathan C. K. Wells (page does not exist)") and [Jay T. Stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jay_T._Stock&action=edit&redlink=1 "Jay T. Stock (page does not exist)") have argued that the dietary changes and increased pathogen exposure associated with agriculture profoundly altered human biology and [life history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_history_theory "Life history theory"), creating conditions where [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection") favoured the allocation of resources towards [reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproduction "Reproduction") over [somatic effort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_effort "Somatic effort").
