# Alexander the Great

**Alexander III of Macedon** ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): [Ἀλέξανδρος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%88%CE%BB%CE%AD%CE%BE%CE%B1%CE%BD%CE%B4%CF%81%CE%BF%CF%82#Ancient_Greek "wikt:Ἀλέξανδρος"), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"): *Aléxandros*; 20/21 July 356 BC – 10/11 June 323 BC), most commonly known as **Alexander the Great**, was king of the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") kingdom of [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom) "Macedonia (ancient kingdom)") from 336 BC until his death. He was the son of [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon"), born to him by his fourth wife, [Olympias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympias "Olympias"), and ascended to the throne at the age of 20 after Philip was assassinated. Alexander spent most of his reign conducting [a lengthy military campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Alexander_the_Great "Wars of Alexander the Great") throughout [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia") and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"), and by the age of 30, he had created one of the [largest empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_empires "List of largest empires") in history, stretching from [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Greece "History of Greece") to northwestern [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India "History of India"). He was undefeated in battle and is widely considered to be one of history's greatest and most successful military commanders.

Born in [Pella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pella "Pella"), Macedon, Alexander was tutored by the philosopher and polymath [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") until the age of 16. In 335 BC, shortly after assuming the throne of Macedon, he launched a [campaign in the Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander%27s_Balkan_campaign "Alexander's Balkan campaign") and reasserted control over [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace") and parts of [Illyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria "Illyria") before marching on the city of [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece "Thebes, Greece"), which was [subsequently destroyed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thebes "Battle of Thebes"). Alexander then assumed leadership of the [League of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Corinth "League of Corinth"), created by his father.

With this authority over all Greeks he launched a pan-Hellenic invasion of the [Achaemenid Persian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") in 334 BC, and with it began [a series of campaigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Alexander_the_Great#Persia "Wars of Alexander the Great") which lasted for ten years. Following his conquest of [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor") and a series of decisive battles, particularly at [Issus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Issus "Battle of Issus") and [Gaugamela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaugamela "Gaugamela"), the power of the Achaemenid Empire was broken. He subsequently overthrew [Darius III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_III "Darius III") and conquered the Achaemenid Empire in its entirety. After the fall of Persia, the [Macedonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Empire "Macedonian Empire") held a vast swath of territory between the [Adriatic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriatic_Sea "Adriatic Sea") and the [Indus River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River "Indus River"). Alexander endeavored to reach the "ends of the world and the Great Outer Sea" and [invaded India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_campaign_of_Alexander_the_Great "Indian campaign of Alexander the Great") in 326 BC, achieving an important victory over [Porus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porus "Porus"), an ancient Indian king of present-day [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab "Punjab"), at the [Battle of the Hydaspes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Hydaspes "Battle of the Hydaspes"). Facing mutiny from his troops, he was eventually forced to turn back at the [Beas River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beas_River "Beas River"), and later died in 323 BC in [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon"), [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), the city which he had planned to make the capital of his empire, while on his return to Greece. [Alexander's death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Alexander_the_Great "Death of Alexander the Great") put an end to his planned invasion of [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia "Arabia").

In the years following his death, [a series of civil wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Diadochi "Wars of the Diadochi") broke out across the Macedonian Empire, eventually leading to its disintegration at the hands of the [Diadochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi "Diadochi"). Alexander's death marks the conventional beginning of the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"). Through his conquests, he built a legacy that includes the [cultural diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_diffusion "Cultural diffusion") and [syncretism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism"), which gave rise to [Greco-Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhism "Greco-Buddhism") and [Hellenistic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Judaism "Hellenistic Judaism"). [He founded more than twenty cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_founded_by_Alexander_the_Great "List of cities founded by Alexander the Great"), with the most prominent being the city of [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria") in Egypt. Alexander's establishment of [Greek colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonisation "Greek colonisation") and the spreading of [Greek culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_culture "Greek culture") led to [Hellenistic civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_civilization "Hellenistic civilization") becoming a major force in the ancient world, influencing regions as far east as the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"). The Hellenistic period developed through the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") into modern [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture "Western culture"); the [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") became the *[lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca "Lingua franca")* of the region and was the predominant language of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") until its collapse in 1453 AD.

Alexander became a legendary hero similar to [Achilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles "Achilles"), featuring prominently in the historical and mythical traditions of both Greek and non-Greek cultures. His military achievements and successes in battle made him the touchstone against which many later military leaders would judge themselves. His tactics remain a significant subject of study in [military academies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_academies "Military academies") worldwide. Legends of Alexander's exploits were coalesced into the 3rd century *[Alexander Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Romance "Alexander Romance")* which, in the premodern period, went through over one hundred recensions, translations, and derivations and was translated into almost every European vernacular and every language of the Islamic world. After the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible"), it was the most popular form of European literature.

## Early life

### Lineage and childhood

Alexander III was born in [Pella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pella "Pella"), the capital of the [Kingdom of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Macedon "Kingdom of Macedon"), on the sixth day of the [ancient Greek month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_calendars "Ancient Greek calendars") of [Hekatombaion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hekatombaion "Hekatombaion"), which probably corresponds to 20 July 356 BC (although the exact date is uncertain). He was the son of the king of Macedon, [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon "Philip II of Macedon") of the [Argead dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argead_dynasty "Argead dynasty"), and his fourth wife, [Olympias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympias "Olympias"), daughter of King [Neoptolemus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoptolemus_I_of_Epirus "Neoptolemus I of Epirus") of [Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epirus_(ancient_state) "Epirus (ancient state)"). Although Philip had seven or eight wives, Olympias was his principal wife for some time, likely because she gave birth to Alexander.

Several legends surround Alexander's birth and childhood. According to the ancient Greek biographer [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch "Plutarch"), on the eve of the consummation of her marriage to Philip, Olympias dreamed that her womb was struck by a thunderbolt that caused a flame to spread "far and wide" before dying away. Sometime after the wedding, Philip is said to have seen himself, in a dream, securing his wife's womb with a [seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_(emblem) "Seal (emblem)") engraved with a lion's image. Plutarch offered a variety of interpretations for these dreams: that Olympias was pregnant before her marriage, indicated by the sealing of her womb; or that Alexander's father was [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus "Zeus"). Ancient commentators were divided about whether the ambitious Olympias promulgated the story of Alexander's divine parentage, variously claiming that she had told Alexander, or that she dismissed the suggestion as impious. Despite this, Alexander could claim, by way of his father Phillip II, descendance from [Temenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temenus "Temenus"), a [descendant of Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracleidae "Heracleidae") (thereby also making him a very distant relation to the hero [Perseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perseus "Perseus")), and descendance by way of his mother's family, the [Aeacidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeacidae "Aeacidae"), to [Neoptolemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoptolemus "Neoptolemus"), son of the warrior [Achilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles "Achilles").

On the day Alexander was born, Philip was preparing a [siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege "Siege") on the city of [Potidea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potidea "Potidea") on the peninsula of [Chalcidice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcidice "Chalcidice"). That same day, Philip received news that his general [Parmenion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmenion "Parmenion") had defeated the combined [Illyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria "Illyria") and [Paeonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paeonian "Paeonian") armies and that his horses had won at the [Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games "Ancient Olympic Games"). It was also said that on this day, the [Temple of Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Artemis "Temple of Artemis") in [Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephesus "Ephesus"), one of the [Seven Wonders of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Wonders_of_the_Ancient_World "Seven Wonders of the Ancient World"), burnt down, with [Hegesias of Magnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegesias_of_Magnesia "Hegesias of Magnesia") claiming that it had burnt down because [Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis "Artemis") was away, attending the birth of Alexander. Such legends may have emerged when Alexander was king, and possibly at his instigation, to show that he was superhuman and destined for greatness from conception.

In his early years, Alexander was raised by a nurse, [Lanike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanike "Lanike"), sister of Alexander's future general [Cleitus the Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleitus_the_Black "Cleitus the Black"). Later in his childhood, Alexander was tutored by the strict [Leonidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonidas_of_Epirus "Leonidas of Epirus"), a relative of his mother, and by [Lysimachus of Acarnania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysimachus_of_Acarnania "Lysimachus of Acarnania"). Alexander was raised in the manner of noble Macedonian youths, learning to read, play the [lyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyre "Lyre"), ride, fight, and hunt. When Alexander was ten years old, a trader from [Thessaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaly "Thessaly") brought Philip a horse, which he offered to sell for thirteen [talents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_talent "Attic talent"). The horse refused to be mounted, and Philip ordered it away. Alexander, however, detecting the horse's fear of its own shadow, asked to tame the horse, which he eventually managed. Plutarch stated that Philip, overjoyed at this display of courage and ambition, kissed his son tearfully, declaring: "My boy, you must find a kingdom big enough for your ambitions. Macedon is too small for you", and bought the horse for him. Alexander named it [Bucephalas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucephalas "Bucephalas"), meaning "ox-head". Bucephalas carried Alexander as far as modern-day [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India"). When Bucephalas died (because of old age, according to Plutarch, at age 30), Alexander named a city after him, [Bucephala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria_Bucephalous "Alexandria Bucephalous").

### Education

When Alexander was 13, Philip began to search for a [tutor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutor "Tutor"), and considered such academics as [Isocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isocrates "Isocrates") and [Speusippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speusippus "Speusippus"), the latter offering to resign from his stewardship of the [Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy "Platonic Academy") to take up the post. In the end, Philip chose [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") and provided the Temple of the Nymphs at [Mieza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mieza,_Macedonia "Mieza, Macedonia") as a classroom. In return for teaching Alexander, Philip agreed to rebuild Aristotle's hometown of [Stageira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stageira "Stageira"), which Philip had razed, and to repopulate it by buying and freeing the ex-citizens who were slaves, or pardoning those who were in exile.

Mieza was like a boarding school for Alexander and the children of Macedonian nobles, such as [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_I_Soter "Ptolemy I Soter"), [Hephaestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestion "Hephaestion"), and [Cassander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassander "Cassander"). Many of these students would become his friends and future generals, and are often known as the "Companions". Aristotle taught Alexander and his companions about medicine, philosophy, morals, religion, logic, and art. Under Aristotle's tutelage, Alexander developed a passion for the works of [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer"), and in particular the *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad "Iliad")*; Aristotle gave him an annotated copy, which Alexander later carried on his campaigns. Alexander was able to quote [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides "Euripides") from memory.

In his youth, Alexander was also acquainted with Persian exiles at the Macedonian court, who received the protection of Philip II for several years as they opposed [Artaxerxes III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_III "Artaxerxes III"). Among them were [Artabazos II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artabazos_II "Artabazos II") and his daughter [Barsine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barsine "Barsine"), possible future mistress of Alexander, who resided at the Macedonian court from 352 to 342 BC, as well as [Amminapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amminapes "Amminapes"), future [satrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrap "Satrap") of Alexander, and a Persian nobleman named Sisines. This gave the Macedonian court a good knowledge of Persian issues, and may even have influenced some of the innovations in the management of the Macedonian state.

[Suda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suda "Suda") writes that [Anaximenes of Lampsacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaximenes_of_Lampsacus "Anaximenes of Lampsacus") was one of Alexander's teachers, and that Anaximenes also accompanied Alexander on his campaigns.

## Heir of Philip II

### Regency and ascent of Macedon

At the age of 16, Alexander's education under Aristotle ended. Philip II had waged war against the [Thracians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thracians "Thracians") to the north, which left Alexander in charge as [regent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent "Regent") and [heir apparent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heir_apparent "Heir apparent"). During Philip's absence, the Thracian tribe of [Maedi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maedi "Maedi") revolted against Macedonia. Alexander responded quickly and drove them from their territory. The territory was colonized, and a city, named [Alexandropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandropolis_Maedica "Alexandropolis Maedica"), was founded.

Upon Philip's return, Alexander was dispatched with a small force to subdue the revolts in southern [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace"). Campaigning against the Greek city of [Perinthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perinthus "Perinthus"), Alexander reportedly saved his father's life. Meanwhile, the city of [Amphissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphissa_(city) "Amphissa (city)") began to work lands that were sacred to [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo "Apollo") near [Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi "Delphi"), a sacrilege that gave Philip the opportunity to further intervene in Greek affairs. While Philip was occupied in Thrace, Alexander was ordered to muster an army for a campaign in southern Greece. Concerned that other Greek states might intervene, Alexander made it look as though he was preparing to attack Illyria instead. During this turmoil, the [Illyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrians "Illyrians") invaded Macedonia, only to be repelled by Alexander.

Philip and his army joined his son in 338 BC, and they marched south through [Thermopylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermopylae "Thermopylae"), taking it after stubborn resistance from its Theban garrison. They went on to occupy the city of [Elatea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elatea "Elatea"), only a few days' march from both [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens") and [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece "Thebes, Greece"). The [Athenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenians "Athenians"), led by [Demosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosthenes "Demosthenes"), voted to seek alliance with Thebes against Macedonia. Both Athens and Philip sent embassies to win Thebes's favour, but Athens won the contest. Philip marched on Amphissa (ostensibly acting on the request of the [Amphictyonic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphictyonic_League "Amphictyonic League")), capturing the mercenaries sent there by Demosthenes and accepting the city's surrender. Philip then returned to Elatea, sending a final offer of peace to Athens and Thebes, who both rejected it.

As Philip marched south, his opponents blocked him near [Chaeronea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaeronea "Chaeronea"), [Boeotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeotia "Boeotia"). During the ensuing [Battle of Chaeronea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chaeronea_(338_BC) "Battle of Chaeronea (338 BC)"), Philip commanded the right wing and Alexander the left, accompanied by a group of Philip's trusted generals. According to the ancient sources, the two sides fought bitterly for some time. Philip deliberately commanded his troops to retreat, counting on the untested Athenian [hoplites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoplites "Hoplites") to follow, thus breaking their line. Alexander was the first to break the Theban lines, followed by Philip's generals. Having damaged the enemy's cohesion, Philip ordered his troops to press forward and quickly routed them. With the Athenians lost, the Thebans were surrounded. Left to fight alone, they were defeated.

Following their victory at Chaeronea, Philip and Alexander marched unopposed into the [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese "Peloponnese"), devastating much of Laconia and ejecting the [Spartans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartans "Spartans") from various parts of it. At [Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth "Ancient Corinth"), Philip established a "Hellenic Alliance" (modelled on the old [anti-Persian alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece#Hellenic_alliance "Second Persian invasion of Greece") of the [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars "Greco-Persian Wars")), which included most Greek city-states except Sparta. Philip was then named *[Hegemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemon "Hegemon")* (often translated as "Supreme Commander") of this league (known by modern scholars as the [League of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Corinth "League of Corinth")), and announced his plans to attack the [Persian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Empire "Persian Empire").

### Exile and return

When Philip returned to Pella, he fell in love with and married [Cleopatra Eurydice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra_Eurydice "Cleopatra Eurydice") in 338 BC, the niece of his general [Attalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attalus_(general) "Attalus (general)"). The marriage made Alexander's position as heir less secure, since any son of Cleopatra Eurydice would be a fully Macedonian heir, while Alexander was only half-Macedonian. During the [wedding banquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banquet "Banquet"), a drunken Attalus publicly prayed to the gods that the union would produce a legitimate heir.

> At the wedding of Cleopatra, whom Philip fell in love with and married, she being much too young for him, her uncle Attalus in his drink desired the Macedonians would implore the gods to give them a lawful successor to the kingdom by his niece. This so irritated Alexander that throwing one of the cups at his head, "You villain," said he, "what, am I then a bastard?" Then Philip, taking Attalus's part, rose up and would have run his son through; but by good fortune for them both, either his over-hasty rage, or the wine he had drunk, made his foot slip, so that he fell down on the floor, at which Alexander reproachfully insulted him: "See there," said he, "the man who makes preparations to pass out of Europe into Asia, overturned in passing from one seat to another."

— Plutarch, describing the feud at Philip's wedding.

In 337 BC, Alexander fled Macedon with his mother, dropping her off with her brother, King [Alexander I of Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Epirus "Alexander I of Epirus") in [Dodona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodona "Dodona"), capital of the [Molossians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molossians "Molossians"). He continued to Illyria where he sought refuge with one or more Illyrian kings, perhaps with [Glaucias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaucias_of_Taulantii "Glaucias of Taulantii"), and was treated as a guest, despite having defeated them in battle a few years before. However, it appears Philip never intended to disown his politically and militarily trained son. Accordingly, Alexander returned to Macedon after six months due to the efforts of a family friend, [Demaratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demaratus_(hetairos) "Demaratus (hetairos)"), who mediated between the two parties.

In the following year, the Persian [satrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrap "Satrap") (governor) of [Caria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caria "Caria"), [Pixodarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixodarus "Pixodarus"), offered his eldest daughter to Alexander's half-brother, [Philip Arrhidaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Arrhidaeus "Philip Arrhidaeus"). Olympias and several of Alexander's friends suggested this showed Philip intended to make Arrhidaeus his heir. Alexander reacted by sending an actor, [Thessalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessalus_(actor) "Thessalus (actor)") of Corinth, to tell Pixodarus that he should not offer his daughter's hand to an illegitimate son, but instead to Alexander. When Philip heard of this, he stopped the negotiations and scolded Alexander for wishing to marry the daughter of a Carian, explaining that he wanted a better bride for him. Philip exiled four of Alexander's friends, [Harpalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpalus "Harpalus"), [Nearchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearchus "Nearchus"), [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_I_Soter "Ptolemy I Soter") and [Erigyius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erigyius "Erigyius"), and had the Corinthians bring Thessalus to him in chains.

## King of Macedon

### Accession

In the 24th day of the [Macedonian month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonian_calendar "Ancient Macedonian calendar") Dios, which probably corresponds to 25 October 336 BC, while at [Aigai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegae_(Macedonia) "Aegae (Macedonia)") (near modern [Vergina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vergina "Vergina")) attending the wedding of his daughter [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra_of_Macedon "Cleopatra of Macedon") to Olympias's brother, [Alexander I of Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Epirus "Alexander I of Epirus"), Philip was assassinated by the captain of his [bodyguards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatophylax "Somatophylax"), [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_of_Orestis "Pausanias of Orestis"), who, according to [Diodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus "Diodorus"), was also his lover. As Pausanias tried to escape, he tripped over a vine and was killed by his pursuers, including two of Alexander's companions, [Perdiccas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdiccas "Perdiccas") and [Leonnatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonnatus "Leonnatus"). Alexander was proclaimed king on the spot by the nobles and [army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonian_army "Ancient Macedonian army") at the age of 20.

### Consolidation of power

Alexander began his reign by eliminating potential rivals to the throne. He had his cousin, the former [Amyntas IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyntas_IV "Amyntas IV"), executed and two Macedonian princes from the region of [Lyncestis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyncestis "Lyncestis") killed for having been involved in his father's assassination, but spared a third, [Alexander Lyncestes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Lyncestes "Alexander Lyncestes"). Olympias had Cleopatra Eurydice, and Europa, her daughter by Philip, burned alive, though this infuriated Alexander. Alexander also ordered the murder of Cleopatra's uncle Attalus, who was in command of the advance guard of the army in Asia Minor. Attalus was at that time corresponding with Demosthenes regarding the possibility of defecting to Athens,[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] and had severely insulted Alexander. After his niece's murder, Alexander may have considered him too dangerous to be left alive. Alexander spared Arrhidaeus, who was by all accounts mentally disabled, possibly as a result of poisoning by Olympias.

News of Philip's death roused many states into revolt, including Thebes, Athens, Thessaly, and the Thracian tribes north of Macedon. When news of the revolts reached Alexander, he responded quickly. Though advised to use diplomacy, Alexander mustered 3,000 Macedonian cavalry and rode south towards Thessaly. He found the Thessalian army occupying the pass between [Mount Olympus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Olympus "Mount Olympus") and [Mount Ossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Ossa_(Greece) "Mount Ossa (Greece)"), and ordered his men to ride over Mount Ossa. When the Thessalians awoke the next day, they found Alexander in their rear and promptly surrendered, adding their cavalry to Alexander's force. He then continued south towards the [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese "Peloponnese").

Alexander stopped at Thermopylae where he was recognized as the leader of the Amphictyonic League before heading south to [Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth "Ancient Corinth"). Athens sued for peace and Alexander pardoned the rebels. The famous [encounter between Alexander and Diogenes the Cynic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_and_Alexander "Diogenes and Alexander") occurred during Alexander's stay in Corinth. When Alexander asked Diogenes what he could do for him, the philosopher disdainfully asked Alexander to stand a little to the side, as he was blocking the sunlight. This reply apparently delighted Alexander who is reported to have said, "But verily, if I were not Alexander, I would like to be Diogenes." At Corinth, Alexander took the title of *Hegemon* ("leader") and, like Philip, was appointed commander for the coming war against Persia. He also received news of a Thracian uprising.

### Balkan campaign

Before crossing to Asia, Alexander wanted to safeguard his northern borders. In the spring of 335 BC, he advanced to suppress several revolts. Starting from [Amphipolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphipolis "Amphipolis"), he travelled east into the country of the "Independent Thracians", and at [Mount Haemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Haemus "Mount Haemus"), the Macedonian army attacked and defeated the Thracian forces manning the heights. The Macedonians marched into the country of the [Triballi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triballi "Triballi") and defeated their army near the [Lyginus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyginus "Lyginus") river (a [tributary of the Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tributaries_of_the_Danube "List of tributaries of the Danube")). Alexander then marched for three days to the [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube "Danube"), encountering the [Getae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getae "Getae") tribe on the opposite shore. Crossing the river at night, he surprised them and forced their army to retreat after the first cavalry [skirmish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skirmish "Skirmish").

News then reached Alexander that the Illyrian chieftain [Cleitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleitus_(son_of_Bardylis) "Cleitus (son of Bardylis)") and [King Glaukias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Glaukias "King Glaukias") of the [Taulantii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taulantii "Taulantii") were in open revolt against his authority. Marching west into Illyria, Alexander defeated each in turn, forcing the two rulers to flee with their troops. With these victories, he secured his northern frontier.

### Destruction of Thebes

While Alexander campaigned north, the Thebans and Athenians rebelled once again. Alexander immediately headed south. While the other cities again hesitated, Thebes decided to fight. The Theban resistance was ineffective and Alexander [razed the city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thebes "Battle of Thebes") and divided its territory between the other Boeotian cities. The end of Thebes cowed Athens, leaving all of Greece temporarily at peace. Alexander then set out on his Asian campaign, leaving [Antipater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipater "Antipater") as regent.

## Conquest of the Persian Empire

### Strategy

Alexander's invasion of Persia as a whole has been denoted as a supreme example of a "strategic line" of conducting war, a line formed by "the chain of logic that connects operations into a single whole." In his book *Strategy*, Soviet military officer and theorist [Alexander Svechin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Svechin "Alexander Svechin") delineates Alexander's strategic steps. After securing his Greek base and the Balkans by subjugating his political opponents, and securing his army's rear through the conquest of all the Afro-Asian coastline, where the Persian fleet was based and from which it was supplied, Alexander moved to confront directly the Persians. He thus resolved the eternal problem of an army conducting operations deep into enemy territory, Svechin states, in an "exemplary manner."

### Asia Minor

After his victory at the Battle of Chaeronea, Philip II began the work of establishing himself as *hēgemṓn* ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language"): ἡγεμών) of a league which according to [Diodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus "Diodorus") was to wage a campaign against the Persians for the sundry grievances Greece [suffered in 480 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece "Second Persian invasion of Greece") and free the Greek cities of the western coast and islands from Achaemenid rule. In 336 he sent [Parmenion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmenion "Parmenion"), [Amyntas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyntas_(son_of_Andromenes) "Amyntas (son of Andromenes)"), Andromenes, Attalus, and an army of 10,000 men into [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") to make preparations for an invasion. The Greek cities on the western coast of Anatolia revolted until the news arrived that Philip had been murdered and had been succeeded by his young son Alexander. The Macedonians were demoralized by Philip's death and were subsequently defeated near [Magnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesia_on_the_Meander "Magnesia on the Meander") by the Achaemenids under the command of the mercenary [Memnon of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memnon_of_Rhodes "Memnon of Rhodes").

Taking over the invasion project of Philip II, Alexander's army crossed the [Hellespont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellespont "Hellespont") in 334 BC with approximately 48,100 soldiers, 6,100 cavalry, and 120 ships with crews numbering 38,000 drawn from Macedon and various Greek city states, mercenaries, and feudally raised soldiers from [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace"), [Paionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paionia "Paionia"), and [Illyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria "Illyria"). He showed his intent to conquer the entirety of the Persian Empire by throwing a spear into Asian soil and saying he accepted Asia as a gift from the gods. This also showed Alexander's eagerness to fight, in contrast to his father's preference for diplomacy.

After an initial victory against Persian forces at the [Battle of the Granicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Granicus "Battle of the Granicus"), Alexander accepted the surrender of the Persian provincial capital and treasury of [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis "Sardis"); he then proceeded along the [Ionian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia "Ionia") coast, granting autonomy and democracy to the cities. [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus "Miletus"), held by Achaemenid forces, required a delicate siege operation, with Persian naval forces nearby. Further south, at [Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halicarnassus "Halicarnassus"), in Caria, Alexander successfully waged his first large-scale siege, eventually forcing his opponents, the mercenary captain Memnon of Rhodes and the Persian satrap of Caria, [Orontobates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orontobates "Orontobates"), to withdraw by sea. Alexander left the government of Caria to a member of the Hecatomnid dynasty, [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_of_Caria "Ada of Caria"), who adopted Alexander.

From Halicarnassus, Alexander proceeded into mountainous [Lycia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycia "Lycia") and the [Pamphylian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamphylia "Pamphylia") plain, asserting control over all coastal cities to deny the Persians naval bases. From Pamphylia onwards, the coast held no major ports and Alexander moved inland. At [Termessos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Termessos "Termessos"), Alexander humbled and did not storm the [Pisidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisidia "Pisidia") city. At the ancient Phrygian capital of [Gordium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordium "Gordium"), Alexander "undid" the hitherto unsolvable [Gordian Knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordian_Knot "Gordian Knot"), a feat said to await the future "king of Asia". According to the story, Alexander proclaimed that it did not matter how the knot was undone, and hacked it apart with his sword.

### The Levant and Syria

In spring 333 BC, Alexander crossed the [Taurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taurus_Mountains "Taurus Mountains") into [Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia "Cilicia"). After a long pause due to an illness, he marched on towards Syria. Though outmanoeuvered by Darius's significantly larger army, he marched back to Cilicia, where he defeated Darius at [Issus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issus_(Cilicia) "Issus (Cilicia)"). Darius fled the battle, causing his army to collapse, and left behind his wife, his two daughters, his mother [Sisygambis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisygambis "Sisygambis"), and a fabulous treasure. He offered a [peace treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_treaty "Peace treaty") that included the lands he had lost, and a ransom of 10,000 [talents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_talent "Attic talent") for his family. Alexander replied that since he was now king of Asia, it was he alone who decided territorial divisions. Alexander proceeded to take possession of [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Syria "Ancient Syria"), and most of the coast of the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"). In the following year, 332 BC, he was forced to attack [Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyre,_Lebanon "Tyre, Lebanon"), which he captured after [a long and difficult siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Tyre_(332_BC) "Siege of Tyre (332 BC)"). The men of military age were massacred and the women and children sold into [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery "Slavery").

### Egypt

When Alexander destroyed Tyre, most of the towns on the route to Egypt quickly capitulated. However, Alexander was met with resistance at [Gaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_City "Gaza City"). The stronghold was heavily fortified and built on a hill, requiring a siege. When "his engineers pointed out to him that because of the height of the mound it would be impossible... this encouraged Alexander all the more to make the attempt". After three unsuccessful assaults, the stronghold fell, but not before Alexander had received a serious shoulder wound. As in Tyre, men of military age were put to the sword, and the women and children were sold into slavery.

Alexander advanced on Egypt in later 332 BC where he was regarded as a liberator. To legitimize taking power and be recognized as the descendant of the long line of [pharaohs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaohs "Pharaohs"), Alexander made sacrifices to the gods at [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt "Memphis, Egypt") and went to consult the famous oracle of [Amun-Ra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amun-Ra "Amun-Ra") at the [Siwa Oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siwa_Oasis "Siwa Oasis") in the [Libyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Libya "Ancient Libya") desert, at which he was pronounced the son of the deity Amun. Henceforth, Alexander often referred to [Zeus-Ammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus-Ammon "Zeus-Ammon") as his true father, and after his death, [currency depicted him adorned with horns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horns_of_Alexander "Horns of Alexander"), using the [Horns of Ammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horns_of_Ammon "Horns of Ammon") as a symbol of his divinity. The Greeks interpreted this message – one that the gods addressed to all pharaohs – as a prophecy.

Egypt was only one of a large number of territories taken by Alexander from the Persians. After his trip to Siwa, Alexander was crowned in the [temple of Ptah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Ptah "Temple of Ptah") at Memphis. It appears that the Egyptian people did not find it disturbing that he was a foreigner – nor that he was absent for virtually his entire reign. Alexander restored the temples neglected by the Persians and dedicated new monuments to the Egyptian gods. In the [temple of Luxor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Luxor "Temple of Luxor"), near [Karnak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnak "Karnak"), he built a chapel for the sacred barge. During his brief months in Egypt, he reformed the taxation system on the Greek models and organized the military occupation of the country, but in early 331 BC he left for Asia in pursuit of the Persians.

During his stay in Egypt, he founded [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"), which would become the prosperous capital of the [Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom "Ptolemaic Kingdom") after his death. Control of Egypt passed to Ptolemy I (son of Lagos), the founder of the Ptolemaic Dynasty (305–30 BC) after the death of Alexander.

### Assyria and Babylonia

Leaving Egypt in 331 BC, Alexander marched eastward into [Achaemenid Assyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Assyria "Achaemenid Assyria") in [Upper Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Mesopotamia "Upper Mesopotamia") (now northern [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq")) and defeated Darius again at the [Battle of Gaugamela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gaugamela "Battle of Gaugamela"). Darius once more fled the field, and Alexander chased him as far as [Arbela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erbil "Erbil"). Gaugamela would be the final and decisive encounter between the two. Darius fled over the mountains to [Ecbatana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecbatana "Ecbatana") (modern [Hamadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamadan "Hamadan")) while Alexander captured [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon").

[Babylonian astronomical diaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomical_diaries "Babylonian astronomical diaries") say that "the king of the world, Alexander" sent his scouts with a message to the people of Babylon before entering the city: "I shall not enter your houses".

### Persia

From Babylon, Alexander went to [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa"), one of the Achaemenid capitals, and captured its treasury. He sent the bulk of his army to the Persian ceremonial capital of [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis "Persepolis") via the Persian [Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road "Royal Road"). Alexander himself took selected troops on the direct route to the city. He then stormed the pass of the [Persian Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gates "Persian Gates") (in the modern [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains "Zagros Mountains")) which had been blocked by a Persian army under [Ariobarzanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariobarzanes_(satrap_of_Persis) "Ariobarzanes (satrap of Persis)") and then hurried to Persepolis before its garrison could loot the treasury.

On entering Persepolis, Alexander allowed his troops to loot the city for several days. Alexander stayed in Persepolis for five months. During his stay, a fire broke out in the eastern palace of [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I "Xerxes I") and spread to the rest of the city. Possible causes include a drunken accident or deliberate revenge for the burning of the [Acropolis of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_of_Athens "Acropolis of Athens") during the [Second Persian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars "Greco-Persian Wars") by Xerxes; Plutarch and [Diodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus "Diodorus") allege that Alexander's companion, the [hetaera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetaera "Hetaera") [Thaïs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tha%C3%AFs "Thaïs"), instigated and started the fire. Even as he watched the city burn, Alexander immediately began to regret his decision. Plutarch claims that he ordered his men to put out the fires but the flames had already spread to most of the city. [Curtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Curtius_Rufus "Quintus Curtius Rufus") claims that Alexander did not regret his decision until the next morning. Plutarch recounts an anecdote in which Alexander pauses and talks to a fallen statue of Xerxes as if it were a live person:

> Shall I pass by and leave you lying there because of the expeditions you led against Greece, or shall I set you up again because of your magnanimity and your virtues in other respects?

### Fall of the Persian Empire and the East

Alexander then chased Darius, first into [Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_(region) "Media (region)"), and then [Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia "Parthia"). The Persian king no longer controlled his own destiny, and was taken prisoner by [Bessus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessus "Bessus"), his [Bactrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria "Bactria") satrap and kinsman. As Alexander approached, Bessus had his men fatally stab the Great King and then declared himself Darius's successor as Artaxerxes V, before retreating into Central Asia to launch a [guerrilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerrilla "Guerrilla") campaign against Alexander. Alexander buried Darius's remains next to his Achaemenid predecessors in a regal funeral. He claimed that, while dying, Darius had named him as his successor to the Achaemenid throne. The Achaemenid Empire is normally considered to have fallen with Darius. However, as basic forms of community life and the general structure of government were maintained and resuscitated by Alexander under his own rule, he, in the words of the [Iranologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_studies "Iranian studies") [Pierre Briant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Briant "Pierre Briant") "may therefore be considered to have acted in many ways as the *last of the Achaemenids*."

Alexander viewed Bessus as an usurper and set out to defeat him. This campaign, initially against Bessus, turned into a grand tour of central Asia. Alexander founded a series of new cities, all called Alexandria, including [Alexandria Arachosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria_Arachosia "Alexandria Arachosia") at modern [Kandahar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar "Kandahar") in Afghanistan, and [Alexandria Eschate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria_Eschate "Alexandria Eschate") ("The Furthest") in modern [Tajikistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tajikistan "Tajikistan"). The campaign took Alexander through a number of satrapies in present-day Afghanistan and Central Asia, including Media, Parthia, [Aria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aria_(satrapy) "Aria (satrapy)"), [Drangiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drangiana "Drangiana"), [Arachosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arachosia "Arachosia"), Bactria, and [Scythia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythia "Scythia").

In 329 BC, [Spitamenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spitamenes "Spitamenes"), who held an undefined position in the satrapy of [Sogdiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Sogdia "Achaemenid Sogdia"), betrayed Bessus to Ptolemy, one of Alexander's trusted companions, and Bessus was executed. However, at some point later when Alexander was on the [Jaxartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaxartes "Jaxartes") dealing with an incursion by a horse nomad army, Spitamenes raised Sogdiana in revolt. Alexander personally defeated the Scythians at the [Battle of Jaxartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jaxartes "Battle of Jaxartes") and immediately launched a campaign against Spitamenes, defeating him in the Battle of Gabai. After the defeat, Spitamenes was killed by his own men, who then sued for peace.

### Problems and plots

During this time, Alexander adopted some elements of Persian dress and customs at his court, notably the custom of *[proskynesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proskynesis "Proskynesis")*, either a symbolic kissing of the hand, or prostration on the ground, that Persians showed to their social superiors. This was one aspect of Alexander's broad strategy aimed at securing the aid and support of the Iranian upper classes. The Greeks however regarded the gesture of *proskynesis* as the province of [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deities "Deities") and believed that Alexander meant to deify himself by requiring it. This cost him the sympathies of many of his countrymen, and he eventually abandoned it.

During the long rule of the Achaemenids, the elite positions in many segments of the empire including the central government, the army, and the many satrapies were specifically reserved for [Iranians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples "Iranian peoples") and to a major degree, [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians "Persians") noblemen. The latter were in many cases additionally connected through marriage alliances with the royal Achaemenid family. This created a problem for Alexander as to whether he had to make use of the various segments and people that had given the empire its solidity and unity for a lengthy period of time. Pierre Briant explains that Alexander realized that it was insufficient to merely exploit the internal contradictions within the imperial system as in Asia Minor, Babylonia or Egypt; he also had to (re)create a central government with or without the support of the Iranians. As early as 334 BC he demonstrated awareness of this, when he challenged incumbent King Darius III "by appropriating the main elements of the Achaemenid monarchy's ideology, particularly the theme of the king who protects the lands and the peasants". Alexander wrote a letter in 332 BC to Darius III, wherein he argued that he was worthier than Darius "to succeed to the Achaemenid throne". However, Alexander's eventual decision to burn the Achaemenid palace at Persepolis in conjunction with the major rejection and opposition of the "entire Persian people" made it impracticable for him to pose himself as Darius's legitimate successor. Against Bessus (Artaxerxes V) however, Briant adds, Alexander reasserted "his claim to legitimacy as the avenger of Darius III".

A plot against his life was revealed, and one of his officers, [Philotas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philotas "Philotas"), was executed for failing to alert Alexander. The death of the son necessitated the death of the father, and thus [Parmenion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmenion "Parmenion"), who had been charged with guarding the treasury at Ecbatana, was assassinated at Alexander's command, to prevent attempts at vengeance. Most infamously, Alexander personally killed the man who had saved his life at Granicus, [Cleitus the Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleitus_the_Black "Cleitus the Black"), during a violent drunken altercation at Maracanda (modern-day [Samarkand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarkand "Samarkand") in [Uzbekistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzbekistan "Uzbekistan")), in which Cleitus accused Alexander of several judgmental mistakes and especially of having forgotten the Macedonian ways in favour of a corrupt oriental lifestyle.

Later, in the Central Asian campaign, a second plot against his life was revealed. This one was instigated by his own royal [pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Page_(servant) "Page (servant)"). His official historian, [Callisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callisthenes "Callisthenes") of [Olynthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olynthus "Olynthus"), was implicated in the plot, and in the *[Anabasis of Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anabasis_of_Alexander "Anabasis of Alexander")*, [Arrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrian "Arrian") states that Callisthenes and the pages were then tortured on the [rack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rack_(torture) "Rack (torture)") as punishment, and likely died soon after. It remains unclear if Callisthenes was actually involved in the plot, for prior to his accusation he had fallen out of favour by leading the opposition to the attempt to introduce proskynesis.

### Macedon in Alexander's absence

When Alexander set out for Asia, he left his general [Antipater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipater "Antipater"), an experienced military and political leader, and part of Philip II's "Old Guard", in charge of Macedon. Alexander's sacking of Thebes ensured that Greece remained quiet during his absence. The one exception was a call to arms by Spartan king [Agis III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agis_III "Agis III") in 331 BC, whom Antipater defeated and killed in the [Battle of Megalopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Megalopolis "Battle of Megalopolis"). Antipater referred the Spartans' punishment to the League of Corinth, which then deferred to Alexander, who chose to pardon them. There was also considerable friction between Antipater and Olympias, and each complained to Alexander about the other.

In general, Greece enjoyed a period of peace and prosperity during Alexander's campaign in Asia. Alexander sent back vast sums from his conquest, which stimulated the economy and increased trade across his empire. However, Alexander's constant demands for troops and the migration of Macedonians throughout his empire depleted Macedon's strength, greatly weakening it in the years after Alexander, and ultimately led to its subjugation by Rome after the [Third Macedonian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Macedonian_War "Third Macedonian War") (171–168 BC).

### Coinage

The conquest by Philip II of [Pangaeum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangaeum "Pangaeum"), and then of the island of [Thasos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thasos "Thasos") between 356 and 342 BC brought rich gold and silver mines under Macedonian control.

Alexander appears to have introduced a new coinage in [Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia "Cilicia") in [Tarsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarsus,_Mersin "Tarsus, Mersin"), after the Battle of Issus in 333 BC, which went on to become the main coinage of the empire. Alexander minted gold [staters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stater "Stater"), silver [tetradrachms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetradrachm "Tetradrachm") and [drachims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_drachma "Ancient drachma"), and various fractional [bronze coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_coinage "Ancient Greek coinage"). The types of these coins remained constant in his empire. The gold series had the head of [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena "Athena") on the obverse and a winged [Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nike_(mythology) "Nike (mythology)") ([Victory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_(mythology) "Victoria (mythology)")) on the reverse. The silver coinage had a beardless head of [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles "Heracles") wearing a lionskin headdress on the obverse and Zeus aetophoros ('eagle bearer') enthroned with a scepter in his left hand, on the reverse. There are both Greek and non-Greek aspects to this design. Heracles and Zeus were important deities for the Macedonians, with Heracles considered to be the ancestor of the Temenid dynasty and Zeus the patron of the main Macedonian sanctuary, [Dium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dium "Dium"). The lion was also the symbolic animal of the Anatolian god [Sandas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandas "Sandas"), worshipped at Tarsus. The reverse design of Alexander's tetradrachms is closely modelled on the depiction of the god [Baaltars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baaltars "Baaltars") (Baal of Tarsus), on the silver staters minted at Tarsus by the Persian satrap [Mazaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazaeus "Mazaeus") before Alexander's conquest.

Alexander did not attempt to impose uniform imperial coinage throughout his new conquests. Persian coins continued to circulate in all the satrapies of the empire.

## Indian campaign

### Forays into the Indian subcontinent

After the death of [Spitamenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spitamenes "Spitamenes") and his marriage to Roxana (Raoxshna in [Old Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Iranian "Old Iranian")) to cement relations with his new satrapies, Alexander turned to the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"). He invited the chieftains of the former satrapy of [Gandāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gand%C4%81ra "Gandāra") (in the present-day [Peshawar Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshawar_Valley "Peshawar Valley") of Pakistan), to come to him and submit to his authority. [Omphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omphis "Omphis") (also known as Taxiles), the ruler of [Taxila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxila "Taxila"), whose kingdom extended from the Indus to the [Hydaspes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydaspes "Hydaspes") (Jhelum), complied, but the chieftains of some hill clans, including the [Aspasioi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspasioi "Aspasioi") and [Assakenoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assakenoi "Assakenoi"), refused to submit.

In the winter of 327/326 BC, Alexander personally led [Cophen campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cophen_campaign "Cophen campaign") against the Aspasioi of the [Kunar Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunar_Valley "Kunar Valley"), the [Guraeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guraeans "Guraeans") of the [Panjkora Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjkora_Valley "Panjkora Valley"), and the Assakenoi of the [Swat Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swat_Valley "Swat Valley"). A fierce contest ensued with the Aspasioi in which Alexander was wounded in the shoulder by a dart, but eventually the Aspasioi lost. Alexander then faced the Assakenoi who fought against him from the strongholds of [Massaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingora "Mingora"), [Ora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odigram "Odigram"), and [Aornos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aornos "Aornos").

The fort of Massaga was reduced after days of bloody fighting in which Alexander was seriously wounded in the ankle. According to [Curtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Curtius_Rufus "Quintus Curtius Rufus"), "Not only did Alexander slaughter the entire population of Massaga, but also did he reduce its buildings to rubble." A similar massacre followed at Ora. In the aftermath of Massaga and Ora, numerous Assakenians fled to the fortress of Aornos. Alexander followed close behind and captured the strategic hill-fort after four bloody days. After the death of Assakenoi leader, his mother, queen [Cleophis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleophis "Cleophis"), submitted to Alexander.

After Aornos, Alexander crossed the Indus. Omphis hastened to relieve Alexander of his apprehension and met him with valuable presents, placing himself and all his forces at his disposal. Alexander not only returned Omphis his title and the gifts but he also presented him with a wardrobe of "Persian robes, gold and silver ornaments, 30 horses and 1,000 talents in gold". Alexander was emboldened to divide his forces, and Omphis assisted Hephaestion and Perdiccas in constructing a bridge over the Indus where it bends at [Udabhanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udabhanda "Udabhanda"), supplied their troops with provisions, and he received Alexander and his whole army in his capital city of Taxila, with every demonstration of friendship and the most liberal hospitality.

On the subsequent advance of the Macedonian king, Omphis accompanied him with a force of 5,000 men and took part in the [Battle of the Hydaspes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Hydaspes "Battle of the Hydaspes") against the King [Porus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porus "Porus"), who ruled territory lying between the Hydaspes and the [Acesines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenab_River "Chenab River") (Chenab) in the [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab "Punjab"), in 326 BC. Alexander won an epic battle against Porus. Omphis was sent by Alexander in pursuit of Porus, to whom he was charged to offer favourable terms, but narrowly escaped losing his life at the hands of his old enemy. Subsequently, the two rivals were reconciled by the personal mediation of Alexander; Omphis contributed zealously to the equipment of the fleet on the Hydaspes and was entrusted by Alexander with the government of the whole territory between that river and the Indus. Alexander was also impressed by Porus's bravery and made him an ally. He appointed Porus as satrap, and added to Porus's territory land that he did not previously own, towards the south-east, up to the Hyphasis ([Beas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beas "Beas")). Choosing local rulers helped him control these lands that were distant from Greece. A considerable accession of power was granted to Porus after the death of [Philip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_(son_of_Machatas) "Philip (son of Machatas)"), son of Machatas, and he was allowed to retain his authority at the death of Alexander himself (323 BC), as well as in the subsequent partition of the provinces at [Triparadisus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Triparadisus "Treaty of Triparadisus"), 321 BC.

Alexander founded two cities on opposite sides of the Hydaspes river, naming one [Bucephala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria_Bucephalous "Alexandria Bucephalous"), in honour of his horse, who died around this time. The other was [Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicaea_(Punjab) "Nicaea (Punjab)") (Victory), believed to be located at the site of modern-day [Mong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mong,_Punjab "Mong, Punjab"). [Philostratus the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philostratus_the_Elder "Philostratus the Elder") in the *[Life of Apollonius of Tyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_of_Apollonius_of_Tyana "Life of Apollonius of Tyana")* writes that in the army of Porus, there was an elephant who fought bravely against Alexander's army, and Alexander dedicated it to the [Helios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helios "Helios") (Sun) and named it Ajax because he thought that such a great animal deserved a great name. The elephant had gold rings around its tusks and an inscription was on them written in Greek: "Alexander the son of Zeus dedicates Ajax to the Helios" (ΑΛΕΞΑΝΔΡΟΣ Ο ΔΙΟΣ ΤΟΝ ΑΙΑΝΤΑ ΤΩΙ ΗΛΙΩΙ).

### Revolt of the Hellenic army

East of Porus's kingdom, near the [Ganges River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_River "Ganges River"), was the [Nanda Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanda_Empire "Nanda Empire") of [Magadha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magadha "Magadha"), and further east, the [Gangaridai Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangaridai "Gangaridai") of the [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal "Bengal") region. Fearing the prospect of facing other large armies and exhausted by years of campaigning, Alexander's army mutinied at the Hyphasis River (Beas), refusing to march farther east. This river thus marks the easternmost extent of Alexander's conquests.

> As for the Macedonians, however, their struggle with Porus blunted their courage and stayed their further advance into India. For having had all they could do to repulse an enemy who mustered only twenty thousand infantry and two thousand horse, they violently opposed Alexander when he insisted on crossing the river Ganges also, the width of which, as they learned, was thirty-two furlongs [6.4 km], its depth one hundred fathoms [180 m], while its banks on the further side were covered with multitudes of [men-at-arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Men-at-arms "Men-at-arms") and horsemen and elephants. For they were told that the kings of the Ganderites and Praesii were awaiting them with eighty thousand horsemen, two hundred thousand footmen, eight thousand chariots, and six thousand [war elephants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_elephant "War elephant").

Alexander tried to persuade his soldiers to march farther, but his general [Coenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coenus_(general) "Coenus (general)") pleaded with him to change his opinion and return; the men, he said, "longed to again see their parents, their wives and children, their homeland". Alexander eventually agreed and turned south, marching along the Indus. Along the way his army conquered the territory inhabited by the [Mallians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallian_people "Mallian people") in the [Mallian campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallian_campaign "Mallian campaign"); while besieging the Mallian citadel, Alexander suffered a near-fatal injury when an arrow penetrated his armor and entered his lung.

Alexander sent much of his army to [Carmania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerman_province "Kerman province") (modern southern Iran) with general [Craterus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craterus "Craterus"), and commissioned a fleet to explore the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf") shore under his admiral [Nearchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearchus "Nearchus"), while he led the rest back to Persia through the more difficult southern route along the [Gedrosian Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gedrosia "Gedrosia") and [Makran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makran "Makran"). Alexander reached Susa in 324 BC, but not before losing many men to the harsh desert.

## Last years in Persia

Discovering that many of his satraps and military governors had misbehaved in his absence, Alexander executed several of them as examples on his way to [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa "Susa"). As a gesture of thanks, he paid off the debts of his soldiers, and announced he would send over-aged and disabled veterans back to Macedon, led by Craterus. His troops misunderstood his intention and mutinied at the town of [Opis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opis "Opis"). They refused to be sent away and criticized his adoption of Persian customs and dress and the introduction of Persian officers and soldiers into Macedonian units.

After three days, unable to persuade his men to back down, Alexander gave Persians command posts in the army and conferred Macedonian military titles upon Persian units. The Macedonians quickly begged forgiveness, which Alexander accepted, and held a great banquet with several thousand of his men. In an attempt to craft a lasting harmony between his Macedonian and Persian subjects, Alexander [held a mass marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa_weddings "Susa weddings") of his senior officers to Persian and other noblewomen at Susa, but few of those marriages seem to have lasted much beyond a year.

Meanwhile, upon his return to Persia, Alexander learned that guards of the [tomb of Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Cyrus_the_Great "Tomb of Cyrus the Great") in [Pasargadae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasargadae "Pasargadae") had desecrated it, and swiftly executed them. Alexander admired [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great"), from an early age reading [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon "Xenophon")'s *[Cyropaedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyropaedia "Cyropaedia")*, which described Cyrus's heroism in battle and governance as a king and legislator. During his visit to Pasargadae, Alexander ordered his architect [Aristobulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristobulus_of_Cassandreia "Aristobulus of Cassandreia") to decorate the interior of the sepulchral chamber of Cyrus's tomb.

Afterwards, Alexander travelled to Ecbatana to retrieve the bulk of the Persian treasure. There, his closest friend, Hephaestion, died of illness or poisoning. Hephaestion's death devastated Alexander and he ordered the preparation of an expensive [funeral pyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funeral_pyre "Funeral pyre") in Babylon along with a decree for public mourning. Back in Babylon, Alexander planned a series of new campaigns, beginning with an invasion of Arabia.

## Death and succession

On either 10 or 11 June 323 BC, Alexander died in the palace of [Nebuchadnezzar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II "Nebuchadnezzar II"), in Babylon, at age 32. There are two versions of Alexander's death, differing slightly in details. Plutarch's account is that roughly 14 days before his death, Alexander entertained admiral Nearchus and spent the night and next day drinking with [Medius of Larissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medius_of_Larissa "Medius of Larissa"). Alexander developed a fever, which worsened until he was unable to speak. The common soldiers, anxious about his health, were granted the right to file past him as he silently waved at them. In the second account, Diodorus recounts that Alexander was struck with pain after downing a large bowl of unmixed wine in honour of Heracles followed by 11 days of weakness; he did not develop a fever, instead dying after some agony. Arrian also mentioned this as an alternative, but Plutarch specifically denied this claim.

Given the propensity of the Macedonian aristocracy to assassination and Alexander's relatively young age, foul play featured in multiple accounts of his death. Diodorus, Plutarch, Arrian and [Justin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_(historian) "Justin (historian)") all mentioned the theory that Alexander was poisoned. Justin stated that Alexander was the victim of a poisoning conspiracy, Plutarch dismissed it as a fabrication, while both Diodorus and Arrian noted that they mentioned it only for the sake of completeness. The accounts were nevertheless fairly consistent in designating [Antipater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipater "Antipater"), recently removed as Macedonian viceroy, replaced by Craterus, as the head of the alleged plot. Perhaps taking his summons to Babylon as a death sentence and having seen the fate of Parmenion and Philotas, Antipater purportedly arranged for Alexander to be poisoned by his son Iollas, who was Alexander's wine-pourer. There was even a suggestion that Aristotle may have participated. The strongest argument against the poison theory is the fact that twelve days passed between the start of his illness and his death; such long-acting poisons were probably not available.

However, in a 2003 [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") documentary investigating the death of Alexander, Leo Schep from the New Zealand National Poisons Centre proposed that the plant white hellebore (*[Veratrum album](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veratrum_album "Veratrum album")*), which was known in antiquity, may have been used to poison Alexander. In a 2014 paper in the journal *[Clinical Toxicology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_Toxicology "Clinical Toxicology")*, Schep suggested Alexander's wine was spiked with *Veratrum album*, and that this would produce poisoning symptoms that match the course of events described in the *[Alexander Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Romance "Alexander Romance")*. *Veratrum album* poisoning can have a prolonged course and it was suggested that if Alexander was poisoned, *Veratrum album* offers the most plausible cause. Another poisoning explanation put forward in 2010 proposed that the circumstances of his death were compatible with poisoning by water of the river Styx (modern-day [Mavroneri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mavroneri "Mavroneri") in Arcadia, Greece) that contained [calicheamicin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calicheamicin "Calicheamicin"), a dangerous compound produced by bacteria.

Several [natural causes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_causes "Natural causes") (diseases) have been suggested, including [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria") and [typhoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid_fever "Typhoid fever"). A 1998 article in the *[New England Journal of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England_Journal_of_Medicine "New England Journal of Medicine")* attributed Alexander's death to typhoid fever complicated by [bowel perforation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowel_perforation "Bowel perforation") and ascending [paralysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralysis "Paralysis"). A 2004 analysis suggested pyogenic (infectious) [spondylitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spondylitis "Spondylitis") or [meningitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meningitis "Meningitis"). Other illnesses fit the symptoms, including [acute pancreatitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_pancreatitis "Acute pancreatitis"), [West Nile virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Nile_virus "West Nile virus"), and [Guillain-Barré syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillain-Barr%C3%A9_syndrome "Guillain-Barré syndrome"). Natural-cause theories also tend to emphasize that Alexander's health may have been in general decline after years of heavy drinking and severe wounds. The anguish that Alexander felt after Hephaestion's death may also have contributed to his declining health.

### Post-death events

Alexander's body was laid in a gold anthropoid [sarcophagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcophagus "Sarcophagus") that was filled with honey, which was in turn placed in a gold casket. According to [Aelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Aelianus "Claudius Aelianus"), a seer called Aristander foretold that the land where Alexander was laid to rest "would be happy and unvanquishable forever". Perhaps more likely, the successors may have seen possession of the body as a symbol of legitimacy, since burying the prior king was a [royal prerogative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_prerogative "Royal prerogative").

While Alexander's funeral cortege was on its way to Macedon, Ptolemy seized it and took it temporarily to Memphis. His successor, [Ptolemy II Philadelphus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_II_Philadelphus "Ptolemy II Philadelphus"), transferred the sarcophagus to Alexandria, where it remained until at least [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity"). [Ptolemy IX Lathyros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_IX_Lathyros "Ptolemy IX Lathyros"), one of Ptolemy's final successors, replaced Alexander's sarcophagus with a glass one so he could convert the original to coinage. The 2014 discovery of an [enormous tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasta_Tomb "Kasta Tomb") in northern Greece, at [Amphipolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphipolis "Amphipolis"), dating from the time of Alexander the Great has given rise to speculation that it was originally intended to be the burial place of Alexander. This would fit with the intended destination of Alexander's funeral cortege. However, the memorial was found to be dedicated to the dearest friend of Alexander the Great, Hephaestion.

[Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey "Pompey"), [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") and [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus "Augustus") all visited the tomb in Alexandria where Augustus, allegedly, accidentally knocked the nose of Alexander's mummified body off. [Caligula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caligula "Caligula") was said to have taken Alexander's breastplate from the tomb for his own use. Around AD 200, Emperor [Septimius Severus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septimius_Severus "Septimius Severus") closed Alexander's tomb to the public. His son and successor, [Caracalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caracalla "Caracalla"), a great admirer, visited the tomb during his own reign. After this, details on the fate of the tomb are hazy.

The so-called "[Alexander Sarcophagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Sarcophagus "Alexander Sarcophagus")", discovered near [Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidon "Sidon") and now in the [Istanbul Archaeology Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Archaeology_Museum "Istanbul Archaeology Museum"), is so named not because it was thought to have contained Alexander's remains, but because its [bas-reliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bas-relief "Bas-relief") depict Alexander and his companions fighting the Persians and hunting. It was originally thought to have been the sarcophagus of [Abdalonymus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdalonymus "Abdalonymus") (died 311 BC), the king of Sidon appointed by Alexander immediately following the Battle of Issus in 332. However, in 1969, it was suggested by [Karl Schefold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Schefold "Karl Schefold") that it may date from earlier than Abdalonymus's death.

[Demades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demades "Demades") likened the Macedonian army, after the death of Alexander, to the blinded [Cyclops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclops "Cyclops") due to the many random and disorderly movements that it made. In addition, [Leosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leosthenes "Leosthenes") also likened the anarchy between the generals, after Alexander's death, to the blinded Cyclops "who after he had lost his eye went feeling and groping about with his hands before him, not knowing where to lay them".

### Division of the Macedonian Empire

Alexander's death was so sudden that when reports of his death reached Greece, they were not immediately believed. Alexander had no obvious or legitimate heir, his son [Alexander IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_IV_of_Macedon "Alexander IV of Macedon") by Roxane being born after Alexander's death. According to Diodorus, Alexander's companions asked him on his deathbed to whom he bequeathed his kingdom; his [laconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laconic_phrase "Laconic phrase") reply was "tôi kratistôi"—"to the strongest". Another theory is that his successors wilfully or erroneously misheard "tôi Kraterôi"—"to Craterus", the general leading his Macedonian troops home and newly entrusted with the regency of Macedonia. Arrian and Plutarch claimed that Alexander was speechless by this time, implying that this was an apocryphal story. Diodorus, Curtius, and Justin offered the more plausible story that Alexander passed his [signet ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_(emblem) "Seal (emblem)") to [Perdiccas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdiccas "Perdiccas"), a bodyguard and leader of the companion cavalry, in front of witnesses, thereby nominating him.

Perdiccas initially did not claim power, instead suggesting that Roxane's baby would be king, if male, with himself, Craterus, Leonnatus, and Antipater as guardians. However, the infantry, under the command of [Meleager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meleager_(general) "Meleager (general)"), rejected this arrangement since they had been excluded from the discussion. Instead, they supported Alexander's half-brother Philip Arrhidaeus. Eventually, the two sides reconciled, and after the birth of Alexander IV, he and Philip III were appointed joint kings, albeit in name only. Dissension and rivalry soon affected the Macedonians. The satrapies handed out by Perdiccas at the [Partition of Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Babylon "Partition of Babylon") became power bases each general used to bid for power. After the assassination of Perdiccas in 321 BC, Macedonian unity collapsed, and 40 years of war between "The Successors" (*[Diadochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi "Diadochi")*) ensued before the Hellenistic world settled into three stable power blocs: [Ptolemaic Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt "Ptolemaic Egypt"), [Seleucid Syria and Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire "Seleucid Empire"), and [Antigonid Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigonid_dynasty "Antigonid dynasty"). In the process, both Alexander IV and Philip III were murdered.

### Last plans

Diodorus stated that Alexander had given detailed written instructions to Craterus some time before his death, which are known as Alexander's "last plans". Craterus started to carry out Alexander's commands, but the successors chose not to further implement them, on the grounds they were impractical and extravagant. Furthermore, Perdiccas had read the notebooks containing Alexander's last plans to the Macedonian troops in Babylon, who voted not to carry them out.

According to Diodorus, Alexander's last plans called for military expansion into the southern and western Mediterranean, monumental constructions, and the intermixing of Eastern and Western populations. It included:

* Construction of 1,000 ships larger than triremes, along with harbours and a road running along the African coast all the way to the [Pillars of Hercules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillars_of_Hercules "Pillars of Hercules"), to be used for an invasion of [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage "Ancient Carthage") and the western Mediterranean;
* Erection of great temples in [Delos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delos "Delos"), [Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi "Delphi"), [Dodona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodona "Dodona"), [Dium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dium "Dium"), [Amphipolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphipolis "Amphipolis"), all costing 1,500 [talents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_talent "Attic talent"), and a monumental temple to [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena "Athena") at [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy "Troy")
* Amalgamation of small settlements into larger cities ("[synoecisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoecism "Synoecism")") and the "transplant of populations from Asia to Europe and in the opposite direction from Europe to Asia, in order to bring the largest continent to common unity and to friendship by means of intermarriage and family ties"
* Construction of a monumental tomb for his father Philip, "to match the greatest of the [pyramids of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramids_of_Egypt "Pyramids of Egypt")"
* While not mentioned by Diodorus, the later historian Arrian mentioned that Alexander had planned to conquer [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia "Arabia")
* Circumnavigation of Africa

The enormous scale of these plans has led many scholars to doubt their historicity. [Ernst Badian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Badian "Ernst Badian") argued that they were exaggerated by Perdiccas in order to ensure that the Macedonian troops voted not to carry them out. Other scholars have proposed that they were invented by later authors within the tradition of the [Alexander Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Romance "Alexander Romance").

## Character

### Generalship

Alexander perhaps earned the epithet "the Great" due to his unparalleled success as a military commander; he never lost a battle, despite typically being outnumbered. This was due to use of terrain, [phalanx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phalanx "Phalanx") and cavalry tactics, bold strategy, and the fierce loyalty of his troops. The [Macedonian phalanx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_phalanx "Macedonian phalanx"), armed with the [sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa "Sarissa"), a spear 6 metres (20 ft) long, had been developed and perfected by Philip II through rigorous training, and Alexander used its speed and manoeuvrability to great effect against larger but more disparate Persian forces. Alexander also recognized the potential for disunity among his diverse army, which employed various languages and weapons. He overcame this by being personally involved in battle, in the manner of a Macedonian king.

In his first battle in Asia, at Granicus, Alexander used only a small part of his forces, perhaps 13,000 infantry with 5,000 cavalry, against a much larger Persian force of 40,000. Alexander placed the phalanx at the center and cavalry and archers on the wings, so that his line matched the length of the Persian cavalry line, about 3 km (1.86 mi). By contrast, the Persian infantry was stationed behind its cavalry. This ensured that Alexander would not be outflanked, while his phalanx, armed with long pikes, had a considerable advantage over the Persians' [scimitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scimitars "Scimitars") and [javelins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javelins "Javelins"). Macedonian losses were negligible compared to those of the Persians.

At Issus in 333 BC, his first confrontation with Darius, he used the same deployment, and again the central phalanx pushed through. Alexander personally led the charge in the center, routing the opposing army. At the decisive encounter with Darius at Gaugamela, Darius equipped his chariots with scythes on the wheels to break up the phalanx and equipped his cavalry with pikes. Alexander arranged a double phalanx, with the center advancing at an angle, parting when the chariots bore down and then reforming. The advance was successful and broke Darius's center, causing the latter to flee once again.

When faced with opponents who used unfamiliar fighting techniques, such as in Central Asia and India, Alexander adapted his forces to his opponents' style. Thus, in Bactria and Sogdiana, Alexander successfully used his javelin throwers and archers to prevent outflanking movements, while massing his cavalry at the center. In India, confronted by Porus's elephant corps, the Macedonians opened their ranks to envelop the elephants and used their sarissas to strike upwards and dislodge the elephants' handlers.

### Physical appearance

Historical sources frequently give conflicting accounts of Alexander's appearance, and the earliest sources are the most scant in their detail; for example, Arrian describes him simply as "very handsome". During his lifetime, Alexander carefully curated his image by commissioning works from famous and great artists of the time. This included commissioning sculptures by [Lysippos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysippos "Lysippos"), paintings by [Apelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apelles "Apelles") and gem engravings by [Pyrgoteles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrgoteles "Pyrgoteles"). Ancient authors recorded that Alexander was so pleased with portraits of himself created by Lysippos that he forbade other sculptors from crafting his image; scholars today, however, find the claim dubious. Andrew Stewart highlights the fact that artistic portraits, not least because of who they are commissioned by, are always partisan, and that artistic portrayals of Alexander "seek to legitimize him (or, by extension, his Successors), to interpret him to their audiences, to answer their critiques, and to persuade them of his greatness", and thus should be considered within a framework of "praise and blame", in the same way sources such as praise poetry are. Nevertheless, though idealised, Lysippos's sculpture was thought to be the most faithful [plastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_arts "Plastic arts") representation.

[Curtius Rufus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Curtius_Rufus "Quintus Curtius Rufus"), a Roman historian from the first century AD, who wrote the *[Histories of Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_of_Alexander_the_Great "Histories of Alexander the Great")*, gives this account of Alexander sitting on the throne of [Darius III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_III "Darius III"):

> Then Alexander seating himself on the royal throne, which was far too high for his bodily stature. Therefore, since his feet did not reach its lowest step, one of the royal pages placed a table under his feet.

Both Curtius and Diodorus report a story that when Darius III's mother, [Sisygambis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisygambis "Sisygambis"), first met Alexander and Hephaestion, she assumed that the latter was Alexander because he was the taller and more physically impressive of the two.

The Greek biographer Plutarch (c. 45 – c. 120 AD) discusses the accuracy of his depictions:

> The outward appearance of Alexander is best represented by the statues of him which Lysippus made, and it was by this artist alone that Alexander himself thought it fit that he should be modelled. For those peculiarities which many of his successors and friends afterwards tried to imitate, namely, the poise of the neck, which was bent slightly to the left, and the melting glance of his eyes, this artist has accurately observed. Apelles, however, in painting him as wielder of the thunder-bolt, did not reproduce his complexion, but made it too dark and swarthy. Whereas he was of a fair colour, as they say, and his fairness passed into ruddiness on his breast particularly, and in his face. Moreover, that a very pleasant odour exhaled from his skin and that there was a fragrance about his mouth and all his flesh, so that his garments were filled with it, this we have read in the *Memoirs of Aristoxenus*.

Historians have understood the detail of the pleasant fragrance attributed to Alexander as stemming from a belief in ancient Greece that pleasant scents are characteristic of gods and heroes.

The [Alexander Mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Mosaic "Alexander Mosaic") and contemporary coins portray Alexander with "a straight nose, a slightly protruding jaw, full lips and eyes deep set beneath a strongly pronounced forehead". He is also described as having a slight upward tilt of his head to the left.

The ancient historian [Aelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Aelianus "Claudius Aelianus") (c. 175 – c. 235 AD), in his *Varia Historia* (12.14), describes Alexander's hair color as "ξανθὴν" (*xanthen*), which at the time, could mean blond, brown or [auburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auburn_hair "Auburn hair"). It is sometimes claimed that Alexander had [one blue and one brown eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterochromia "Heterochromia"), referring to the *[Alexander Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Romance "Alexander Romance")*, which is however a fictional account that also claims Alexander "had sharp teeth like fangs" and "did not look like Philip or Olympias". Reconstruction, based on remaining traces of paint of the original [polychromy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychromy "Polychromy") on the Alexander Sarcophagus from Sidon, indicates that he was depicted with brown eyes and chestnut brown hair. The [Acropolis Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_Museum "Acropolis Museum") suggests that trace amounts of red paint on a head statue of Alexander found in 1886 were most likely a base coat for golden hues to be painted over for his hair. The Pella mosaic of a lion hunt (late 4th century BC) depicts him as having blond hair, with empty spots where their eye stones (which would've been used with semi-precious gems) having been plucked out.

### Personality

Both of Alexander's parents encouraged his ambitions. His father Philip was probably Alexander's most immediate and influential role model, as the young Alexander watched him campaign practically every year, winning victory after victory while ignoring severe wounds. Alexander's relationship with his father "forged" the competitive side of his personality; he had a need to outdo his father, illustrated by his reckless behavior in battle. While Alexander worried that his father would leave him "no great or brilliant achievement to be displayed to the world", he also downplayed his father's achievements to his companions. Alexander's mother Olympias similarly had huge ambitions, and encouraged her son to believe it was his destiny to conquer the Persian Empire. She instilled a sense of destiny in him, and Plutarch tells how his ambition "kept his spirit serious and lofty in advance of his years".

According to Plutarch, Alexander also had a violent temper and rash, impulsive nature, which could influence his decision making. Although Alexander was stubborn and did not respond well to orders from his father, he was open to reasoned debate. He had a calmer side—perceptive, logical, and calculating. He had a great desire for knowledge, a love for philosophy, and was an avid reader. This was no doubt in part due to Aristotle's tutelage; Alexander was intelligent and quick to learn. His intelligent and rational side was amply demonstrated by his ability and success as a general. He had great self-restraint in "pleasures of the body", in contrast with his lack of [self-control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-control "Self-control") with alcohol.

Alexander was erudite and patronized both arts and sciences. However, he had little interest in sports or the [Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games "Ancient Olympic Games") (unlike his father), seeking only the [Homeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric "Homeric") ideals of honour (*timê*) and glory (*kudos*). He had great [charisma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charisma "Charisma") and force of personality, characteristics which made him a great leader. His unique abilities were further demonstrated by the inability of any of his generals to unite Macedonia and retain the Empire after his death—only Alexander had the ability to do so.

During his final years, and especially after the death of Hephaestion, Alexander began to exhibit signs of [megalomania](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/megalomania "wikt:megalomania") and [paranoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranoia "Paranoia"). His extraordinary achievements, coupled with his own ineffable sense of destiny and the flattery of his companions, may have combined to produce this effect. His [delusions of grandeur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delusions_of_grandeur "Delusions of grandeur") are readily visible in his [will](#Will) and in his desire to conquer the world, in as much as he is by various sources described as having *boundless ambition*, an epithet, the meaning of which has descended into a historical cliché.

He appears to have believed himself a deity, or at least sought to deify himself. Olympias always insisted to him that he was the son of Zeus, an idea apparently confirmed to him by the oracle of Amun at Siwa. He began to identify himself as the son of Zeus-Ammon. Alexander adopted elements of Persian dress and customs at court, notably *[proskynesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proskynesis "Proskynesis")*, which was one aspect of Alexander's broad strategy aimed at securing the aid and support of the Iranian upper classes; however the practise of *proskynesis* was disapproved by the Macedonians, and they were unwilling to perform it. This behaviour cost him the sympathies of many of his countrymen. Alexander also was a pragmatic ruler who understood the difficulties of ruling culturally disparate peoples, many of whom lived in societies where the king was treated as divine. Thus, rather than megalomania, his behaviour may have been a practical attempt at strengthening his rule and keeping his empire together.

### Personal relationships

Alexander married three times: [Roxana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roxana "Roxana"), daughter of the Sogdian nobleman [Oxyartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyartes "Oxyartes") of Bactria, out of love; the Persian princesses [Stateira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stateira_(wife_of_Alexander_the_Great) "Stateira (wife of Alexander the Great)") and [Parysatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parysatis_II "Parysatis II"), the former a daughter of Darius III and the latter a daughter of [Artaxerxes III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artaxerxes_III "Artaxerxes III"), for political reasons. Alexander apparently had two children by Roxana: a son, who was born in India and died in infancy in November 326 BC, and [Alexander IV of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_IV_of_Macedon "Alexander IV of Macedon"), born after his father's death. Additionally [Heracles of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles_of_Macedon "Heracles of Macedon") was claimed to be his illegitimate son born of mistress, [Barsine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barsine "Barsine").

Alexander also had a close relationship with his friend, general, and bodyguard Hephaestion, the son of a Macedonian noble. Hephaestion's death devastated Alexander. This event may have contributed to Alexander's failing health and detached [mental state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_health "Mental health") during his final months.

### Sexuality

Alexander's sexuality has been the subject of speculation and controversy in modern times. The Roman era writer [Athenaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenaeus "Athenaeus") says, based on the scholar [Dicaearchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicaearchus "Dicaearchus"), who was Alexander's contemporary, that the king "was quite excessively keen on boys", and that Alexander kissed the [eunuch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eunuch "Eunuch") [Bagoas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagoas_(courtier) "Bagoas (courtier)") in public. This episode is also told by Plutarch, probably based on the same source. Historian [William Woodthorpe Tarn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Woodthorpe_Tarn "William Woodthorpe Tarn") rejected the stories of Bagoas as fabricated in ancient times to defame Alexander, mainly referring to the [Rufus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Curtius_Rufus "Quintus Curtius Rufus")'s fairly fictionalized biography of Alexander that criticized the Macedonian's "degeneration" in embracing foreign Persian customs. However, in 1958 Badian countered Tarn's arguments, though his concern was the issue of the reliability of sources for Alexander rather than the figure of the eunuch himself. None of Alexander's contemporaries, however, are known to have explicitly described Alexander's relationship with Hephaestion as sexual, though the pair was often compared to [Achilles and Patroclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles_and_Patroclus "Achilles and Patroclus"), who are often interpreted as a couple. Aelian writes of Alexander's visit to [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy "Troy") where "Alexander garlanded the tomb of Achilles, and Hephaestion that of [Patroclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patroclus "Patroclus"), the latter hinting that he was a beloved of Alexander, in just the same way as Patroclus was of Achilles." At the same time, ancient writers did not conclusively identify them as lovers. Some modern historians (e.g., [Robin Lane Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Lane_Fox "Robin Lane Fox")) believe not only that Alexander's youthful relationship with Hephaestion was sexual, but also that their sexual contacts may have continued into adulthood, which went against the social norms of at least some Greek cities, such as Athens. However, some modern researchers have tentatively proposed that Macedonia (or at least the Macedonian court) may have been more tolerant of homosexuality between adults.

[Peter Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Green_(historian) "Peter Green (historian)") argues that there is little evidence in ancient sources that Alexander had much sexual interest in women; he did not produce an heir until the very end of his life. However, Ogden calculates that Alexander, who impregnated his partners three times in eight years, had fathered more children than his father at the same age. Two of these pregnancies—Stateira's and Barsine's—are of dubious legitimacy.

According to Diodorus Siculus, Alexander accumulated a harem in the style of Persian kings, but he used it rather sparingly, "not wishing to offend the Macedonians", showing great self-control in "pleasures of the body". Nevertheless, Plutarch described how Alexander was infatuated by Roxana while complimenting him on not forcing himself on her. Green suggested that, in the context of the period, Alexander formed quite strong friendships with women, including [Ada of Caria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_of_Caria "Ada of Caria"), who adopted him, and even Darius's mother [Sisygambis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisygambis "Sisygambis"), who supposedly died from grief upon hearing of Alexander's death.

## Legacy

Alexander's legacy extended beyond his military conquests, and his reign marked a turning point in European and Asian history. His campaigns greatly increased contacts and trade between [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_world "Eastern world") and [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world"), and vast areas to the east were significantly exposed to [Greek civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_civilization "Greek civilization") and influence. Some of the cities he founded became major cultural centers, many surviving into the 21st century. His chroniclers recorded valuable information about the areas through which he marched, while the Greeks themselves got a sense of belonging to a world beyond the Mediterranean.

### Hellenistic kingdoms

Alexander's most immediate legacy was the introduction of Macedonian rule to huge new swathes of Asia. At the time of his death, Alexander's empire covered some 5,200,000 km2 (2,000,000 sq mi), and was the largest state of its time. Many of these areas remained in Macedonian hands or under Greek influence for the next 200–300 years. The successor states that emerged were, at least initially, dominant forces, and these 300 years are often referred to as the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period").

The eastern borders of Alexander's empire began to collapse even during his lifetime. However, the power vacuum he left in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent directly gave rise to one of the most powerful Indian dynasties in history, the [Maurya Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya_Empire "Maurya Empire"). Taking advantage of this power vacuum, [Chandragupta Maurya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandragupta_Maurya "Chandragupta Maurya") (referred to in Greek sources as "Sandrokottos"), of relatively humble origin, took control of the Punjab, and with that power base proceeded to conquer the Nanda Empire.

### Founding of cities

Over the course of his conquests, Alexander founded [many cities that bore his name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_founded_by_Alexander_the_Great "List of cities founded by Alexander the Great"), most of them east of the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris"). The first, and greatest, was Alexandria in Egypt, which would become one of the leading Mediterranean cities. The cities' locations reflected trade routes as well as defensive positions. At first, the cities must have been inhospitable, little more than defensive garrisons. Following Alexander's death, many Greeks who had settled there tried to return to Greece. However, a century or so after Alexander's death, many of the Alexandrias were thriving, with elaborate public buildings and substantial populations that included both Greek and local peoples.

### Funding of temples

In 334 BC, Alexander the Great donated funds for the completion of the new temple of [Athena Polias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena_Polias "Athena Polias") in [Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene "Priene"), in modern-day western Turkey. An [inscription from the temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene_inscription_of_Alexander_the_Great "Priene inscription of Alexander the Great"), now housed in the [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum "British Museum"), declares: "King Alexander dedicated [this temple] to Athena Polias." This inscription is one of the few independent archaeological discoveries confirming an episode from Alexander's life. The temple was designed by [Pytheos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythius_of_Priene "Pythius of Priene"), one of the architects of the [Mausoleum at Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum_at_Halicarnassus "Mausoleum at Halicarnassus").

[Libanius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libanius "Libanius") wrote that Alexander founded the temple of Zeus Bottiaios ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): Βοττιαίου Δῖός), in the place where later the city of [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch "Antioch") was built. Similarly [Suda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suda "Suda") wrote that Alexander built a large temple to [Sarapis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarapis "Sarapis").

In 2023, [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum "British Museum") experts have suggested the possibility that a Greek temple at [Girsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girsu "Girsu") in Iraq, was founded by Alexander. According to the researchers, recent discoveries suggest that "this site honours Zeus and two divine sons. The sons are Heracles and Alexander."

### Hellenization

The term *Hellenization* was coined by the German historian [Johann Gustav Droysen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Gustav_Droysen "Johann Gustav Droysen") to denote the spread of Greek language, culture, and population into the former Persian empire after Alexander's conquest. This process can be seen in such great Hellenistic cities as Alexandria, Antioch and [Seleucia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucia "Seleucia") (south of modern [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad")). Alexander sought to insert Greek elements into [Persian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_culture "Persian culture") and to hybridize Greek and Persian culture, homogenizing the populations of Asia and Europe. Although his successors explicitly rejected such policies, Hellenization occurred throughout the region, accompanied by a distinct and opposite 'Orientalization' of the successor states.

The core of the Hellenistic culture promulgated by the conquests was essentially [Athenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian "Athenian"). The close association of men from across Greece in Alexander's army directly led to the emergence of the largely [Attic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_Greek "Attic Greek")-based "[koine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine "Koine")", or "common" Greek dialect. Koine spread throughout the Hellenistic world, becoming the [lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca "Lingua franca") of Hellenistic lands, and eventually the ancestor of [modern Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek "Modern Greek"). Furthermore, [town planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_planning "Town planning"), education, local government, and art current in the Hellenistic period were all based on Classical Greek ideals, evolving into distinct new forms commonly grouped as Hellenistic. Also, the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament") was written in the [Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek "Koine Greek") language. Aspects of Hellenistic culture were still evident in the traditions of the Byzantine Empire in the mid-15th century.

#### Hellenization in South and Central Asia

Some of the most pronounced effects of Hellenization can be seen in Afghanistan and India, in the region of the relatively late-rising [Greco-Bactrian Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_Kingdom "Greco-Bactrian Kingdom") (250–125 BC) (in modern Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Tajikistan) and the [Indo-Greek Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom "Indo-Greek Kingdom") (180 BC – 10 AD) in modern Afghanistan, Pakistan and India. On the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road") trade routes, Hellenistic culture hybridized with Iranian and [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist "Buddhist") cultures. The cosmopolitan art and mythology of [Gandhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara "Gandhara") (a region spanning the upper confluence of the Indus, Swat and Kabul rivers in modern Pakistan) of the ~3rd century BC to the ~5th century AD are most evident of the direct contact between Hellenistic civilization and South Asia, as are the [Edicts of Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka "Edicts of Ashoka"), which directly mention the Greeks within Ashoka's dominion as converting to Buddhism and the reception of Buddhist emissaries by Ashoka's contemporaries in the Hellenistic world. The resulting [syncretism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism") known as [Greco-Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhism "Greco-Buddhism") influenced the development of Buddhism and created a culture of [Greco-Buddhist art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art "Greco-Buddhist art"). These Greco-Buddhist kingdoms sent some of the first Buddhist missionaries to [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China"), [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") and Hellenistic Asia and Europe ([Greco-Buddhist monasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_monasticism "Greco-Buddhist monasticism")).

Some of the first and most influential figurative portrayals of [The Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha "The Buddha") appeared at this time, perhaps modelled on Greek statues of [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo "Apollo") in the Greco-Buddhist style. Several Buddhist traditions may have been influenced by the [ancient Greek religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_religion "Ancient Greek religion"): the concept of [Boddhisatvas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boddhisatvas "Boddhisatvas") is reminiscent of Greek divine heroes, and some [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana "Mahayana") [ceremonial practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offering_(Buddhism) "Offering (Buddhism)") (burning [incense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incense "Incense"), gifts of flowers, and food placed on altars) are similar to those practised by the ancient Greeks; however, similar practices were also observed amongst the native Indic culture. One Greek king, [Menander I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menander_I "Menander I"), probably became Buddhist, and was immortalized in [Buddhist literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_literature "Buddhist literature") as 'Milinda'. The process of Hellenization also spurred trade between the east and west. For example, Greek astronomical instruments dating to the 3rd century BC were found in the Greco-Bactrian city of [Ai Khanoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ai_Khanoum "Ai Khanoum") in modern-day Afghanistan, while the Greek concept of a [spherical Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_Earth "Spherical Earth") surrounded by the spheres of planets eventually supplanted the long-standing Indian cosmological belief of a disc consisting of four continents grouped around a central mountain (Mount Meru) like the petals of a flower. The [Yavanajataka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yavanajataka "Yavanajataka") (lit. 'Greek astronomical treatise') and [Paulisa Siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulisa_Siddhanta "Paulisa Siddhanta") texts depict the influence of Greek astronomical ideas on Indian astronomy.

Following the conquests of Alexander the Great in the east, [Hellenistic influence on Indian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_influence_on_Indian_art "Hellenistic influence on Indian art") was far-reaching. In [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"), a few examples of the [Ionic order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_order "Ionic order") can be found as far as Pakistan with the [Jandial temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jandial_temple "Jandial temple") near Taxila. Several examples of [capitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_(architecture) "Capital (architecture)") displaying Ionic influences can be seen as far as [Patna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patna "Patna"), especially with the [Pataliputra capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pataliputra_capital "Pataliputra capital"), dated to the 3rd century BC. The [Corinthian order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_order "Corinthian order") is also heavily represented in the [art of Gandhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Gandhara "Art of Gandhara"), especially through [Indo-Corinthian capitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Corinthian_capital "Indo-Corinthian capital").

### Influence on Rome

Alexander and his exploits were admired by many Romans, especially generals, who wanted to associate themselves with his achievements. [Polybius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybius "Polybius") began his *[Histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_(Polybius) "Histories (Polybius)")* by reminding Romans of Alexander's achievements, and thereafter Roman leaders saw him as a role model. [Pompey the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey_the_Great "Pompey the Great") adopted the epithet "Magnus" and even Alexander's anastole-type haircut, and searched the conquered lands of the east for Alexander's 260-year-old cloak, which he then wore as a sign of greatness. [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") dedicated a [Lysippean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysippus "Lysippus") [equestrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrian_statue "Equestrian statue") [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze "Bronze") statue, but replaced Alexander's head with his own, while [Octavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavian "Octavian") visited Alexander's tomb in Alexandria and temporarily changed his seal from a [sphinx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphinx "Sphinx") to Alexander's profile. The emperor [Trajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajan "Trajan") also admired Alexander, as did [Nero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero "Nero") and [Caracalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caracalla "Caracalla"). The Macriani, a Roman family that in the person of [Macrinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrinus "Macrinus") briefly ascended to the imperial throne, kept images of Alexander on their persons, either on jewellery or embroidered into their clothes.

On the other hand, some Roman writers, particularly Republican figures, used Alexander as a cautionary tale of how [autocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocratic "Autocratic") tendencies can be kept in check by the values of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic"). Alexander was used by these writers as an example of ruler values such as *amicitia* (friendship) and *clementia* (clemency), but also *iracundia* (anger) and *cupiditas gloriae* (over-desire for glory).

[Emperor Julian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Julian "Emperor Julian") in his satire called "The Caesars", describes a contest between the previous Roman emperors, with Alexander the Great called in as an extra contestant, in the presence of the assembled gods.

The [Itinerarium Alexandri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itinerarium_Alexandri "Itinerarium Alexandri") is a 4th-century Latin description of Alexander the Great's campaigns.

[Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") went to serve his quaestorship in Hispania after his wife's funeral, in the spring or early summer of 69 BC. While there, he encountered a statue of Alexander the Great, and realised with dissatisfaction that he was now at an age when Alexander had the world at his feet, while he had achieved comparatively little.

[Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey "Pompey") posed as the "new Alexander" since he was his boyhood hero.

After Caracalla concluded his campaign against the Alamanni, it became evident that he was inordinately preoccupied with Alexander the Great. He began openly mimicking Alexander in his personal style. In planning his invasion of the Parthian Empire, Caracalla decided to arrange 16,000 of his men in Macedonian-style [phalanxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phalanx "Phalanx"), despite the Roman army having made the phalanx an obsolete tactical formation. The historian Christopher Matthew mentions that the term *Phalangarii* has two possible meanings, both with military connotations. The first refers merely to the Roman battle line and does not specifically mean that the men were armed with [pikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pike_(weapon) "Pike (weapon)"), and the second bears similarity to the 'Marian Mules' of the late Roman Republic who carried their equipment suspended from a long pole, which were in use until at least the 2nd century AD. As a consequence, the *Phalangarii* of [Legio II Parthica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legio_II_Parthica "Legio II Parthica") may not have been pikemen, but rather standard battle line troops or possibly *[Triarii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triarii "Triarii")*.

Caracalla's mania for Alexander went so far that Caracalla visited Alexandria while preparing for his Persian invasion and persecuted philosophers of the [Aristotelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism "Aristotelianism") school based on a legend that Aristotle had poisoned Alexander. This was a sign of Caracalla's increasingly erratic behaviour. But this mania for Alexander, strange as it was, was overshadowed by subsequent events in Alexandria.

In AD 39, [Caligula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caligula "Caligula") performed a spectacular stunt by ordering a temporary [floating bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontoon_bridge "Pontoon bridge") to be built using ships as [pontoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontoon_(boat) "Pontoon (boat)"), stretching for over two miles from the resort of [Baiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baiae "Baiae") to the neighbouring port of [Puteoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puteoli "Puteoli"). It was said that the bridge was to rival the Persian king [Xerxes's pontoon bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes%27s_pontoon_bridge "Xerxes's pontoon bridge") crossing of the Hellespont. Caligula, who could not swim, then proceeded to ride his favourite horse [Incitatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incitatus "Incitatus") across, wearing the [breastplate of Alexander the Great](#Post-death_events). This act was in defiance of a prediction by Tiberius's soothsayer [Thrasyllus of Mendes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrasyllus_of_Mendes "Thrasyllus of Mendes") that Caligula had "no more chance of becoming emperor than of riding a horse across the Bay of Baiae".

The diffusion of Greek culture and language cemented by Alexander's conquests in West Asia and North Africa served as a "precondition" for the [later Roman expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithridatic_Wars "Mithridatic Wars") into these territories and [entire basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Greeks "Byzantine Greeks") for the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"), according to [Errington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Malcolm_Errington "Robert Malcolm Errington").

### Letters

Alexander wrote and received numerous letters, but no [originals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autograph_manuscript "Autograph manuscript") survive. A few official letters addressed to the Greek cities survive in copies inscribed in stone and the content of others is sometimes reported in historical sources. These only occasionally quote the letters and it is an open question how reliable such quotations are. Several fictitious letters, some perhaps based on actual letters, made their way into the *Romance* tradition.

### In legend

Many of the legends about Alexander derive from his own lifetime, probably encouraged by Alexander himself. His court historian Callisthenes portrayed the sea in [Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia "Cilicia") as drawing back from him in proskynesis. Writing shortly after Alexander's death, [Onesicritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onesicritus "Onesicritus") invented a tryst between Alexander and [Thalestris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalestris "Thalestris"), queen of the mythical [Amazons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazons "Amazons"). He reportedly read this passage to his patron King [Lysimachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysimachus "Lysimachus"), who had been one of Alexander's generals and who quipped, "I wonder where I was at the time."

In the first centuries after Alexander's death, probably in Alexandria, a quantity of the legendary material coalesced into a text known as the *[Alexander Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Romance "Alexander Romance")*, later falsely ascribed to Callisthenes and therefore known as *Pseudo-Callisthenes*. This text underwent over one hundred recensions, translations, and derivations throughout the Islamic and European worlds in premodern times, containing many dubious stories, and was translated into twenty-five languages, for example [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian "Middle Persian"), [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language") and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic").

### In ancient and modern culture

Alexander the Great's accomplishments and legacy have been depicted in many cultures. Alexander has featured in both high and popular culture, beginning from his own era to the present day. The *Alexander Romance*, in particular, has had a significant impact on portrayals of Alexander in later cultures, from Persian to medieval European, to modern Greek.

Alexander features prominently in modern Greek folklore, more than any other ancient figure. The colloquial form of his name in modern Greek ("O Megalexandros") is a household name, and he is the only ancient hero to appear in the [Karagiozis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karagiozis "Karagiozis") shadow play. One well-known fable among Greek seamen involves a solitary [mermaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mermaid "Mermaid") who would grasp a ship's prow during a storm and ask the captain, "Is King Alexander alive?" The answer should be "He is alive and well and rules the world!" causing the mermaid to vanish and the sea to calm. Any other answer would cause the mermaid to turn into a raging [Gorgon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorgon "Gorgon") who would drag the ship to the bottom of the sea, all hands aboard.

In pre-Islamic [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian "Middle Persian") ([Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrian "Zoroastrian")) literature, Alexander is referred to by the epithet *gujastak*, meaning "accursed", and is accused of destroying temples and burning the sacred texts of Zoroastrianism. In [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") Persia, under the influence of the *Alexander Romance* (in [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"): اسکندرنامه *[Iskandarnameh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iskandarnameh "Iskandarnameh")*), a more positive portrayal of Alexander emerges. [Firdausi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firdausi "Firdausi")'s *[Shahnameh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahnameh "Shahnameh")* ("The Book of Kings") [includes Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great_in_the_Shahnameh "Alexander the Great in the Shahnameh") in a line of legitimate Persian [shahs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah "Shah"), a mythical figure who explored the far reaches of the world in search of the [Fountain of Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountain_of_Youth "Fountain of Youth"). In the *Shahnameh*, Alexander's first journey is to [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca "Mecca") to pray at the [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba "Kaaba"). Alexander was depicted as performing a [Hajj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajj "Hajj") (pilgrimage to Mecca) many times in subsequent Islamic art and literature. Later Persian writers associate him with philosophy, portraying him at a symposium with figures such as [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates"), [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") and Aristotle, in search of immortality.

The figure of [Dhu al-Qarnayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhu_al-Qarnayn "Dhu al-Qarnayn") ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"): ذو القرنين; lit. 'The Two-Horned One') is believed by the majority of modern researchers of the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur%27an "Qur'an") as well as Islamic commentators to be a reference to Alexander. The figure is also believed by scholars to be based on later legends of Alexander. In this tradition, he was a heroic figure who [built a wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_Alexander "Gates of Alexander") to defend against the nations of [Gog and Magog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gog_and_Magog "Gog and Magog"). He also travelled the known world in search of the Water of Life and Immortality, eventually becoming a prophet.

The Syriac version of the *Alexander Romance* portrays him as an ideal [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian "Christian") world conqueror who prayed to "the [one true God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity "God in Christianity")". In Egypt, Alexander was portrayed as the son of [Nectanebo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectanebo_II "Nectanebo II"), the last pharaoh before the Persian conquest. His defeat of Darius was depicted as Egypt's salvation, "proving" Egypt was still ruled by an Egyptian.

According to [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus "Josephus"), Alexander was shown the [Book of Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Daniel "Book of Daniel") when he entered Jerusalem, which described a mighty Greek king who would conquer the Persian Empire. This is cited as a reason for sparing Jerusalem.

In [Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi "Hindi") and [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu "Urdu"), the name "[Sikandar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iskandar_(name) "Iskandar (name)")", derived from the Persian name for Alexander, denotes a rising young talent, and the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate") ruler [Alauddin Khalji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alauddin_Khalji "Alauddin Khalji") stylized himself as "Sikandar-i-Sani" (the Second Alexander the Great). In [medieval India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_India "Medieval India"), Turkic and Afghan sovereigns from the Iranian-cultured region of Central Asia brought positive cultural connotations of Alexander to the Indian subcontinent, resulting in the efflorescence of *Sikandernameh* ([Alexander Romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Romances "Alexander Romances")) written by Indo-Persian poets such as [Amir Khusrau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amir_Khusrau "Amir Khusrau") and the prominence of Alexander the Great as a popular subject in Mughal-era Persian miniatures. Alexander's legacy would also be evoked in [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), with the rulers of the [Malacca Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca_Sultanate "Malacca Sultanate") claiming their descent all the way back to him as "Raja Iskander Zul-karnain" ([Alexander of the Two Horns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horns_of_Alexander "Horns of Alexander")). The name "[Iskander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_Iskandar_(disambiguation) "Sultan Iskandar (disambiguation)")" has been used by multiple Southeast Asian sultans. In [medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Europe "Medieval Europe"), Alexander the Great was revered as a member of the [Nine Worthies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Worthies "Nine Worthies"); a group of heroes whose lives were believed to encapsulate all the ideal qualities of [chivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chivalry "Chivalry"). During the first [Italian campaign of the French Revolutionary Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_campaigns_of_the_French_Revolutionary_Wars "Italian campaigns of the French Revolutionary Wars"), in a question from [Bourrienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourrienne "Bourrienne"), asking whether he gave his preference to Alexander or Caesar, [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon") said that he places Alexander The Great in the first rank, the main reason being his campaign on Asia.

## Historiography

Apart from a few inscriptions and fragments, texts written by people who actually knew Alexander or who gathered information from men who served with Alexander were all lost. Contemporaries who wrote accounts of his life included Alexander's campaign historian Callisthenes, Alexander's generals; Ptolemy and [Nearchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearchus "Nearchus"), [Aristobulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristobulus_of_Cassandreia "Aristobulus of Cassandreia"), a junior officer on the campaigns, and Onesicritus, Alexander's chief helmsman. Their works are lost, but later works based on these [original sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source "Primary source") have survived. The earliest of these is [Diodorus Siculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus "Diodorus Siculus") (1st century BC), followed by Quintus Curtius Rufus (mid-to-late 1st century AD), [Arrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrian "Arrian") (1st to 2nd century AD), the biographer Plutarch (1st to 2nd century AD), and finally [Justin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_(historian) "Justin (historian)"), whose work dated as late as the 4th century. Of these, Arrian is generally considered the most reliable, given that he used Ptolemy and Aristobulus as his sources, closely followed by Diodorus.
