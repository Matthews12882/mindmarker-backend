# Moon

The **Moon** is the only [natural satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite "Natural satellite") of [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth"). It [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon "Orbit of the Moon") Earth in an [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve "Elliptic curve") path, at a [distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance "Lunar distance")  that varies over the year, averaging around 378,000 kilometers (235,000 mi). It [completes an orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period "Orbital period") ([lunar month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_month "Lunar month")) in relation to Earth and the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun") ([synodically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunation "Lunation")) every 29.5 days. The Moon and Earth are bound by [gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational "Gravitational") attraction, which is stronger on the sides facing each other. The resulting [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force "Tidal force") are the main driver of Earth's [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide "Tide"), and have pulled the Moon to always face Earth with the same [near side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_side_of_the_Moon "Near side of the Moon"). This [tidal locking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_locking "Tidal locking") effectively synchronizes the Moon's [rotation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_period_(astronomy) "Rotation period (astronomy)") ([lunar day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_day "Lunar day")) to its orbital period (lunar month).

In [geophysical terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_definition_of_planet "Geophysical definition of planet"), the Moon is a [planetary-mass object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary-mass_object "Planetary-mass object") or [satellite planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_planet "Satellite planet"). Its mass is 1.2% that of the Earth, and its diameter is 3,474 km (2,159 mi), roughly one-quarter of Earth's (about as wide as the [contiguous United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contiguous_United_States "Contiguous United States")). Within the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System"), it is [larger and more massive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size "List of Solar System objects by size") than any known [dwarf planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_planet "Dwarf planet"), and the fifth-largest and fifth–most massive moon, as well as the largest and most massive in relation to its [parent planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_body "Primary body"). Its [surface gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_gravity "Surface gravity") is about one-sixth of Earth's, about half that of [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars "Mars"), and the second-highest among all moons in the Solar System after [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter")'s moon [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_(moon) "Io (moon)"). The body of the Moon is [differentiated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_differentiation "Planetary differentiation") and [terrestrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_planet "Terrestrial planet"), with only a minuscule [hydrosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosphere "Hydrosphere"), [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon "Atmosphere of the Moon"), and [magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_of_the_Moon "Magnetic field of the Moon"). The [lunar surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_surface "Lunar surface") is covered in [regolith dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_regolith "Lunar regolith"), which mainly consists of the fine [material ejected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejecta_blanket "Ejecta blanket") from the [lunar crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_crust "Lunar crust") by [impact events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event "Impact event"). The lunar crust is marked by [impact craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater "Impact crater"); some younger ones feature bright [ray-like streaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_system "Ray system"). The Moon was volcanically active until 1.2 billion years ago, surfacing lava mostly on the thinner near side of the Moon, filling ancient craters, which through cooling formed the today prominently visible dark plains of [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt "Basalt") called [maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_mare "Lunar mare") ('seas'). The Moon is thought to have been formed out of material from Earth that was ejected by [a giant impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant-impact_hypothesis "Giant-impact hypothesis") of a [Mars-sized body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theia_(planet) "Theia (planet)"). Studies based on isotopic dating crystallization of the [lunar magma ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_magma_ocean "Lunar magma ocean") suggested that the Moon formed 4.35 billion years ago, late in the [history of the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System "Formation and evolution of the Solar System").

From a distance, the day and night phases of the lunar day are visible as the [lunar phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phases "Lunar phases"), and when the Moon passes through [Earth's shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_shadow "Earth's shadow") a [lunar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse "Lunar eclipse") is observable. The Moon's [apparent size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_size "Apparent size") in Earth's sky is about the same as that of the Sun, which causes it to cover the Sun completely during a total [solar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse "Solar eclipse"). The Moon is the [brightest celestial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_brightest_natural_objects_in_the_sky "List of brightest natural objects in the sky") in Earth's [night sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_sky "Night sky") because of its large apparent size, while the [reflectance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflectance "Reflectance") (albedo) of its surface is comparable to that of [asphalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asphalt_concrete "Asphalt concrete"). About 59% of the surface of the Moon is visible from Earth owing to the different angles at which the Moon can appear in Earth's sky ([libration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration "Libration")), making parts of the [far side of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon "Far side of the Moon") visible.

The Moon has been an important source of inspiration and knowledge in human history, having been crucial to [cosmography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmography "Cosmography"), [mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythology "Mythology"), [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_deity "Lunar deity"), art, [time keeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_calendar "Lunar calendar"), [natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science") and [spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceflight "Spaceflight"). The [first spaceflights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Solar_System_exploration "Timeline of Solar System exploration") to an extraterrestrial body were to the Moon, starting in 1959 with the [flyby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flyby_(spaceflight) "Flyby (spaceflight)") of *[Luna 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_1 "Luna 1")* (sent by the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union")), and the [intentional impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impactor_(spacecraft) "Impactor (spacecraft)") of *[Luna 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_2 "Luna 2")*, followed in 1966 by the first [soft landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_landing "Soft landing") (by *[Luna 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_9 "Luna 9")*) and [orbital insertion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_insertion "Orbital insertion") (by *[Luna 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_10 "Luna 10")*). Humans first arrived in orbit with [Apollo 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_8 "Apollo 8") (sent by the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States")) on December 24, 1968, and then on the surface with [Apollo 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11 "Apollo 11") on July 20, 1969, making the Moon the only celestial body beyond Earth that humans have visited. By 1972, six Apollo missions had landed [twelve people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_who_have_flown_to_the_Moon#People_who_walked_on_the_Moon "List of people who have flown to the Moon") on the Moon and stayed up to three days. Renewed robotic [exploration of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon "Exploration of the Moon"), in particular to confirm the presence of [water on the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water "Lunar water"), has fueled plans to return humans to the Moon. These plans include the [Artemis program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_program "Artemis program"), which included the [Artemis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_II "Artemis II") crewed lunar flyby in 2026.

## Names and etymology

The English proper name for Earth's natural satellite is typically written as *Moon*, with a capital *M*. The noun *moon* is derived from [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") *mōna*, which stems from \**mēnōn*, which in turn comes from [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European "Proto-Indo-European") \**mēnsis* ('month') – from earlier \**mēnōt* (genitive \**mēneses*), which may be related to a verb meaning 'to measure [time]'.

The Latin name for the Moon is *lūna*. The English adjective *lunar* was ultimately borrowed from Latin, likely through French. In scientific writing and science fiction, the Moon is sometimes referred to as *Luna* [/ˈluːnə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") to distinguish it from other moons. In poetry, *Luna* may also refer to the [personification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personification "Personification") of the Moon as a woman.

The Ancient Greek word *selḗnē* referred to the Moon as a celestial body, and also to the moon goddess [Selene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selene "Selene") [/səˈliːniː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"). The rare English adjective *selenian* [/səliːniən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") is used to describe the Moon as a world, as opposed to a celestial object. Its cognate *selenic*, originally a rare synonym, now almost always refers to the chemical element [selenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selenium "Selenium"). The corresponding prefix *seleno-* appears in terms including *[selenography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selenography "Selenography")* (the study of the lunar surface).

The [astronomical symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_symbols "Astronomical symbols") for the Moon are the crescent  and decrescent , for example in *M*☾ 'lunar mass'.

### Classification

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) calls Earth's satellite "the Moon", with a capital "M". Other natural satellites of planets are called 'moons', with a lower-case "m".
The Moon has historically been identified as a [classical planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_planet "Classical planet"), in the original sense of a wandering object in the sky. When Galileo discovered satellites orbiting Jupiter, he named them 'moons' because, like the Moon, they can be considered planets orbiting another planet. Some experts hold to a [geophysical definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_definition_of_planet "Geophysical definition of planet") that classifies moons as planets that orbit the Sun while incidentally orbiting another planet.

In a [heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentrism#Modern_use_of_geocentric_and_heliocentric "Heliocentrism") [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference "Frame of reference") the Moon orbits the Sun, [perturbed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbation_(astronomy) "Perturbation (astronomy)") by Earth. This has led some scientists to argue that the Moon could be identified as a planet, both historically and qualitatively, adding that its mass would be enough to clear its orbit around the Sun if it were on its own. Some have suggested that this implies that the Earth–Moon system is a [double planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_planet "Double planet"). The [International Astronomical Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union "International Astronomical Union") standards organization has no definition for planets in a [binary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_system "Binary system"), or for what constitutes a double planet system, but most scientists agree that this would require the Moon-Earth [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter_(astronomy) "Barycenter (astronomy)") to be outside of Earth, which it is not.

## Natural history

### Formation

[Isotope dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_dating "Isotope dating") of lunar samples suggests the Moon formed sometime in the first 200 million years after the [origin of the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_the_Solar_System "Origin of the Solar System"). The most reliable measurements across the major rock types report 4.35 Gyr for the age.

The prevailing theory is that the Earth–Moon system formed after a [giant impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_impact "Giant impact") of a [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars "Mars")-sized body (named *[Theia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theia_(planet) "Theia (planet)")*) with the [proto-Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Earth "Proto-Earth"). The oblique impact blasted material into orbit about the Earth and the material accreted and formed the Moon just beyond the Earth's [Roche limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roche_limit "Roche limit") of ~2.56 [*R*🜨](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius "Earth radius").

Giant impacts are thought to have been common in the early Solar System. Computer simulations of giant impacts have produced results that are consistent with the mass of the lunar core and the angular momentum of the Earth–Moon system. These simulations show that most of the Moon derived from the impactor, rather than the proto-Earth. However, models from 2007 and later suggest a larger fraction of the Moon derived from the proto-Earth. Other bodies of the inner Solar System such as Mars and [Vesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4_Vesta "4 Vesta") have, according to meteorites from them, very different oxygen and tungsten [isotopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope "Isotope") compositions compared to Earth. However, Earth and the Moon have nearly identical isotopic compositions. The isotopic equalization of the Earth–Moon system might be explained by the post-impact mixing of the vaporized material that formed the two, although this is debated.

The impact would have released enough energy to liquefy both the ejecta and the Earth's crust, forming a magma ocean. The liquefied ejecta could have then re-accreted into the Earth–Moon system. The newly formed Moon would have had [its own magma ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_magma_ocean "Lunar magma ocean"); its depth is estimated from about 500 km (300 miles) to 1,737 km (1,079 miles).
Two [continent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent") sized chunks of Earth's lower mantle have been proposed as remnants of Theia still present inside the Earth.

High-resolution simulations (up to 108 particles) find that giant impacts can immediately place a satellite with similar mass and iron content to the Moon into orbit sufficiently far from Earth to avoid tidal destruction (outside the [Roche limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roche_limit "Roche limit")). Even simulated satellites that initially pass within the Roche limit can reliably and predictably survive, by being partially stripped and then torqued onto wider, stable orbits.

While the giant-impact theory explains many lines of evidence, some questions are still unresolved, most of which involve the Moon's composition. Models that have the Moon acquiring a significant amount of the proto-Earth are more difficult to reconcile with geochemical data for the isotopes of zirconium, oxygen, silicon, and other elements.

### Natural development

The newly formed Moon settled into a much closer Earth orbit than it has today. Each body therefore appeared much larger in the sky of the other, [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse "Eclipse") were more frequent, and [tidal effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_effect "Tidal effect") were stronger. Due to [tidal acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_acceleration "Tidal acceleration"), the Moon's orbit around Earth has become significantly larger, with a longer period.

Following formation, the Moon has cooled and most of [its atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon "Atmosphere of the Moon") has been stripped. The [lunar surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_of_the_Moon "Surface of the Moon") has since been shaped by large [impact events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event "Impact event") and many small ones, forming a landscape featuring [craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_crater "Lunar crater") of all ages.

The Moon was [volcanically active](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism "Volcanism") until 1.2 billion years ago, which laid down the prominent [lunar maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_maria "Lunar maria"). Most of the [mare basalts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_mare "Lunar mare") erupted during the [Imbrian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imbrian "Imbrian"), 3.3–3.7 billion years ago, though some are as young as 1.2 billion years and some as old as 4.2 billion years. The distribution of the mare basalts is uneven, with the basalts predominantly appearing on the Moon's near-side hemisphere. The reasons for this are not yet known, although the relative thinness of the crust on the near side of the Moon is hypothesized to be a factor. Causes of the distribution of the [lunar highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_highlands "Lunar highlands") on the [far side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon "Far side of the Moon") are also not well understood. Topological measurements show the crust on the near side to be thinner than on the far side. One possible explanation for this is that large impacts on the near side may have made it easier for lava to flow onto the surface.

### Lunar geologic timescale

Millions of years before present

The [lunar geologic timescale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_geologic_timescale "Lunar geologic timescale") is based on the most prominent impact events, such as multi-ring formations like [Nectaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectarian "Nectarian"), [Imbrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Imbrian "Lower Imbrian"), and [Orientale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Orientale "Mare Orientale") that are between hundreds and thousands of kilometers in diameter and associated with a broad apron of ejecta deposits that form a regional [stratigraphic horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratigraphy "Stratigraphy"). The lunar geological periods are named after their characteristic features, from most [impact craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater "Impact crater") outside the dark [mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_mare "Lunar mare"), to the mare and later craters, and finally the young, still bright and therefore readily visible craters with [ray systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_system "Ray system") like [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus_(lunar_crater) "Copernicus (lunar crater)") or [Tycho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_(lunar_crater) "Tycho (lunar crater)"). The lack of an atmosphere, weather, and recent geological processes mean that many of these craters are well-preserved.
Although only a few [multi-ring basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-ring_basins "Multi-ring basins") have been definitively dated, they are useful for assigning relative ages. Because impact craters accumulate at a nearly constant rate, counting the number of craters per unit area can be used to estimate the age of the surface.
However care needs to be exercised with the [crater counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater_counting "Crater counting") technique due to the potential presence of [secondary craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_crater "Secondary crater"). Ejecta from impacts can create secondary craters that often appear in clusters or chains but can also occur as isolated formations at a considerable distance from the impact. These can resemble primary craters, and may even dominate small crater populations, so their unidentified presence can distort age estimates.

The radiometric ages of impact-melted rocks collected during the [Apollo missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_missions "Apollo missions") cluster between 3.8 and 4.1 billion years old: this has been used to propose a [Late Heavy Bombardment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment "Late Heavy Bombardment") period of increased impacts.

High-resolution images from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter in the 2010s show a contemporary crater-production rate significantly higher than was previously estimated. A secondary cratering process caused by [distal ejecta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distal_ejecta "Distal ejecta") is thought to churn the top two centimeters of regolith on a timescale of 81,000 years. This rate is 100 times faster than the rate computed from models based solely on direct micrometeorite impacts.

### Long term predictions

In five billion years the Moon will orbit 40% further away from Earth than it is now. However, about two to three billion years after that, the Sun will have become a [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant "Red giant"). Assuming the Sun envelops the Earth–Moon system, the consequent drag from the Sun's atmosphere may cause the orbital distance between the Earth and the Moon to decay to the point where the Moon comes within the Earth's [Roche limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roche_limit "Roche limit"), leading it to disintegrate.

## Physical characteristics

### Size and mass

The Moon is the fifth largest (by size and mass) natural satellite of the Solar System. It is categorizable as a [planetary-mass moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary-mass_moon "Planetary-mass moon"), making it a satellite planet under the [geophysical definitions of the term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_definition_of_planet "Geophysical definition of planet"). It is smaller than [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_(planet) "Mercury (planet)") but considerably larger than the largest [dwarf planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_planet "Dwarf planet") of the Solar System, [Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto "Pluto"). The Moon is the second-densest satellite in the Solar System, after [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_(moon) "Io (moon)").

With a mass of 1⁄81 of Earth's, and a diameter about 3,500 km (more than one-quarter of Earth's), the Moon is the largest natural satellite in the Solar System relative to its [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_(astronomy) "Primary (astronomy)") planet. The face of the Moon is comparable to the width of [mainland Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Australia "Mainland Australia"), Europe or the [contiguous United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contiguous_United_States "Contiguous United States"). The whole surface area of the Moon is about 38 million square kilometers, comparable to that of the whole [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"), the areas of the lunar hemispheres being comparable to the areas of [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America") and [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America "South America").

### Shape

The Moon was initially in [hydrostatic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium "Hydrostatic equilibrium") but has since departed from this condition. It is a very slightly scalene [ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoid "Ellipsoid") due to tidal stretching, with its long axis displaced 30° from facing the Earth, due to gravitational anomalies from impact basins. Its shape is more elongated than current tidal forces can account for. This 'fossil bulge' indicates that the Moon solidified when it orbited at half its current distance to the Earth, and that it is now too cold for its shape to restore hydrostatic equilibrium at its current orbital distance. Today tidal crust deformation is limited to lobate thrust fault scarps formation.

### Structure

The Moon is a [differentiated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_differentiation "Planetary differentiation") body with a [geochemically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geochemically "Geochemically") distinct [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crust_(geology) "Crust (geology)"), [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_(geology) "Mantle (geology)"), and [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_core "Planetary core"). It a solid iron-rich inner core with a radius possibly as small as 240 kilometers (150 mi) and a fluid outer core primarily made of liquid iron with a radius of roughly 300 kilometers (190 mi). Around the core is a partially molten boundary layer with a radius of about 500 kilometers (310 mi). This structure is thought to have developed through the [fractional crystallization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_crystallization_(geology) "Fractional crystallization (geology)") of a global magma ocean shortly after the Moon's formation 4.5 billion years ago.

Crystallization of this magma ocean would have created a [mafic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafic "Mafic") mantle from the [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_(chemistry) "Precipitation (chemistry)") and sinking of the minerals [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine "Olivine"), [clinopyroxene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinopyroxene "Clinopyroxene"), and [orthopyroxene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthopyroxene "Orthopyroxene"); after about three-quarters of the magma ocean had crystallized, lower-density [plagioclase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagioclase "Plagioclase") minerals could form and float into a crust atop. The final liquids to crystallize would have been initially sandwiched between the crust and mantle, with a high abundance of [incompatible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatibility_(geochemistry) "Compatibility (geochemistry)") and heat-producing elements. Consistent with this perspective, geochemical mapping made from orbit suggests a crust of mostly [anorthosite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anorthosite "Anorthosite"). The [Moon rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_rock "Moon rock") samples of the flood lavas that erupted onto the surface from partial melting in the mantle confirm the mafic mantle composition, which is more iron-rich than that of Earth. The crust is on average about 50 kilometers (31 mi) thick.

However, the inner core of the Moon is small, with a radius of about 350 kilometers (220 mi) or less, around 20% of the radius of the Moon. Its composition is not well understood but is probably metallic iron alloyed with a small amount of sulfur and nickel. Analyses of the Moon's time-variable rotation suggest that it is at least partly molten. The pressure at the lunar core is estimated to be 5 GPa (49,000 atm).

### Gravitational field

On average the Moon's [surface gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_gravity "Surface gravity") is 1.62 [m/s2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_per_second_squared "Metre per second squared") (0.1654 [g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force "G-force"); 5.318 [ft/s2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_per_second_squared "Foot per second squared")), about half of the [surface gravity of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_of_Mars "Gravity of Mars") and about a sixth of [Earth's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_of_Earth "Gravity of Earth"). Among the planetary moons, it has the second highest surface gravity, after [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_(moon) "Io (moon)"). It has an escape velocity of 2.38 km/s (8600 km/h; 5300 mph).

The [Moon's gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation_of_the_Moon "Gravitation of the Moon") is not uniform. The details of the gravitational field have been measured through tracking the [Doppler shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_shift "Doppler shift") of radio signals emitted by [orbiting spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_orbiter "Lunar orbiter"). The main lunar gravity features are [mascons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_concentration_(astronomy) "Mass concentration (astronomy)"), large positive gravitational anomalies associated with some of the giant impact basins, partly caused by the dense mare basaltic lava flows that fill those basins. The anomalies greatly influence the [orbit of spacecraft about the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_orbit "Lunar orbit"). There are some puzzles: lava flows by themselves cannot explain all of the gravitational signature, and some mascons exist that are not linked to mare volcanism.

The [sphere of influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_of_influence_(astrodynamics) "Sphere of influence (astrodynamics)") of the Moon's gravity field, in which it dominates over Earth's, has a [Hill radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_sphere "Hill sphere") of 60,000 km (i.e., extending less than one-sixth the distance of the 378,000 km between the Moon and the Earth), extending to the Earth–Moon [lagrange points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_points "Lagrange points"). This space is called [cislunar space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cislunar_space "Cislunar space").

### Magnetic field

The Moon has [an external magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_of_the_Moon "Magnetic field of the Moon") of less than 0.2 [nanoteslas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_(unit) "Tesla (unit)"), or less than one hundred thousandth [that of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_magnetic_field "Earth's magnetic field"). The Moon does not have a global [dipolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipolar "Dipolar") magnetic field; it only has crustal magnetization. The magnetic field history of the Moon is controversial, and there is still no consensus on the past intensity and timing of the Moons paleomagnetic field. Many paleomagnetic measurements have been taken from lunar samples collected during the Apollo missions. These measurements indicate that the Moon's paleomagnetic field experienced two epochs with distinct characteristics. In the first epoch (4.5 billion to 3.56 billion years ago), magnetic field intensities reached around 100 microteslas (1 Gauss), close to that of Earth's today. The second epoch was much longer (3.58 billion to 1 billion years ago), yet much weaker, with strengths only reaching 5-7 microteslas. It is unlikely that a single convection mechanism, thermal or compositional, generated this specific dynamo history without external influence. Theoretically, some of the remnant magnetization may originate from transient magnetic fields generated during large impacts through the expansion of plasma clouds. These clouds are generated during large impacts in an ambient magnetic field. This is supported by the location of the largest crustal magnetizations situated near the [antipodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodes_on_Earth "Antipodes on Earth") of the giant impact basins.

### Atmosphere

The Moon has an [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere "Atmosphere") consisting of only an [exosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exosphere "Exosphere"), which is so tenuous as to be nearly [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum "Vacuum"), with a total mass of less than 10 tonnes (9.8 long tons; 11 short tons). The surface pressure of this small mass is around 3 × 10−15 [atm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_(unit) "Atmosphere (unit)") (0.3 [nPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanopascal "Nanopascal")); it varies with the lunar day. Its sources include [outgassing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgassing "Outgassing") and [sputtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputtering "Sputtering"), a product of the bombardment of lunar soil by solar wind ions. Elements that have been detected include [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium "Sodium") and [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium "Potassium"), produced by sputtering (also found in the atmospheres of Mercury and [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_(moon) "Io (moon)")); [helium-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium-4 "Helium-4") and [neon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neon "Neon") from the solar wind; and [argon-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon "Argon"), [radon-222](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon "Radon"), and [polonium-210](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium-210 "Polonium-210"), outgassed after their creation by [radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay "Radioactive decay") within the crust and mantle. The absence of such neutral species (atoms or molecules) as [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen"), [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen"), [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon"), [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") and [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium "Magnesium"), which are present in the [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith"), is not understood. Water vapor has been detected by *[Chandrayaan-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrayaan-1 "Chandrayaan-1")* and found to vary with latitude, with a maximum at ~60–70 degrees; it is possibly generated from the [sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_(chemistry) "Sublimation (chemistry)") of water ice in the regolith. These gases either return into the regolith because of the Moon's gravity or are lost to space, either through solar radiation pressure or, if they are ionized, by being swept away by the solar wind's magnetic field.

A permanent [Moon dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_dust "Moon dust") cloud exists around the Moon, generated by small particles from comets. 5 tons of comet particles are estimated to strike the Moon's surface every 24 hours, resulting in the ejection of dust particles. The dust stays above the Moon for approximately 10 minutes, taking 5 minutes to rise, and 5 minutes to fall. On average, 120 kilograms of dust are present above the Moon, rising up to 100 kilometers above the surface. Dust counts made by [LADEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LADEE "LADEE")'s Lunar Dust EXperiment (LDEX) found particle counts peaked during the [Geminid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geminid "Geminid"), [Quadrantid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrantid "Quadrantid"), [Northern Taurid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taurids "Taurids"), and [Omicron Centaurid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omicron_Centaurids "Omicron Centaurids") [meteor showers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_shower "Meteor shower"), when the Earth, and Moon pass through comet debris. The lunar dust cloud is asymmetric, being denser near the boundary between the Moon's dayside and nightside.

Studies of Moon magma samples retrieved by the [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program "Apollo program") missions demonstrate that the Moon had once possessed a relatively thick atmosphere for a period of 70 million years between 3 and 4 billion years ago. This atmosphere, sourced from gases ejected from lunar volcanic eruptions, was twice the thickness of that of present-day [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars "Mars"). The ancient lunar atmosphere was eventually stripped away by solar winds and dissipated into space.

### Surface conditions

[Ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation "Ionizing radiation") from [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray"), their resulting [neutron radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_radiation "Neutron radiation"), and the Sun results in an average radiation level of 1.369 [millisieverts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisievert "Millisievert") per day during lunar [daytime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytime "Daytime"), which is about 2.6 times more than the level on the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station"), 5–10 times more than the level during a trans-Atlantic flight, and 200 times more than the level on Earth's surface. For further comparison, radiation levels average about 1.84 millisieverts per day on a [flight to Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mission_to_Mars "Human mission to Mars") and about 0.64 millisieverts per day on Mars itself, with some locations on Mars possibly having levels as low as 0.342 millisieverts per day.
Solar radiation also [electrically charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_electricity "Static electricity") the highly abrasive [lunar dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_dust "Lunar dust") and makes it levitate. This effect contributes to the easy spread of the sticky, lung- and gear-damaging lunar dust.

The lunar surface is exposed to temperature differences ranging from 120 °C to −171 °C depending on the [solar irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance "Solar irradiance").
Because of the lack of atmosphere, temperatures of different areas vary particularly upon whether they are in sunlight or shadow, making topographical details play a decisive role on local [surface temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surface_temperature "Global surface temperature").
Parts of many craters, particularly the bottoms of many polar craters, are permanently shadowed. These [craters of eternal darkness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater_of_eternal_darkness "Crater of eternal darkness") have extremely low temperatures. The *Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter* measured the lowest summer temperatures in craters at the southern pole at 35 K (−238 °C; −397 °F) and just 26 K (−247 °C; −413 °F) close to the lunar winter solstice in the north polar crater [Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_(crater) "Hermite (crater)"). This is the coldest temperature in the Solar System ever measured by a spacecraft, colder even than the surface of [Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto "Pluto").

Blanketed on top of the Moon's crust is a highly [comminuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comminution "Comminution") (broken into ever smaller particles) and [impact gardened](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_gardening "Impact gardening") mostly gray surface layer called [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith"), formed by impact processes. The finer regolith, the [lunar soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_soil "Lunar soil") of [silicon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide "Silicon dioxide") glass, has a texture resembling snow and a scent resembling spent [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder "Gunpowder"). The regolith of older surfaces is generally thicker than for younger surfaces: it varies in thickness from 10–15 m (33–49 ft) in the highlands and 4–5 m (13–16 ft) in the maria. Beneath the finely comminuted regolith layer is the megaregolith, a layer of highly fractured bedrock many kilometers thick.

These extreme conditions are considered to make it unlikely for spacecraft to harbor bacterial spores at the Moon for longer than just one lunar orbit.

### Surface features

The [topography of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography_of_the_Moon "Topography of the Moon") has been measured with [laser altimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_altimetry "Laser altimetry") and [stereo image analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereoscopy "Stereoscopy"). Its most extensive [topographic feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_features_on_the_Moon "List of features on the Moon") is the giant far-side [South Pole–Aitken basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole%E2%80%93Aitken_basin "South Pole–Aitken basin"), some 2,240 km (1,390 mi) in diameter, the largest crater on the Moon and the second-largest confirmed impact [crater in the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_craters_in_the_Solar_System "List of largest craters in the Solar System"). At 13 km (8.1 mi) deep, its floor is the lowest point on the surface of the Moon, reaching −9.178 kilometers (−5.703 mi) at [70°22′05″S 172°24′47″W﻿ / ﻿70.368°S 172.413°W﻿ / -70.368; -172.413](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Moon&params=70.368_S_172.413_W_globe:moon_type:landmark) in a crater within [Antoniadi crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoniadi_(lunar_crater) "Antoniadi (lunar crater)"). The [highest elevations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_mountains_in_the_Solar_System "List of tallest mountains in the Solar System") of the Moon's surface, with the so-called [Selenean summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selenean_summit "Selenean summit") at 10.629 kilometers (6.605 mi), are located directly to the northeast ([5°26′28″N 158°39′22″W﻿ / ﻿5.441°N 158.656°W﻿ / 5.441; -158.656](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Moon&params=5.441_N_158.656_W_globe:moon_type:landmark)), which might have been thickened by the oblique formation impact of the South Pole–Aitken basin. Other large impact basins such as [Imbrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Imbrium "Mare Imbrium"), [Serenitatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Serenitatis "Mare Serenitatis"), [Crisium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Crisium "Mare Crisium"), [Smythii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Smythii "Mare Smythii"), and [Orientale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Orientale "Mare Orientale") possess regionally low elevations and elevated rims. The far side of the lunar surface is on average about 1.9 km (1.2 mi) higher than that of the near side.

The discovery of [fault scarp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_scarp "Fault scarp") cliffs suggest that the Moon has shrunk by about 90 meters (300 ft) within the past billion years. Similar shrinkage features exist on [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_Mercury "Geology of Mercury"). Mare Frigoris, a basin near the north pole long assumed to be geologically dead, has cracked and shifted. Since the Moon does not have tectonic plates, its tectonic activity is slow, and cracks develop as it loses heat.

Scientists have confirmed the presence of a cave on the Moon near the [Sea of Tranquility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Tranquility "Sea of Tranquility"), not far from the 1969 [Apollo 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11 "Apollo 11") landing site. The cave, identified as an entry point to a collapsed [lava tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_lava_tube "Lunar lava tube"), is roughly 45 meters wide and up to 80 m long. This discovery marks the first confirmed entry point to a lunar cave. The analysis was based on photos taken in 2010 by NASA's [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Reconnaissance_Orbiter "Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter"). The cave's stable temperature of around 17 °C could provide a hospitable environment for future astronauts, protecting them from extreme temperatures, solar radiation, and micrometeorites. However, challenges include accessibility and risks of avalanches and cave-ins. This discovery offers potential for future lunar bases or emergency shelters.

#### Volcanic features

The main features visible from Earth by the naked eye are dark and relatively featureless lunar plains called *[maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_mare "Lunar mare")* (sing. *mare*; from Latin meaning 'seas', as they were once believed to be filled with water) which are vast solidified pools of ancient [basaltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt "Basalt") lava. Although similar to terrestrial basalts, lunar basalts have more iron and no minerals altered by water. The majority of these lava deposits erupted or flowed into the depressions associated with [impact basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_basins "Impact basins"), though the Moon's largest expanse of basalt flooding, [Oceanus Procellarum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanus_Procellarum "Oceanus Procellarum"), does not correspond to an obvious impact basin. Different episodes of lava flow in maria can often be recognized by variations in surface albedo and distinct flow margins.

As the maria formed, cooling and contraction of the basaltic lava created [wrinkle ridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrinkle_ridge "Wrinkle ridge") in some areas. These low, sinuous ridges can extend for hundreds of kilometers and often outline buried structures within the mare. Another result of maria formation is the creation of concentric depressions along the edges, known as [arcuate rilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rille "Rille"). These features occur as the mare basalts sink inward under their own weight, causing the edges to fracture and separate.

In addition to the visible maria, the Moon has mare deposits covered by ejecta from impacts. Called cryptomares, these hidden mares are likely older than the exposed ones. Conversely, mare lava has obscured many [impact melt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_melt "Impact melt") sheets and pools. Impact melts are formed when intense shock pressures from collisions vaporize and melt zones around the impact site. Where still exposed, impact melt can be distinguished from mare lava by its distribution, albedo, and texture.

[Sinuous rilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinuous_rilles "Sinuous rilles"), found in and around maria, are likely extinct [lava channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_channels "Lava channels") or collapsed [lava tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_tubes "Lava tubes"). They typically originate from volcanic [vents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism_on_the_Moon "Volcanism on the Moon"), meandering and sometimes branching as they progress. The largest examples, such as [Schroter's Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schroter%27s_Valley "Schroter's Valley") and [Rima Hadley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley%E2%80%93Apennine "Hadley–Apennine"), are significantly longer, wider, and deeper than terrestrial lava channels, sometimes featuring bends and sharp turns that again, are uncommon on Earth.

Mare volcanism has altered impact craters in various ways, including filling them to varying degrees, and raising and fracturing their floors from uplift of mare material beneath their interiors. Examples of such craters include [Taruntius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taruntius_(crater) "Taruntius (crater)") and [Gassendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gassendi_(crater) "Gassendi (crater)"). Some craters, such as [Hyginus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyginus_(crater) "Hyginus (crater)"), are of wholly volcanic origin, forming as [calderas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caldera "Caldera") or [collapse pits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pit_crater "Pit crater"). Such craters are relatively rare and tend to be smaller (typically a few kilometers wide), shallower, and more irregularly shaped than impact craters. They also lack the upturned rims characteristic of impact craters.

Several [geologic provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_province "Geologic province") containing [shield volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shield_volcano "Shield volcano") and volcanic [domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_dome "Lunar dome") are found within the near side maria. There are also some regions of [pyroclastic deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_rock "Pyroclastic rock"), [scoria cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoria_cones "Scoria cones") and [non-basaltic domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism_on_the_Moon "Volcanism on the Moon") made of particularly high-viscosity lava.

Almost all maria are on the near side of the Moon, and cover 31% of the surface of the near side compared with 2% of the far side. This is likely due to a [concentration of heat-producing elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KREEP "KREEP") under the crust on the near side, which would have caused the underlying mantle to heat up, partially melt, rise to the surface and erupt. Most of the Moon's [mare basalts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_mare "Lunar mare") erupted during the [Imbrian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imbrian "Imbrian"), 3.3–3.7 billion years ago, though some are as young as 1.2 billion years and as old as 4.2 billion years.

In 2006, a study of [Ina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ina_(crater) "Ina (crater)"), a tiny depression in [Lacus Felicitatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacus_Felicitatis "Lacus Felicitatis"), found jagged, relatively dust-free features that, because of the lack of erosion by infalling debris, appeared to be only 2 million years old. [Moonquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonquake "Moonquake") and releases of gas indicate continued lunar activity. Evidence of recent lunar volcanism has been identified at 70 [irregular mare patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irregular_mare_patch "Irregular mare patch"), some less than 50 million years old. This raises the possibility of a much warmer lunar mantle than previously believed, at least on the near side where the deep crust is substantially warmer because of the greater concentration of radioactive elements. Evidence has been found for 2–10 million years old basaltic volcanism within the crater Lowell, inside the Orientale basin. Some combination of an initially hotter mantle and local enrichment of heat-producing elements in the mantle could be responsible for prolonged activities on the far side in the Orientale basin.

The lighter-colored regions of the Moon are called *terrae*, or more commonly *highlands*, because they are higher than most maria. They have been radiometrically dated to having formed 4.4 billion years ago and may represent [plagioclase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagioclase "Plagioclase") [cumulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulates "Cumulates") of the lunar magma ocean. In contrast to Earth, no major lunar mountains are believed to have formed as a result of tectonic events.

The concentration of maria on the near side likely reflects the substantially thicker crust of the highlands of the Far Side, which may have formed in a slow-velocity impact of a second moon of Earth a few tens of millions of years after the Moon's formation. Alternatively, it may be a consequence of asymmetrical [tidal heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_heating "Tidal heating") when the Moon was much closer to the Earth.

#### Impact craters

A major geologic process that has affected the Moon's surface is [impact cratering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater "Impact crater"), with craters formed when asteroids and comets collide with the lunar surface. There are estimated to be roughly 300,000 craters wider than 1 km (0.6 mi) on the Moon's near side. Lunar craters exhibit a variety of forms, depending on their size. In order of increasing diameter, the basic types are simple craters with smooth bowl shaped interiors and upturned rims, [complex craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_crater "Complex crater") with flat floors, terraced walls and central peaks, [peak ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_ring "Peak ring") basins, and [multi-ring basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-ring_basin "Multi-ring basin") with two or more concentric rings of peaks. The vast majority of impact craters are circular, but some, like [Cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor_(crater) "Cantor (crater)") and [Janssen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janssen_(lunar_crater) "Janssen (lunar crater)"), have more polygonal outlines, possibly guided by underlying tectonic rifts. Others, such as the [Messier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messier_(crater) "Messier (crater)") pair, [Schiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiller_(crater) "Schiller (crater)"), and [Daniell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniell_(crater) "Daniell (crater)"), are elongated. Such elongation can result from highly oblique impacts, [binary asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_asteroid "Binary asteroid") impacts, fragmentation of impactors before surface strike, or closely spaced [secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_crater "Secondary crater") impacts.

#### Lunar swirls

Lunar swirls are enigmatic features found across the Moon's surface. They are characterized by a high albedo, appear optically immature (i.e. have the optical characteristics of a relatively young [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith")), and often have a sinuous shape. Their shape is often accentuated by low [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo "Albedo") regions that wind between the bright swirls. They are located in places with enhanced surface [magnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field "Magnetic field") and many are located at the [antipodal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodal_point "Antipodal point") of major impacts. Well known swirls include the [Reiner Gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reiner_Gamma "Reiner Gamma") feature and [Mare Ingenii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Ingenii "Mare Ingenii"). They are hypothesized to be areas that have been partially shielded from the [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind "Solar wind"), resulting in slower [space weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_weathering "Space weathering").

### Presence of water

Liquid water cannot persist on the lunar surface. When exposed to solar radiation, water quickly decomposes through a process known as [photodissociation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photodissociation "Photodissociation") and is lost to space. However, since the 1960s, scientists have hypothesized that water ice may be deposited by impacting [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comets "Comets") or possibly produced by the reaction of oxygen-rich lunar rocks, and hydrogen from [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind "Solar wind"), leaving traces of water which could possibly persist in cold, permanently shadowed craters at either pole on the Moon. Computer simulations suggest that up to 14,000 km2 (5,400 sq mi) of the surface may be in permanent shadow. The presence of usable quantities of water on the Moon is an important factor in rendering [lunar habitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonization_of_the_Moon "Colonization of the Moon") as a cost-effective plan; the alternative of transporting water from Earth would be prohibitively expensive.

In years since, signatures of water have been found to exist on the lunar surface. In 1994, the [bistatic radar experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clementine_mission#Bistatic_Radar_Experiment "Clementine mission") located on the *[Clementine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clementine_(spacecraft) "Clementine (spacecraft)")* spacecraft, indicated the existence of small, frozen pockets of water close to the surface. However, later radar observations by [Arecibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_Telescope "Arecibo Telescope"), suggest these findings may rather be rocks ejected from young impact craters. In 1998, the [neutron spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Prospector#Neutron_Spectrometer_(NS) "Lunar Prospector") on the *Lunar Prospector* spacecraft showed that high concentrations of hydrogen are present in the first meter of depth in the regolith near the polar regions. Volcanic lava beads, brought back to Earth aboard Apollo 15, showed small amounts of water in their interior.

The 2008 *[Chandrayaan-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrayaan-1 "Chandrayaan-1")* spacecraft has since confirmed the existence of surface water ice, using the on-board [Moon Mineralogy Mapper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Mineralogy_Mapper "Moon Mineralogy Mapper"). The spectrometer observed absorption lines common to [hydroxyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxyl "Hydroxyl"), in reflected sunlight, providing evidence of large quantities of water ice, on the lunar surface. The spacecraft showed that concentrations may possibly be as high as 1,000 [ppm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_per_million "Parts per million"). Using the mapper's reflectance spectra, indirect lighting of areas in shadow confirmed water ice within 20° latitude of both poles in 2018. In 2009, *[LCROSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCROSS "LCROSS")* sent a 2,300 kg (5,100 lb) impactor into a [permanently shadowed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanently_shadowed_crater "Permanently shadowed crater") polar crater, and detected at least 100 kg (220 lb) of water in a plume of ejected material. Another examination of the LCROSS data showed the amount of detected water to be closer to 155 ± 12 kg (342 ± 26 lb).

In May 2011, 615–1410 ppm water in [melt inclusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melt_inclusions "Melt inclusions") in lunar sample 74220 was reported, the famous high-titanium "orange glass soil" of volcanic origin collected during the [Apollo 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_17 "Apollo 17") mission in 1972. The inclusions were formed during explosive eruptions on the Moon approximately 3.7 billion years ago. This concentration is comparable with that of magma in Earth's [upper mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_mantle "Upper mantle").

Analysis of the findings of the Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3) revealed in August 2018 for the first time "definitive evidence" for water-ice on the lunar surface. The data revealed the distinct reflective signatures of water-ice, as opposed to dust and other reflective substances. The ice deposits were found on the North and South poles, although it is more abundant in the South, where water is trapped in permanently shadowed craters and crevices, allowing it to persist as ice on the surface since they are shielded from the sun.

In October 2020, astronomers reported detecting [molecular water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water "Water") on the sunlit surface of the Moon by several independent spacecraft, including the [Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratospheric_Observatory_for_Infrared_Astronomy "Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy") (SOFIA).

## Earth–Moon system

### Orbit

The [semi-major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-major_axis "Semi-major axis") of the geocentric lunar orbit, called the [lunar distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance "Lunar distance"), is approximately 385,000 km (239,000 mi), or 1.3 [light-seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-second "Light-second"), comparable to going [around Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumference_of_Earth "Circumference of Earth") approximately 9.6 times. The Moon's orbit is not perfectly circular but rather slightly elliptical, with an [orbital eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity "Orbital eccentricity") of 0.055. This means the distance between the Moon and Earth varies from around 356,400 km (221,500 mi) ([perigee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perigee "Perigee")) to 406,700 km (252,700 mi) (apogee), making the Moon's distance and apparent size fluctuate up to 14%.

The Earth–Moon system has a shared center of mass, or [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter "Barycenter"), which is located 5,000 km (3,100 mi) (about 3/4 of Earth's radius) from the Earth's center. Both the Earth and the Moon actually orbit this center of mass, so Earth wobbles around this point for every orbit the Moon makes. This barycenter is slowly moving outward as tidal friction drains energy from the rotating pair, and eventually the barycenter will be outside the Earth.

The Moon makes a complete orbit around Earth with respect to the fixed stars, its [sidereal period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_period "Sidereal period"), about once every 27.3 days. However, because the Earth–Moon system moves at the same time in its orbit around the Sun, it takes slightly longer to return to the same [lunar phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase "Lunar phase"), 29.5 days, completing a full cycle as seen from Earth. This [synodic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synodic_period "Synodic period") or synodic month is commonly known as the [lunar month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_month "Lunar month") and is equal to the length of the [solar day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_day "Solar day") on the Moon.

The Moon's orbital plane maintains a relatively constant inclination, about 5 degrees, relative to Earth's orbit around the Sun (the [ecliptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic "Ecliptic")). The direction of this tilt gradually rotates westward, called [nodal precession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_precession#Nodal_precession "Lunar precession"), completing a circle once every 18.61 years. This means that the Moon's orbit changes its tilt relative to Earth's equator from 18 to 28 degrees in that time. The shape of the orbit rotates as well, along the plane of the Moon's orbit, called [apsidal precession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_precession#Apsidal_precession "Lunar precession"). Measured from the [intersection points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_node "Lunar node") of the Moon's orbit with the ecliptic plane (which themselves move westward by nodal precession), it rotates eastward with a period of 8.85 years. Unlike most [regular satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_satellite "Regular satellite") of other planets, the Moon's orbital plane is closer to the [ecliptic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_plane "Ecliptic plane") than to their planet's [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane "Equatorial plane"). The Moon's orbit is subtly [perturbed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbation_(astronomy) "Perturbation (astronomy)") by the Sun and Earth in many small, complex and interacting ways. These follow-on effects are mathematically described by [Cassini's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini%27s_laws "Cassini's laws").

### Rotation

Due to it being [tidally locked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidally_locked "Tidally locked") to Earth, the Moon has a 1:1 spin–orbit resonance. The rotation–orbit ratio means the Moon's orbital period around Earth is equal to its [rotation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_period "Rotation period"), called synchronous rotation. This results in it always keeping nearly the same face turned towards the Earth as it orbits. The side of the Moon that faces Earth is called the [near side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_side "Near side"), and the opposite the [far side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon "Far side of the Moon"). Since the Moon is not tidally locked to the Sun, lunar daylight and night times both occur around the Moon. The far side is often inaccurately called the "dark side", but it is in fact illuminated as often as the near side: once every 29.5 Earth days. During [dark moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_moon "Dark moon") to [new moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_moon "New moon"), the near side is dark.

Lunar night is the darkest on the far side and [during lunar eclipses on the near side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipses_on_the_Moon "Solar eclipses on the Moon") (and darker than a moonless night on Earth). During its night, the near side is illuminated by [Earthlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthlight "Earthlight") to the extent that lunar surface features may be observable from Earth. Earthshine makes the night on the near side about 43 times brighter, and sometimes even 55 times brighter than a night on Earth illuminated by the light of the full moon.

The moon's [axial tilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt "Axial tilt") with respect to the [ecliptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic "Ecliptic") is 1.5427°, so in each [draconic year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draconic_year "Draconic year") (346.62 days), the sun moves from being 1.5427° north of the lunar equator to being 1.5427° south of it and then back. This is analogous to how, on Earth, the sun's position moves from the [Tropic of Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer "Tropic of Cancer") to the [Tropic of Capricorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn "Tropic of Capricorn") and back every [tropical year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_year "Tropical year"). This puts the poles of the moon in darkness (or with only part of the sun visible) for half a draconic year and in sunlight for half a draconic year. The amount of sunlight falling on horizontal areas near the poles depends on the [altitude angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_angle "Altitude angle") of the sun. These seasons have little effect in more equatorial areas.

The Moon originally rotated at a faster rate, but early in its history its rotation slowed and became tidally locked in this orientation as a result of [frictional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction "Friction") effects associated with [tidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force "Tidal force") deformations caused by Earth. With time, the energy of rotation of the Moon on its axis was dissipated as heat, until there was no net rotation of the Moon relative to Earth. In 2016, planetary scientists using data collected on the 1998–99 NASA *[Lunar Prospector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Prospector "Lunar Prospector")* mission found two hydrogen-rich areas (most likely former water ice) on opposite sides of the Moon. It is speculated that these patches were the poles of the Moon billions of years ago before it was tidally locked to Earth.

### Tidal effects

The gravitational attraction that Earth and the Moon (as well as the Sun) exert on each other manifests in a slightly greater attraction on the sides closest to each other, resulting in [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_forces "Tidal forces"). [Ocean tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_tides "Ocean tides") are the most widely experienced result of this, but tidal forces also considerably affect other mechanics of Earth, as well as the Moon and their system.

The lunar solid crust experiences tides of around 10 cm (4 in) amplitude over 27 days, with three components: a fixed one due to Earth, because they are in [synchronous rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_rotation "Synchronous rotation"), a variable tide due to orbital eccentricity and inclination, and a small varying component from the Sun. The Earth-induced variable component arises from changing distance and [libration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration "Libration"), a result of the Moon's orbital eccentricity and inclination (if the Moon's orbit were perfectly circular and un-inclined, there would only be solar tides). According to recent research, scientists suggest that the Moon's influence on the Earth may contribute to maintaining [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_magnetic_field "Earth's magnetic field").

The cumulative effects of stress built up by these tidal forces produces [moonquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonquakes "Moonquakes"). Moonquakes are much less common and weaker than are earthquakes, although moonquakes can last for up to an hour – significantly longer than terrestrial quakes – because of scattering of the seismic vibrations in the dry fragmented upper crust. The existence of moonquakes was an unexpected discovery from [seismometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismometer "Seismometer") placed on the Moon by [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program "Apollo program") [astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronaut "Astronaut") from 1969 through 1972.

The most commonly known effect of tidal forces is elevated sea levels called ocean tides. While the Moon exerts most of the tidal forces, the Sun also exerts tidal forces and therefore contributes to the tides as much as 40% of the Moon's tidal force; producing in interplay the [spring and neap tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_tide "Spring tide").

The tides are two bulges in the Earth's oceans, one on the side facing the Moon and the other on the side opposite. As the Earth rotates on its axis, one of the ocean bulges (high tide) is held in place "under" the Moon, while another such tide is opposite. The tide under the Moon is explained by the Moon's gravity being stronger on the water close to it. The tide on the opposite side can be explained either by the centrifugal force as the Earth orbits the [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter "Barycenter") or by the water's inertia as the Moon's gravity is stronger on the solid Earth close to it and it is pulled away from the farther water.

Thus, there are two high tides, and two low tides in about 24 hours. Since the Moon is orbiting the Earth in the same direction of the Earth's rotation, the high tides occur about every 12 hours and 25 minutes; the 25 minutes is due to the Moon's time to orbit the Earth.

If the Earth were a water world (one with no continents) it would produce a tide of only one meter, and that tide would be very predictable, but the ocean tides are greatly modified by other effects:

* the frictional coupling of water to Earth's rotation through the ocean floors
* the [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia "Inertia") of water's movement
* ocean basins that grow shallower near land
* the sloshing of water between different ocean basins

### System evolution

Delays in the tidal peaks of both ocean and solid-body tides cause [torque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque "Torque") in opposition to the Earth's rotation. This "drains" [angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum "Angular momentum") and rotational [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy "Kinetic energy") from Earth's rotation, slowing the Earth's rotation. 3.2 billion years ago, during the [Archean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archean "Archean"), the solar day was only around 13 hours. The angular momentum, lost from the rotation of the Earth, is transferred to the Moon in a process known as [tidal acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_acceleration "Tidal acceleration"), which lifts the Moon into a higher orbit while lowering orbital speed around the Earth.

Thus the distance between Earth and Moon is increasing, and the Earth's rotation is slowing in reaction. Measurements from laser reflectors left during the Apollo missions ([lunar ranging experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Laser_Ranging_experiments "Lunar Laser Ranging experiments")) have found that the Moon's distance increases by 38 mm (1.5 in) per year (roughly the rate at which human fingernails grow).
[Atomic clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_clock "Atomic clock") show that Earth's Day lengthens by about 17 [microseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsecond "Microsecond") every year, slowly increasing the rate at which [UTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC "UTC") is adjusted by [leap seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_second "Leap second").

Over time this tidal drag causes the Earth's rotation and the Moon's orbital period to match, resulting in the lighter body becoming tidally locked (as the Moon currently is). Theoretically, in 50 billion years, the Earth's rotation will slow to match the Moon's orbital period, causing the Earth to always present the same side to the Moon. Long before this would happen, however, the Sun will become a [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant#The_Sun_as_a_red_giant "Red giant"), possibly engulfing the Earth and the Moon. If the Earth–Moon system survives this engulfment, as a 2026 study suggests, the drag from the solar atmosphere could cause the orbit of the Moon to decay. Once the orbit of the Moon closes to a distance of 18,470 km (11,480 mi), it will cross Earth's [Roche limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roche_limit "Roche limit") and the tidal interaction with Earth would break the Moon apart. It would turn into a [ring system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_system "Ring system"), with most of the orbiting rings decaying and falling to Earth.

## Observation

### Apparent size and position

The Moon appears in Earth's sky differently depending on the position of observation on Earth and the time of [lunar year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_year "Lunar year"), [lunar month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_month "Lunar month") and day on Earth. During a year the Moon [culminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culmination "Culmination") during a day at different [altitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_(astronomy) "Altitude (astronomy)"). The full Moon appears highest in the sky during winter and lowest during summer, unlike the Sun, which is true for the seasons of both of Earth's northern and southern hemispheres.

At the [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole "North Pole") and [South Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole "South Pole") the Moon is 24 hours above the horizon for two weeks every [tropical month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_month "Tropical month") (about 27.3 days), comparable to the [polar day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_day "Polar day") of the [tropical year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_year "Tropical year"). [Zooplankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zooplankton "Zooplankton") in the [Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic "Arctic") use [moonlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonlight "Moonlight") when the Sun is [below the horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night "Polar night") for months on end.

On average the Moon's [angular diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_diameter "Angular diameter") is about 0.52°, roughly the same apparent size as the Sun (see [§ Eclipses](#Eclipses)). Its apparent size and brightness vary due to its elliptical orbit, appearing up to 30% brighter and 14% larger at perigee compared to apogee. In addition, a purely psychological effect, known as the [Moon illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_illusion "Moon illusion"), makes the Moon appear larger when close to the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon "Horizon").

### Orientation and appearance

The apparent orientation of the Moon depends on its position in the sky and the hemisphere of the Earth from which it is being viewed. In the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere "Northern Hemisphere") it appears upside down compared to the view from the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere "Southern Hemisphere"). Sometimes the "horns" of a crescent moon appear to be pointing more upwards than sideways. This phenomenon is called a [wet moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_moon "Wet moon") and occurs more frequently in the [tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics "Tropics").

The tidally locked [synchronous rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_rotation "Synchronous rotation") of the Moon as it orbits the Earth results in only one side of the Moon, its so-called [near side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_side "Near side"), being visible from Earth. That said, while the movement of the Moon is in resonance, it still is not without nuances such as [libration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration "Libration"), resulting in slightly changing perspectives, making over time and location on Earth about 59% of the Moon's surface visible from Earth. Due to lunar libration, it is sometimes possible to see slightly more than the full moon (up to about 101%) or a small part of its far side (up to 5%).

#### Observational phenomena

There has been historical controversy over whether observed features on the Moon's surface change over time. Today, many of these claims are thought to be illusory, resulting from observation under different lighting conditions, poor [astronomical seeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_seeing "Astronomical seeing"), or inadequate drawings. However, [outgassing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgassing "Outgassing") does occasionally occur and could be responsible for a minor percentage of the reported [lunar transient phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_transient_phenomena "Lunar transient phenomena"). Recently, it has been suggested that a roughly 3 km (1.9 mi) diameter region of the lunar surface was modified by a gas release event about a million years ago.

### Phases

The Moon rotates, as it orbits Earth, changing orientation toward the Sun, experiencing a [lunar day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_day "Lunar day"). A lunar day is equal to one [lunar month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_month "Lunar month") (one synodic orbit around Earth). The changing position of the illumination of the Moon by the Sun during a lunar day is observable from Earth as the changing [lunar phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase "Lunar phase"). The phases of the moon follow a repeating cycle, with its starting point at new moon. In the first half of the cycle, the sequence is new moon, crescent, quarter, gibbous, and full moon. In the second half of the cycle, the sequence is reversed, then the cycle repeats.

The brightness and apparent size of the Moon changes also due to its elliptic orbit around Earth. Consequently, given the same phase, the Moon's brightness also varies by up to 30% between apogee and perigee. At [perigee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perigee "Perigee") (closest), since the Moon is up to 14% closer to Earth than at [apogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee "Apogee") (most distant), it subtends a [solid angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_angle "Solid angle") which is up to 30% larger. A full (or new) moon at such a position is called a [supermoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermoon "Supermoon").

Each of the four intermediate phases of the Moon lasts, on average, about seven days (approximately 7.38 days), but this duration can vary by about ±11% due to the change in the distance between the Moon and Earth between apogee (farthest point) and perigee (closest point).

The number of days since the most recent new moon is called the Moon's age. A complete cycle of lunar phases is known as a lunation.

The approximate age and phase of the Moon for any date can be calculated by determining the number of days since a known new moon (e.g., January 1, 1900, or August 11, 1999) and dividing that value by the average length of a synodic month (29.53059 days). The remainder of the division represents the Moon's age. This method assumes a perfectly circular orbit and does not consider the exact time of the new moon, so results may be off by a few hours. Accuracy decreases as the date moves away from the reference date. This simplified calculation is suitable for many purposes, but more precise applications require more complex calculation.

### True albedo and color of the surface

The Moon's appearance is shaped by its reflective properties and surface composition. Its [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo "Albedo") is about 0.12, meaning it reflects 12% of the light that hits it. This is similar to an asteroid's albedo. [Chromophore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromophore "Chromophore") elements, iron and titanium, on the Moon surface absorb light, making areas like the lunar mares darker (lower albedo) than the highlands.

The Moon's true color, without atmospheric effects, is a muted brownish-gray. From Earth, the Moon's color can be altered by the atmosphere, at times appearing red or blue. It may appear red during a [lunar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse "Lunar eclipse"), because of the red spectrum of the Sun's light being [refracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracted "Refracted") onto the Moon by Earth's atmosphere. Because of this red color, lunar eclipses are also sometimes called blood moons. The Moon can also seem red when it appears at low angles and through a thick atmosphere. The Moon may also appear blue depending on the presence of certain particles in the air, such as volcanic particles. Because the words "blood moon" and "[blue moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_moon "Blue moon")" can also be used to refer to specific [full moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_moon "Full moon") of the year, they do not always refer to the presence of red or blue [moonlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonlight "Moonlight").

### Eclipses

Eclipses only occur when the Sun, Earth, and Moon are all in a straight line (termed "[syzygy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syzygy_(astronomy) "Syzygy (astronomy)")"). [Solar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse "Solar eclipse") occur at [new moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_moon "New moon"), when the Moon is between the Sun and Earth. In contrast, [lunar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse "Lunar eclipse") occur at full moon, when Earth is between the Sun and Moon. The apparent size of the Moon is roughly the same as that of the Sun, with both being viewed at close to one-half a degree wide. The Sun is much larger than the Moon, but it is the vastly greater distance that gives it the same apparent size as the much closer and much smaller Moon from the perspective of Earth. The variations in apparent size, due to the non-circular orbits, are nearly the same as well, though occurring in different cycles. This makes possible both [total](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_eclipse "Total eclipse") (with the Moon appearing larger than the Sun) and [annular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annular_eclipse "Annular eclipse") (with the Moon appearing smaller than the Sun) solar eclipses. In a total eclipse, the Moon completely covers the disc of the Sun and the [solar corona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_corona "Solar corona") becomes visible to the [naked eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_eye "Naked eye").

Because the distance between the Moon and Earth is very slowly increasing over time, the angular diameter of the Moon is decreasing. As the Sun evolves toward becoming a [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant "Red giant"), its size and its apparent diameter in the sky are slowly increasing. The combination of these two changes means that hundreds of millions of years ago, the Moon would always completely cover the Sun on solar eclipses, and no annular eclipses were possible. Likewise, hundreds of millions of years in the future, the Moon will no longer cover the Sun completely, and total solar eclipses will not occur.

As the Moon's orbit around Earth is inclined by about 5.145° (5° 9′) to the [orbit of Earth around the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic "Ecliptic"), eclipses do not occur at every full and new moon. For an eclipse to occur, the Moon must be near the intersection of the two orbital planes. The periodicity and recurrence of eclipses of the Sun by the Moon, and of the Moon by Earth, is described by the [saros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saros_(astronomy) "Saros (astronomy)"), which has a period of approximately 18 years.

Because the Moon continuously blocks the view of a half-degree-wide circular area of the sky, the related phenomenon of [occultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation "Occultation") occurs when a bright star or planet passes behind the Moon and is occulted: hidden from view. In this way, a solar eclipse is an occultation of the Sun. Because the Moon is comparatively close to Earth, occultations of individual stars are not visible everywhere on the planet, nor at the same time. Because of the [precession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession "Precession") of the lunar orbit, each year different stars are occulted.

## History of scientific understanding and exploration

### Pre-telescopic observation (before 1609)

It is possible that the waxing and waning of the [lunar phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phases "Lunar phases") were tracked to keep time in the oldest cave paintings of bulls and geometric shapes, from up to 40,000 years ago, or by the use of [tally sticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tally_stick "Tally stick") from 20–30,000 years ago.
Aspects of the Moon were identified and aggregated in [lunar deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_deities "Lunar deities") from prehistoric times and were eventually documented and put into symbols from the very first instances of writing in the 4th millennium BC. One of the earliest-discovered possible depictions of the Moon is a 3,000 BCE rock carving *Orthostat 47* at [Knowth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowth "Knowth"), Ireland. The crescent depicting the Moon as with the lunar deity [Nanna/Sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin_(mythology) "Sin (mythology)") have been found from the 3rd millennium BC.

The oldest named astronomer and poet [Enheduanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna "Enheduanna"), [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire "Akkadian Empire") high priestess to the lunar deity Nanna/Sin and princess, daughter of [Sargon the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_the_Great "Sargon the Great") (c. 2334 – c. 2279 BCE), had the Moon tracked in her chambers. The oldest identified depiction of the Moon in an astronomical relation to other astronomical features is the [Nebra sky disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebra_sky_disc "Nebra sky disc") from c. 1800–1600 BCE, depicting features like the [Pleiades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiades "Pleiades") next to the Moon.

The [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") philosopher [Anaxagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxagoras "Anaxagoras") (d. 428 BC) reasoned that the Sun and Moon were both giant spherical rocks, and that the Moon reflected the Sun's light. Elsewhere in the 5th century BC to 4th century BC, [Babylonian astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomers "Babylonian astronomers") had recorded the 18-year [Saros cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saros_cycle "Saros cycle") of [lunar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse "Lunar eclipse"), and [Indian astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy "Indian astronomy") had described the Moon's monthly elongation. The [Chinese astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_astronomer "Chinese astronomer") [Shi Shen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi_Shen "Shi Shen") ([fl.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fl. "Fl.") 4th century BC) gave instructions for predicting solar and lunar eclipses.

In [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle")'s (384–322 BC) [description of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Heavens "On the Heavens"), the Moon marked the boundary between the spheres of the mutable elements (earth, water, air and fire), and the imperishable stars of [aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aether_(classical_element) "Aether (classical element)"), an influential concept from [Aristotelian physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_physics "Aristotelian physics") that would dominate for centuries. [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes") (287–212 BC) designed a planetarium that could calculate the motions of the Moon and other objects in the Solar System. In the 2nd century BC, [Seleucus of Seleucia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_of_Seleucia "Seleucus of Seleucia") correctly thought that tides were due to the attraction of the Moon, and that their height depends on the Moon's position relative to the Sun. In the same century, [Aristarchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Sizes_and_Distances_(Aristarchus) "On the Sizes and Distances (Aristarchus)") computed the size and distance of the Moon from Earth, obtaining a value of about twenty times the radius of Earth for the distance.

The Chinese of the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") believed the Moon to be energy equated to *[qi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qi "Qi")* and their 'radiating influence' theory recognized that the light of the Moon was merely a reflection of the Sun; [Jing Fang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jing_Fang "Jing Fang") (78–37 BC) noted the sphericity of the Moon. [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy") (90–168 AD) greatly improved on the numbers of Aristarchus, calculating a mean distance of 59 times Earth's radius and a diameter of 0.292 Earth diameters, close to the correct values of about 60 and 0.273 respectively. In 510 AD, the Indian astronomer [Aryabhata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata "Aryabhata") mentioned in his *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya "Aryabhatiya")* that reflected sunlight is the cause of the shining of the Moon. The astronomer and physicist [Ibn al-Haytham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham") (965–1039) found that sunlight was not reflected from the Moon like a mirror, but that light was emitted from every part of the Moon's sunlit surface in all directions. [Shen Kuo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shen_Kuo "Shen Kuo") (1031–1095) of the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty "Song dynasty") created an allegory equating the [waxing and waning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waxing_and_waning "Waxing and waning") of the Moon to a round ball of reflective silver that, when doused with white powder and viewed from the side, would appear to be a crescent. During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), before the invention of the telescope, the Moon was increasingly recognized as a sphere, though many believed that it was "perfectly smooth".

### Telescopic exploration (1609–1959)

The [telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope "Telescope") was developed and reported on in 1608. The first record of telescopic astronomy and rough mapping of the Moon's features is from early summer 1609 by [Thomas Harriot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Harriot "Thomas Harriot"), but which he did not publish. At the same time [Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei") too started to use telescopes to observe the sky and the Moon, recording later that year more detailed observations and crucial conclusions, such as that the Moon was not smooth, featuring mountains and craters, which he published in 1610 in his ground-breaking and soon widely discussed book *[Sidereus Nuncius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius "Sidereus Nuncius")*.

Later in the 17th century, the efforts of [Giovanni Battista Riccioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Riccioli "Giovanni Battista Riccioli") and [Francesco Maria Grimaldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Maria_Grimaldi "Francesco Maria Grimaldi") led to the system of naming of lunar features in use today. The more exact 1834–1836 *[Mappa Selenographica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mappa_Selenographica "Mappa Selenographica")* of [Wilhelm Beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Beer "Wilhelm Beer") and [Johann Heinrich von Mädler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Heinrich_von_M%C3%A4dler "Johann Heinrich von Mädler"), and their associated 1837 book *Der Mond*, the first [trigonometrically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry") accurate study of lunar features, included the heights of more than a thousand mountains, and introduced the study of the Moon at accuracies possible in earthly geography. Lunar craters, first noted by Galileo, were thought to be [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic "Volcanic") until the 1870s proposal of [Richard Proctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Proctor "Richard Proctor") that they were formed by collisions. This view gained support in 1892 from the experimentation of geologist [Grove Karl Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_Karl_Gilbert "Grove Karl Gilbert"), and from comparative studies from 1920 to the 1940s, leading to the development of [lunar stratigraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_geologic_timescale "Lunar geologic timescale"), which by the 1950s was becoming a new and growing branch of [astrogeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrogeology "Astrogeology").

### First missions to the Moon (1959–1976)

After [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") the first [launch systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_system "Launch system") were developed and by the end of the 1950s they reached capabilities that allowed the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") to launch [spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft "Spacecraft") into space. The [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") fueled a closely followed development of launch systems by the two states, resulting in the so-called [Space Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Race "Space Race") and its later phase the Moon Race, accelerating efforts and interest in [exploration of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon "Exploration of the Moon").

After the first spaceflight of [Sputnik 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1 "Sputnik 1") in 1957 during [International Geophysical Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Geophysical_Year "International Geophysical Year") the spacecraft of the Soviet Union's [*Luna* program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_programme "Luna programme") were the first to accomplish a number of goals. Following three unnamed failed missions in 1958, the first human-made object *[Luna 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_1 "Luna 1")* escaped Earth's gravity and passed the Moon on 4 January 1959. Later that year the first human-made object *[Luna 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_2 "Luna 2")* reached the Moon's surface by [intentionally impacting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lander_(spacecraft)#Impactors "Lander (spacecraft)"). By the end of the year *[Luna 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_3 "Luna 3")* reached as the first human-made object the normally occluded [far side of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon "Far side of the Moon"), taking the first photographs of it. The first spacecraft to perform a successful lunar [soft landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lander_(spacecraft) "Lander (spacecraft)") was *[Luna 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_9 "Luna 9")* and the first vehicle to orbit the Moon was *[Luna 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_10 "Luna 10")*, both in 1966.

Following President [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy "John F. Kennedy")'s 1961 commitment to a crewed Moon landing before the end of the decade, the United States, under NASA leadership, launched a series of uncrewed probes to develop an understanding of the lunar surface in preparation for human missions: the [Jet Propulsion Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory "Jet Propulsion Laboratory")'s [Ranger program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranger_program "Ranger program"), the [Lunar Orbiter program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Orbiter_program "Lunar Orbiter program") and the [Surveyor program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveyor_program "Surveyor program"). The crewed [Apollo program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program "Apollo program") was developed in parallel; after a series of uncrewed and crewed tests of the Apollo spacecraft in Earth orbit, and spurred on by a potential [Soviet lunar human landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_human_lunar_programs "Soviet human lunar programs"), in 1968 [Apollo 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_8 "Apollo 8") made the first human mission to lunar orbit.The first Earthlings, two tortoises, had circled the Moon three months earlier on the Soviet Union's [Zond 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zond_5 "Zond 5"), followed by turtles on [Zond 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zond_6 "Zond 6").

The first time a person landed on the Moon and any extraterrestrial body was when [Neil Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Armstrong "Neil Armstrong"), the commander of the American mission [Apollo 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11 "Apollo 11"), set foot on the Moon at 02:56 UTC on July 21, 1969. Considered the culmination of the [Space Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Race "Space Race"), an estimated 500 million people worldwide watched the transmission by the [Apollo TV camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_TV_camera "Apollo TV camera"), the largest television audience for a live broadcast at that time. While at the same time another mission, the robotic sample return mission [Luna 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_15 "Luna 15") by the Soviet Union had been in orbit around the Moon, becoming together with Apollo 11 the first ever case of two extraterrestrial missions being conducted at the same time.

The Apollo missions 11 to 17 (except [Apollo 13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_13 "Apollo 13"), which aborted its planned lunar landing) removed 380.05 kilograms (837.87 lb) of lunar rock and soil in 2,196 [separate samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_rock "Moon rock").
Scientific instrument packages were installed on the lunar surface during all the Apollo landings. Long-lived [instrument stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Surface_Experiments_Package "Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Package"), including heat flow probes, [seismometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismometer "Seismometer"), and [magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetometer "Magnetometer"), were installed at the [Apollo 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_12 "Apollo 12"), [14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_14 "Apollo 14"), [15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_15 "Apollo 15"), [16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_16 "Apollo 16"), and [17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_17 "Apollo 17") landing sites. Direct transmission of data to Earth concluded in late 1977 because of budgetary considerations, but as the stations' [lunar laser ranging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_laser_ranging "Lunar laser ranging") corner-cube retroreflector arrays are passive instruments, they are still being used.
Apollo 17 in 1972 was the last crewed mission to the Moon's orbit or surface, and the last to the Moon's vicinity until the [Artemis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_II "Artemis II") lunar flyby in 2026. [Explorer 49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorer_49 "Explorer 49") in 1973 was the last dedicated U.S. lunar probe until 1994.

The Soviet Union continued sending robotic missions to the Moon until 1976, deploying in 1970 with [Luna 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_17 "Luna 17") the first remote controlled [rover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rover_(space_exploration) "Rover (space exploration)") [Lunokhod 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunokhod_1 "Lunokhod 1") on an extraterrestrial surface, and collecting and returning 0.3 kg of rock and soil samples with three *Luna* [sample return missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_return_mission "Sample return mission") (*[Luna 16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_16 "Luna 16")* in 1970, *[Luna 20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_20 "Luna 20")* in 1972, and *[Luna 24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_24 "Luna 24")* in 1976).

### Moon Treaty and explorational absence (1976–1990)

Following the [last Soviet mission to the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_24 "Luna 24") of 1976, there was little further lunar exploration for fourteen years. Astronautics had shifted its focus towards the exploration of the [inner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Solar_System "Inner Solar System") (e.g. [Venera program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_program "Venera program")) and [outer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Solar_System "Outer Solar System") (e.g. [Pioneer 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_10 "Pioneer 10"), 1972) [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System") [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet "Planet"), but also towards [Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_orbit "Geocentric orbit"), developing and continuously operating, beside [communication satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_satellite "Communication satellite"), [Earth observation satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_observation_satellite "Earth observation satellite") (e.g. [Landsat program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landsat_program "Landsat program"), 1972), [space telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_telescope "Space telescope") and particularly [space stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_station "Space station") (e.g. [Salyut program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_program "Salyut program"), 1971).

Negotiation in 1979 of [Moon treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_treaty "Moon treaty"), and its subsequent ratification in 1984 was the only major activity regarding the Moon until 1990.

### Renewed exploration (1990–present)

In 1990 *[Hiten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiten_(spacecraft) "Hiten (spacecraft)")* – *Hagoromo*, the first dedicated lunar mission since 1976, reached the Moon's orbit. Sent by Japan, it became the first lunar mission that was not a Soviet Union or U.S. mission.

In 1994, the U.S. dedicated a mission to fly a spacecraft (*[Clementine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clementine_(spacecraft) "Clementine (spacecraft)")*) to the Moon again for the first time since 1973. This mission obtained the first near-global topographic map of the Moon, and the first global [multispectral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multispectral "Multispectral") images of the lunar surface. In 1998, this was followed by the *[Lunar Prospector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Prospector "Lunar Prospector")* mission, whose instruments indicated the presence of excess hydrogen at the lunar poles, which is likely to have been caused by the presence of water ice in the upper few meters of the regolith within permanently shadowed craters.

The next years saw a row of first missions to the Moon by a new group of states actively exploring the Moon.
Between 2004 and 2006 the first spacecraft by the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency "European Space Agency") (ESA) ([SMART-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMART-1 "SMART-1")) orbited the Moon, recording the first detailed survey of chemical elements on the lunar surface.
The [Chinese Lunar Exploration Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Lunar_Exploration_Program "Chinese Lunar Exploration Program") reached the Moon for the first time with the orbiter *[Chang'e 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_1 "Chang'e 1")* (2007–2009), obtaining a full image map of the Moon.
[India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Space_Research_Organisation "Indian Space Research Organisation") reached, orbited, and impacted the Moon in 2008 for the first time with its [Chandrayaan-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrayaan-1 "Chandrayaan-1") and [Moon Impact Probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Impact_Probe "Moon Impact Probe"), becoming the fifth and sixth state to do so, creating a high-resolution chemical, mineralogical and photo-geological map of the lunar surface, and confirming the presence of [water molecules in lunar soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water "Lunar water").

The U.S. launched the [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Reconnaissance_Orbiter "Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter") and the [LCROSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCROSS "LCROSS") impactor on June 18, 2009. LCROSS completed its mission by making a planned and widely observed impact in the crater [Cabeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabeus "Cabeus") on October 9, 2009, whereas *LRO* is currently in operation, obtaining precise lunar [altimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altimetry "Altimetry") and high-resolution imagery.

China continued its lunar program in 2010 with [Chang'e 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_2 "Chang'e 2"), mapping the surface at a higher resolution over an eight-month period, and in 2013 with [Chang'e 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_3 "Chang'e 3"), a lunar [lander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lander_(spacecraft) "Lander (spacecraft)") along with a [lunar rover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_rover "Lunar rover") named *[Yutu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yutu_(rover) "Yutu (rover)")* ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language"): 玉兔; lit. 'Jade Rabbit'). This was the first lunar rover mission since *[Lunokhod 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunokhod_2 "Lunokhod 2")* in 1973 and the first lunar [soft landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_landing "Soft landing") since *[Luna 24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_24 "Luna 24")* in 1976, making China the third country to achieve this.

In 2014 the first privately funded probe, the [Manfred Memorial Moon Mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manfred_Memorial_Moon_Mission "Manfred Memorial Moon Mission"), flew by the Moon. Another Chinese rover mission, [Chang'e 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_4 "Chang'e 4"), achieved the first landing on [the Moon's far side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon "Far side of the Moon") in early 2019. Also in 2019, India successfully sent its second probe, [Chandrayaan-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrayaan-2 "Chandrayaan-2"), to the Moon. In 2020, China carried out its first robotic [sample return mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_return_mission "Sample return mission") ([Chang'e 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_5 "Chang'e 5")), bringing back 1,731 grams of lunar material to Earth.

The U.S. [developed plans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vision_for_Space_Exploration "Vision for Space Exploration") for returning astronauts to the Moon beginning in 2004, and with the signing of the U.S.-led [Artemis Accords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords "Artemis Accords") in 2020, the [Artemis program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_program "Artemis program") aims to return humans to the Moon in the 2020s. The Accords have been joined by a growing number of countries. The introduction of the Artemis Accords has fueled a renewed discussion about the international framework and cooperation of lunar activity, building on the [Moon Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Treaty "Moon Treaty") and the ESA-led [Moon Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Village "Moon Village") concept.

In 2022, South Korea launched [Danuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danuri "Danuri") successfully, its first lunar probe, from the US.<ref?["Danuri, South Korea's first Moon mission"](https://www.planetary.org/space-missions/kplo). The Planetary Society. Retrieved September 21, 2026.</ref>
In 2023 and 2024, India and Japan became the fourth and fifth country to [soft land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_landing "Soft landing") a spacecraft on the Moon, following the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") and United States in the 1960s, and China in 2013. Notably, Japan's spacecraft, the [Smart Lander for Investigating Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_Lander_for_Investigating_Moon "Smart Lander for Investigating Moon"), survived three lunar nights. In February 2024, the [IM-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-1 "IM-1") lander became the first commercially built lander to land on the Moon.

China launched [Chang'e 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_6 "Chang'e 6") in May 2024, which conducted the first sample return from the [far side of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon "Far side of the Moon"). It also carried a Chinese rover to conduct [infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_spectroscopy "Absorption spectroscopy") of lunar surface. [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan") sent a lunar orbiter called [ICUBE-Q](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICUBE-Q "ICUBE-Q") along with Chang'e 6. [Nova-C 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-2 "IM-2"), [iSpace Lander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ispace_(Japanese_company)#Hakuto-R_program "Ispace (Japanese company)") and [Blue Ghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Ghost "Blue Ghost") were all launched to the Moon in 2024.

NASA's [Artemis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_II "Artemis II") crewed lunar flyby mission launched in April 2026. It was the first crewed mission around the Moon since the Apollo era. [Christina Koch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christina_Koch "Christina Koch") became the first woman, [Victor Glover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Glover "Victor Glover") the first person of color, Canadian [Jeremy Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Hansen "Jeremy Hansen") the first non-American, and commander [Reid Wiseman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reid_Wiseman "Reid Wiseman") the oldest person, to leave [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit") and to fly around the Moon.

### Theories of formation

Historically, several formation mechanisms have been proposed, but none satisfactorily explains the features of the Earth–Moon system. A fission of the Moon from Earth's crust through [centrifugal force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_force "Centrifugal force") would require too great an initial rotation rate of Earth. Gravitational capture of a pre-formed Moon depends on an unfeasibly extended [atmosphere of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth "Atmosphere of Earth") to [dissipate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipate "Dissipate") the energy of the passing Moon. A co-formation of Earth and the Moon together in the [primordial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumplanetary_disk "Circumplanetary disk") [accretion disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accretion_disk "Accretion disk") does not explain the depletion of metals in the Moon. None of these hypotheses can account for the high [angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum "Angular momentum") of the Earth–Moon system.

## Human interaction and presence

Human presence on the Moon began with [remains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_artificial_objects_on_the_Moon "List of artificial objects on the Moon") of impactors, landings and [lunar orbiters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extraterrestrial_orbiters#Moon "List of extraterrestrial orbiters"). Orbiters like the 2009-launched [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Reconnaissance_Orbiter "Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter") continue to surveil the Moon for future missions, while landers such as the 2013-launched [Chang'e 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_3 "Chang'e 3") with its Lunar Ultraviolet Telescope are still operational as of 2020. Five retroreflectors have been installed on the Moon since the 1970s and since used for accurate measurements of the physical [librations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration "Libration") through [laser ranging to the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Laser_Ranging_experiment "Lunar Laser Ranging experiment").

### Human impact

While the Moon has the lowest [planetary protection target-categorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_protection#Target_categories "Planetary protection"), its degradation as a pristine body and scientific place has been discussed. If there is [astronomy performed from the Moon](#Astronomy_from_the_Moon), it will need to be free from any physical and [radio pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_pollution "Radio pollution"). While the Moon has no significant atmosphere, traffic and impacts on the Moon causes clouds of dust that can spread far and possibly contaminate the original state of the Moon and its special scientific content. Scholar [Alice Gorman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Gorman "Alice Gorman") asserts that, although the Moon is inhospitable, describing the Moon as dead negates its dynamics, which requires sustainable human activity to treat the Moon's ecology as a co-participant.

The so-called "[Tardigrade affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tardigrades_on_the_Moon "Tardigrades on the Moon")" of the 2019 crashed *[Beresheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beresheet "Beresheet")* lander and its carrying of [tardigrades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tardigrade "Tardigrade") has been discussed as an example for lacking measures and lacking international regulation for [planetary protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_protection "Planetary protection").

[Space debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_debris "Space debris") beyond Earth around the Moon has been considered as a future challenge with increasing numbers of missions to the Moon, particularly as a danger for such missions. As such lunar waste management has been raised as an issue which future lunar missions, particularly on the surface, need to tackle.

Human remains have been transported to the Moon, including by private companies such as [Celestis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestis "Celestis") and [Elysium Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elysium_Space "Elysium Space"). Because the Moon has been sacred or significant to many cultures, the practice of [space burials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_burial "Space burial") have attracted criticism from [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples "Indigenous peoples") leaders. For example, then–[Navajo Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_Nation "Navajo Nation") president [Albert Hale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Hale "Albert Hale") criticized NASA for sending the [cremated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremated "Cremated") ashes of scientist [Eugene Shoemaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Shoemaker "Eugene Shoemaker") to the Moon in 1998.

Beside the remains of human activity on the Moon, there have been some intended permanent installations like the *[Moon Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Museum "Moon Museum")* art piece, [Apollo 11 goodwill messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11_goodwill_messages "Apollo 11 goodwill messages"), six [lunar plaques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_plaque "Lunar plaque"), the *[Fallen Astronaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallen_Astronaut "Fallen Astronaut")* memorial, and other artifacts.

### Astronomy from the Moon

The Moon has been used as a site for astronomical and [Earth observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_observation "Earth observation"). The Earth appears in the [Moon's sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_sky#The_Moon "Extraterrestrial sky") with an [apparent size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_size "Apparent size") of 1° 48′ to 2°, three to four times the size of the Moon or Sun in Earth's sky, or about the apparent width of two little fingers at an arm's length away. Observations from the Moon started as early as 1966 with the [first images of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_first_images_of_Earth_from_space "Timeline of first images of Earth from space") from the Moon, taken by [Lunar Orbiter 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Orbiter_1 "Lunar Orbiter 1"). Of particular cultural significance is the 1968 photograph called *[Earthrise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthrise "Earthrise")*, taken by [Bill Anders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Anders "Bill Anders") of [Apollo 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_8 "Apollo 8") in 1968. In April 1972 the [Apollo 16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_16 "Apollo 16") mission set up the first dedicated telescope, the [Far Ultraviolet Camera/Spectrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_Ultraviolet_Camera/Spectrograph "Far Ultraviolet Camera/Spectrograph"), recording various astronomical photos and spectra.

The Moon is recognized as an excellent site for telescopes. It is relatively nearby; certain craters near the poles are permanently dark and cold and especially useful for [infrared telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_telescope "Infrared telescope"); and [radio telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescope "Radio telescope") on the far side would be shielded from the radio chatter of Earth. The [lunar soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_soil "Lunar soil"), although it poses a problem for any moving parts of [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope "Telescope"), can be mixed with [carbon nanotubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_nanotube "Carbon nanotube") and [epoxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoxy "Epoxy") and employed in the construction of mirrors up to 50 meters in diameter. A lunar [zenith telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith_telescope "Zenith telescope") can be made cheaply with an [ionic liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_liquid "Ionic liquid").

With increased human activity on the surface of the Moon the atmospheric circulation of lunar dust will increase, reducing the favorable conditions for astronomy from the surface depending on measures to mitigate the spread of lunar dust.

### Proposed missions

Both the U.S. and Chinese lunar programs have the goal to establish a [lunar base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_base "Lunar base") in the 2030s with their international partners, being the first crewed lunar landings since [Apollo 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_17 "Apollo 17") in 1972.

Following the [Artemis III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_III "Artemis III") test flight in [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit") scheduled for 2027, NASA plans to return humans to the lunar surface in 2028, with two [Artemis IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_IV "Artemis IV") astronauts landing on the lunar south pole. While the Apollo missions were explorational in nature, the Artemis program plans to establish a more permanent presence. To this end, the U.S. is partnering with industry for lunar communications, payload delivery, and [in situ resource utilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_situ_resource_utilization "In situ resource utilization") through programs such as NASA's [Commercial Lunar Payload Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services "Commercial Lunar Payload Services"), and [DARPA lunar programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DARPA_lunar_programs "DARPA lunar programs").

China's [Chang'e program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_program "Chang'e program") announced joint missions with Russia's [Luna-Glob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna-Glob "Luna-Glob") program. Two lunar survey missions are planned, [Chang'e 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_7 "Chang'e 7") in 2026 and [Chang'e 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_8 "Chang'e 8") in 2028, before a crewed landing in 2029 or 2030.

### Living on the Moon

Humans have lived on the Moon in groups of two and for up to three days. Altogether twelve people have stayed on the Moon over the course of six visits. They all lived in one type of [surface habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_habitat_(facility)#Extraterrestrial_surface_habitat "Space habitat (facility)"), the [Apollo Lunar Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Module "Apollo Lunar Module"). In the course of these stays some people have spent in total up to one day roaming the surface.

Challenges when roaming the surface arise from [lunar dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_dust "Lunar dust") sticking to suits and tools, and has been carried inside the habitats. Astronauts could taste and smell the dust, which smells like gunpowder and was called the "Apollo aroma". This fine lunar dust can [cause health issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_dust#Harmful_effects_of_lunar_dust "Lunar dust").

In 2019, at least one plant seed sprouted in an experiment on the [Chang'e 4 lander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_4#Lunar_lander "Change 4"). It was carried from Earth along with other small life in its *Lunar Micro Ecosystem*.

Because the Moon is tidally locked, and only shows one face to Earth, a permanent lunar presence will require a constellation of satellites to relay signals from the Moon's far side, as well as over its topographically inhibitive south pole. The Chinese [Queqiao-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queqiao-1 "Queqiao-1") [and -2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queqiao-2 "Queqiao-2") satellites are already in place, and NASA plans the [Lunar Communications Relay and Navigation Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LunaNet "LunaNet") (LCRNS) for November 2026.

### Legal status

Although *[Luna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_program "Luna program")* landers scattered pennants of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") on the Moon, and [U.S. flags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Flag_Assembly "Lunar Flag Assembly") were symbolically planted at their landing sites by the [Apollo astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_astronauts "Apollo astronauts"), no nation claims ownership of any part of the Moon's surface. Likewise no [private ownership of parts of the Moon, or as a whole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_real_estate "Extraterrestrial real estate"), is considered credible.

The 1967 [Outer Space Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Space_Treaty "Outer Space Treaty") defines the Moon and all outer space as the "[province of all mankind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_heritage_of_mankind "Common heritage of mankind")". It restricts the use of the Moon to peaceful purposes, explicitly banning military installations and [weapons of mass destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_of_mass_destruction "Weapons of mass destruction"). A majority of countries are parties of this treaty.
The 1979 [Moon Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Agreement "Moon Agreement") was created to elaborate, and restrict the exploitation of the [Moon's resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_resources "Lunar resources") by any single nation, leaving it to a yet unspecified international regulatory regime. As of January 2020, it has been signed and ratified by 18 nations, none of which have [human spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight "Human spaceflight") capabilities.

Since 2020, countries have joined the U.S. in their [Artemis Accords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_Accords "Artemis Accords"), which are challenging the treaty. The U.S. has furthermore emphasized in a presidential [executive order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_order "Executive order") ("Encouraging International Support for the Recovery and Use of Space Resources.") that "the United States does not view outer space as a 'global commons'" and calls the Moon Agreement "a failed attempt at constraining free enterprise".

With Australia signing and ratifying both the Moon Treaty in 1986 as well as the Artemis Accords in 2020, there has been a discussion if they can be harmonized. In this light an *Implementation Agreement* for the Moon Treaty has been advocated for, as a way to compensate for the shortcomings of the Moon Treaty and to harmonize it with other laws and agreements such as the Artemis Accords, allowing it to be more widely accepted.

In the face of such increasing commercial and national interest, particularly prospecting territories, U.S. lawmakers have introduced in late 2020 specific regulation for the conservation of historic landing sites and interest groups have argued for making such sites [World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Heritage_Site "World Heritage Site") and zones of scientific value protected zones, all of which add to the legal availability and territorialization of the Moon.

In 2021, the *Declaration of the Rights of the Moon* was created by a group of "lawyers, space archaeologists and concerned citizens", drawing on precedents in the [Rights of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rights_of_Nature "Rights of Nature") movement and the concept of legal personality for non-human entities in space.

#### Coordination and regulation

Increasing human activity at the Moon has raised the need for coordination to safeguard international and commercial lunar activity. Issues from cooperation to mere coordination, through for example the development of a shared [Lunar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_time "Lunar time"), have been raised.

In particular the establishment of an [international](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalism_(politics) "Internationalism (politics)") or [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations") regulatory regime for lunar human activity has been called for by the [Moon Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Treaty "Moon Treaty") and suggested through an *Implementation Agreement*, but remains contentious. Current lunar programs are [multilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilateralism "Multilateralism"), with the US-led [Artemis program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_program "Artemis program") and the China-led [International Lunar Research Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Lunar_Research_Station "International Lunar Research Station"). For broader international cooperation and coordination, the [International Lunar Exploration Working Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Lunar_Exploration_Working_Group "International Lunar Exploration Working Group") (ILEWG), the [Moon Village Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Village "Moon Village") (MVA) and more generally the International Space Exploration Coordination Group (ISECG) has been established.

## In culture and life

### Mythology

[Lunar deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_deity "Lunar deity") are common across all human cultures. Many cultures have implicitly linked the 29.5-day [lunar cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_cycle "Lunar cycle") to women's [menstrual cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menstrual_cycle "Menstrual cycle"), as evident in the shared linguistic roots of "menstruation" and "moon" words in multiple [language families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family "Language family"). This identification was not universal, as demonstrated by the fact that not all moon deities are female. Still, many well-known mythologies feature moon goddesses, including the Greek goddess [Selene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selene "Selene"), the Roman goddess [Luna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_(mythology) "Luna (mythology)"), the Chinese goddess [Chang'e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e "Chang'e"), and the Aztec goddess [Coyolxāuhqui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyolx%C4%81uhqui "Coyolxāuhqui"), whose decapitation may represent a lunar eclipse. Some male gods like Nanna-Sin where linked to childbirth and pregnancy as well. Several goddesses including [Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis "Artemis"), [Hecate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecate "Hecate"), [Melinoë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melino%C3%AB "Melinoë"), [Phoebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoebe_(Titaness) "Phoebe (Titaness)"), [Theia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theia "Theia") and [Isis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isis "Isis") did not originally have lunar aspects, and only acquired them late in antiquity due to [syncretism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism") with the de facto Greco-Roman lunar deity [Selene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selene "Selene")/[Luna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_(goddess) "Luna (goddess)").

The [splitting of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splitting_of_the_Moon "Splitting of the Moon") into two halves in the sky is a miracle attributed to the prophet [Mohammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad "Mohammad").

### Timekeeping

Since prehistoric times people have taken note of [the Moon's phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phases "Lunar phases") and its [waxing and waning cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_cycle "Lunar cycle") and used it to keep record of time.
The words for the month in a range of different languages carry this relation between the period of the month and the Moon etymologically.
This lunar timekeeping gave rise to the historically dominant, but varied, [lunisolar calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunisolar_calendar "Lunisolar calendar"). The 7th-century [Islamic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar "Islamic calendar") is an example of a purely [lunar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_calendar "Lunar calendar"), where months are traditionally determined by the visual sighting of the hilal, or earliest crescent moon, over the horizon.

Of particular significance has been the occasion of [full moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_moon "Full moon"), highlighted and celebrated in a range of calendars and cultures, an example being the Buddhist [Vesak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesak "Vesak"). The full moon around the [southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox "March equinox") or [northern autumnal equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_equinox "September equinox") is often called the [harvest moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvest_moon "Harvest moon") and is celebrated with festivities such as the [Harvest Moon Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvest_Moon_Festival "Harvest Moon Festival") of the [Chinese lunar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_lunar_calendar "Chinese lunar calendar"), its second most important celebration after [the Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_New_Year "Chinese New Year") lunisolar [Lunar New Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_New_Year "Lunar New Year").

### Cultural representation

Humans have depicted the Moon since [prehistoric times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_times "Prehistoric times"), as evidenced by visual depictions from 40,000 [BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_present "Before present") and written depictions from the [4th millennium BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4th_millennium_BCE "4th millennium BCE"). The oldest named astronomer and poet [Enheduanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna "Enheduanna"), [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire "Akkadian Empire") high priestess to the lunar deity [Nanna/Sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin_(mythology) "Sin (mythology)") and daughter of [Sargon the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_the_Great "Sargon the Great") (c. 2334 – c. 2279 BCE), tracked the Moon and wrote poems about her.

#### Crescent

For the representation of the Moon, especially its [lunar phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase "Lunar phase"), the [crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent "Crescent") (🌙) has been a recurring symbol in a range of cultures since at least 3,000 BCE or possibly earlier with bull horns dating to the earliest [cave paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_painting "Cave painting") at 40,000 [BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_present "Before present"). In [writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system "Writing system") such as Chinese the crescent has developed into the symbol [月](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%88 "wikt:月"), the word for Moon, and in ancient Egyptian it was the symbol [𓇹](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%93%87%B9 "wikt:𓇹"), meaning Moon and spelled like the ancient Egyptian lunar deity *[Iah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iah "Iah")*, which the other ancient Egyptian lunar deities [Khonsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khonsu "Khonsu") and [Thoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth "Thoth") were associated with.

Iconographically the crescent was used in [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") as the primary symbol of [Nanna/Sîn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin_(mythology) "Sin (mythology)"), the ancient [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumeria "Sumeria") lunar deity, who was the father of [Inanna/Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inanna "Inanna"), the goddess of the planet [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus "Venus") (symbolized as the [eight pointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octagram "Octagram") [Star of Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_of_Ishtar "Star of Ishtar")), and [Utu/Shamash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utu "Utu"), the god of the Sun ([symbolized as a disc, optionally with eight rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_symbol "Solar symbol")), all three often depicted next to each other. Nanna/Sîn is, like some other lunar deities, for example Iah and Khonsu of ancient Egypt, [Mene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mene_(goddess) "Mene (goddess)")/[Selene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selene "Selene") of ancient Greece and [Luna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_(mythology) "Luna (mythology)") of ancient Rome, depicted as a [horned deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horned_deity "Horned deity"), featuring crescent shaped headgears or crowns.

The particular arrangement of the crescent with a star known as the [star and crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_and_crescent "Star and crescent") (☪️) goes back to the Bronze Age, representing either the Sun and Moon, or the Moon and the planet Venus, in combination. Since then the [heraldric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry "Heraldry") use of the star and crescent proliferated, Byzantium's symbolism possibly influencing the development of the [Ottoman flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_flag "Ottoman flag"), specifically the combination of the Turkish crescent with a star, and becoming a popular [symbol for Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbols_of_Islam "Symbols of Islam") (as the *[hilal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hilal#Noun "wikt:hilal")* of the [Islamic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar "Islamic calendar")) and [for a range of nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_and_crescent#Contemporary_use "Star and crescent").

#### Other associations

The features of the Moon, the contrasting brighter highlands and darker maria, have been seen by different cultures [forming abstract shapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_pareidolia "Lunar pareidolia"). Such shapes are among others the [Man in the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_in_the_Moon "Man in the Moon") (e.g. [Coyolxāuhqui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyolx%C4%81uhqui "Coyolxāuhqui")) or the [Moon Rabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Rabbit "Moon Rabbit") (e.g. the Chinese [Tu'er Ye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu%27er_Ye "Tu'er Ye") or in [Indigenous American mythologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythologies_of_the_indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas "Mythologies of the indigenous peoples of the Americas") the aspect of the [Maya moon goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_moon_goddess "Maya moon goddess"), from which possibly [Awilix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awilix "Awilix") is derived, or of [Metztli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metztli "Metztli")/[Tēcciztēcatl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C4%93ccizt%C4%93catl "Tēcciztēcatl")).

Throughout the global Middle Ages, the Moon held an important cultural role in topics as wide-ranging as [love poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_poetry "Love poetry"), [prophecy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecy "Prophecy"), medical thinking. In western [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy "Alchemy"), the Moon was associated with [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver "Silver"), while gold was associated with the Sun.

### Representation in modern culture

The perception of the Moon in the [modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_era "Modern era") has been informed by [telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope "Telescope")-enabled [modern astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_astronomy "Modern astronomy"). These new insights inspired cultural references, connecting romantic reflections about the Moon and speculative fiction such as science-fiction dealing with the Moon.

Later [spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceflight "Spaceflight") ushered in the [space age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_age "Space age"), which produced especially with the first [Moon landings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_landing "Moon landing") historic achievements and references for [global society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_society "Global society"), represented through [images of Earth from a distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_image_of_Earth "First image of Earth") and summarized in an awe and awareness called the [overview effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overview_effect "Overview effect"), amounting to [Moon joy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_joy "Moon joy") by reaching the Moon.

This has been accompanied
by public and critical reflection on humanity's cultural and [legal relation to the celestial body](#Legal_status), particularly as an object of [imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism "Imperialism"), as in the [space race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_race "Space race"), and as an object of [colonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_colonization#Colonialism "Space colonization") through [lunar resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_resources "Lunar resources") [commercialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercialization_of_space "Commercialization of space"). The 1970 poem "[Whitey on the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitey_on_the_Moon "Whitey on the Moon")" has become famous for its expression of such critical reflections. Other reflections have invoked the Moon's nature, raising issues like lunar conservation and [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_sustainability "Space sustainability"), understanding the Moon as a [common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commons "Commons").

In 2021 the 20 July became the *International Moon Day*, the date of the [first crewed Moon landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11#Landing "Apollo 11").

### Lunar effect

The lunar effect is a supposed correlation between specific stages of the roughly 29.5-day lunar cycle, and behavior and physiological changes in humans on Earth. Lunar cycles have significant impacts on human culture, but no solid evidence connects these cycles to human biology.

The Moon has long been associated with insanity and irrationality; the words *lunacy* and *[lunatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunatic "Lunatic")* are derived from the Latin name for the Moon, *Luna*. Philosophers [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") and [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder "Pliny the Elder") argued that the full moon induced insanity in susceptible individuals, believing that the brain, which is mostly water, must be affected by the Moon and its power over the tides, but the Moon's gravity is too slight to affect any single person.
In modern society lunar effects have been claimed to be responsible for supposed increased admissions to psychiatric hospitals, traffic accidents, homicides or suicides during a full moon, but these claims have been invalidated by at least 37 studies.
