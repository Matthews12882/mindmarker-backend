# Ethiopia

**Ethiopia**, officially the **Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia** (**FDRE**), is a [landlocked country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landlocked_country "Landlocked country") located in the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa") region of [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa "East Africa"). It shares borders with [Eritrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrea "Eritrea") to the north, [Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti "Djibouti") to the northeast, [Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia "Somalia") to the southeast, [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya") to the south, [South Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sudan "South Sudan") to the west, and [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan "Sudan") to the northwest. Ethiopia covers a land area of 1,104,300 km2 (426,400 sq mi). As of 2026[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), it has around 138 million inhabitants, making it the [10th-most populous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population "List of countries and dependencies by population") country in the world and 2nd most populous country in Africa. The national capital and largest city, [Addis Ababa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_Ababa "Addis Ababa"), lies several kilometres west of the [East African Rift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_African_Rift "East African Rift") that splits the country into the [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Plate "African Plate") and [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_Plate "Somali Plate") tectonic plates.

[Anatomically modern humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_human "Early modern human") emerged in what is now Ethiopia. In 980 BC, the Kingdom of [D'mt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%CA%BFmt "Dʿmt") extended its realm over Eritrea and the northern region of Ethiopia, while the [Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum "Kingdom of Aksum") maintained a unified civilisation in the region for 900 years. Christianity arrived to the kingdom in 330 AD, while Islam gained influence during the [first Hijra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_to_Abyssinia "Migration to Abyssinia") in 615. After the collapse of Aksum in 960, the [Zagwe dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagwe_dynasty "Zagwe dynasty") ruled the north-central parts of Ethiopia. The [Shewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shewa "Shewa") usurper, [Yekuno Amlak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yekuno_Amlak "Yekuno Amlak"), overthrew the dynasty in 1270 at the [Battle of Ansata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ansata "Battle of Ansata"), inaugurating the [Ethiopian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Empire "Ethiopian Empire") and the Solomonic dynasty – asserting its lineage through Aksumite legitimacy by the biblical [Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon "Solomon") and [Queen of Sheba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Sheba "Queen of Sheba"), and [Menelik I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_I "Menelik I") was the first emperor. Under Emperor [Amda Seyon I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amda_Seyon_I "Amda Seyon I") (r. 1314–1344), the empire grew in prestige through territorial expansion against neighbouring Muslim territories. Later, the [Ethiopian–Adal War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian%E2%80%93Adal_War "Ethiopian–Adal War") (1529–1543) weakened the empire and opened the way for the [Oromo expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_expansions "Oromo expansions"). The growing influence of the Oromo at the Gondarine court ultimately contributed to the fragmentation known as [Zemene Mesafint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zemene_Mesafint "Zemene Mesafint"), beginning in 1769. [Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_Ethiopia "Emperor of Ethiopia") [Tewodros II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tewodros_II "Tewodros II") ended the *Zemene Mesafint* at the beginning of his reign in 1855, marking the reunification and modernisation of Ethiopia.

In the late 19th century, Ethiopia repelled foreign invasions from both [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") and [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy"), most notably defeating an Italian invasion at the [Battle of Adwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa "Battle of Adwa") in 1896. Under Emperor [Menelik II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II "Menelik II"), it also [expanded its territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II%27s_conquests "Menelik II's conquests"), remaining independent during the [Scramble for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa "Scramble for Africa") and establishing its current borders. Ethiopia lost its sovereignty in 1936, when [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy "Kingdom of Italy") under [Benito Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini "Benito Mussolini") invaded during the [Second Italo-Ethiopian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Italo-Ethiopian_War "Second Italo-Ethiopian War"), and following Ethiopia's defeat, [Italian East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_East_Africa "Italian East Africa") was established. In 1941, during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), it was [liberated by the British Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_African_campaign_(World_War_II) "East African campaign (World War II)"), and its full sovereignty was [restored in 1944](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Ethiopian_Agreement#Negotiating_a_new_agreement "Anglo-Ethiopian Agreement"). The [Derg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derg "Derg"), a Soviet-backed military junta, took power in 1974 after [deposing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Ethiopian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat "1974 Ethiopian coup d'état") Emperor [Haile Selassie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haile_Selassie "Haile Selassie") and the [Solomonic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonic_dynasty "Solomonic dynasty"), and ruled the country for nearly 17 years amidst the [Ethiopian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Civil_War "Ethiopian Civil War"). Following the [dissolution of the Derg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Derg "Fall of the Derg") in 1991, the [Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_People%27s_Revolutionary_Democratic_Front "Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front") (EPRDF) dominated the country with a [new constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_Constitution_of_Ethiopia "1995 Constitution of Ethiopia") and [ethnic-based federalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_federalism#Ethiopia "Ethnic federalism"). Since then, Ethiopia has suffered from prolonged and unsolved inter-ethnic clashes and political instability marked by [authoritarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarianism "Authoritarianism"). From 2018, regional and ethnically based factions have carried out armed attacks in [multiple ongoing wars throughout Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_civil_conflict_(2018%E2%80%93present) "Ethiopian civil conflict (2018–present)").

Ethiopia is a [multi-ethnic state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_state "Multinational state") with over 80 different [ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ethnic_groups_in_Ethiopia "List of ethnic groups in Ethiopia"). [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") is the most widely professed faith in the country, with the largest denomination being the [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church "Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church"), followed by [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Ethiopia "Islam in Ethiopia") and a small percentage of [traditional faiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religion "Traditional African religion"). The country is a founding member of the UN, and hosts the headquarters of the [African Union Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union_Commission "African Union Commission") and many other global [non-governmental organisations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-governmental_organisation "Non-governmental organisation") focused on Africa, also becoming a full member of [BRICS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRICS "BRICS") in 2024. Ethiopia is one of the [least developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_developed_countries "Least developed countries") but is sometimes considered an [emerging power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerging_power "Emerging power"), having the [fastest economic growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_African_countries_by_GDP_growth "List of African countries by GDP growth") in [sub-Saharan African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa") countries because of [foreign direct investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment "Foreign direct investment") in expansion of agricultural and manufacturing industries; [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Ethiopia "Agriculture in Ethiopia") is the country's largest economic sector, accounting for over 37% of the [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product "Gross domestic product") as of 2022. Although the Ethiopian economy has experienced consistent growth, in terms of [per capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita_income "Per capita income") and the [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_Human_Development_Index "List of countries by Human Development Index") the country remains among the poorest in Africa. Ethiopia faces numerous challenges, including high rates of [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_in_Ethiopia "Poverty in Ethiopia"), [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Ethiopia "Human rights in Ethiopia") violations, widespread ethnic discrimination, and a literacy rate of 60%.

## Names

Tradition holds that the name Ethiopia (ኢትዮጵያ) comes from the name of the first King of Ethiopia, Ethiop, or [Ethiopis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopis "Ethiopis").

Ayele Berkerie explains:

> According to an Ethiopian tradition, the term Ethiopia is derived from the word Ethiopis, a name of the Ethiopian king, the seventh in the ancestral lines. Metshafe Aksum or the Ethiopian Book of Aksum identifies Itiopis as the twelfth king of Ethiopia and the father of Aksumawi. The Ethiopians pronounce Ethiopia እትዮጵያ with a Sades or the sixth sound እ as in incorporate and the graph ጰ has no equivalent in English or Latin graphs. Ethiopis is believed to be the twelfth direct descendant of Adam. His father is identified as Kush, while his grandfather is known as Kam.

In the 15th-century Ge'ez *[Book of Axum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Axum "Book of Axum")*, the name is ascribed to a legendary individual called *Ityopp'is*. He was an extra-biblical son of [Cush, son of Ham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cush_(Bible) "Cush (Bible)"), said to have founded the city of [Axum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axum "Axum").

The [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") name Αἰθιοπία (from Αἰθίοψ, 'an Ethiopian') is a compound word, later explained as derived from the Greek words αἴθω and ὤψ (*eithō* "I burn" + *ōps* "face"). According to the [Liddell-Scott Jones Greek-English Lexicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Greek%E2%80%93English_Lexicon "A Greek–English Lexicon"), the designation properly translates as *burnt-face* in noun form and *red-brown* in adjectival form. The historian [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") used the appellation to denote those parts of Africa south of the [Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara") that were then known within the [Ecumene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumene "Ecumene") (habitable world). The earliest mention of the term is found in the works of [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer"), where it is used to refer to two people groups, one in Africa and one in the east from eastern Turkey to India. This Greek name was borrowed into [Amharic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amharic "Amharic") as ኢትዮጵያ, *ʾĪtyōṗṗyā*.

In [Greco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece")-[Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") epigraphs, *Aethiopia* was a specific toponym for ancient [Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia"). At least as early as c. 850, the name *Aethiopia* also occurs in many translations of the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") (Tanakh) in allusion to Nubia. The ancient [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language "Hebrew language") texts identify Nubia instead as [Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush"). However, in the [Christian Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible#Christian_Bible "Bible"), the Greek term Aithiops does occur, referring to a servant of the [Kandake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandake "Kandake"), the queen of Kush.

Following the Hellenic and biblical traditions, the [Monumentum Adulitanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monumentum_Adulitanum "Monumentum Adulitanum"), a 3rd-century inscription belonging to the Aksumite Empire, indicates that Aksum's ruler governed an area that was flanked to the west by the territory of Ethiopia and Sasu. The Aksumite King [Ezana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezana_of_Axum "Ezana of Axum") eventually conquered Nubia the following century, and the Aksumites thereafter appropriated the designation "Ethiopians" for their own kingdom. In the [Ge'ez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%CA%BDez "Geʽez") version of the Ezana inscription, Aἰθίοπες is equated with the unvocalised *Ḥbšt* and *Ḥbśt* (Ḥabashat), and denotes for the first time the highland inhabitants of Aksum. This new [demonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonym "Demonym") was subsequently rendered as *ḥbs* ('Aḥbāsh) in [Sabaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabaean_language "Sabaean language") and as *Ḥabasha* in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"). Derivatives of this are used in some languages that use loanwords from Arabic, for example in [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language "Malay language") *Habsyah*.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In English, and generally outside of Ethiopia, the country was historically known as [Abyssinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abyssinia "Abyssinia"). This toponym was derived from the Latinised form of the ancient *Habash*.

## History

### Prehistory

Several important finds have propelled Ethiopia and the surrounding region to the forefront of [palaeontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleontology "Paleontology"). The oldest [hominid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hominidae "Hominidae") discovered to date in Ethiopia is the 4.2 million-year-old *[Ardipithecus ramidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardipithecus "Ardipithecus")* ([Ardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardi "Ardi")) found by [Tim D. White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_D._White "Tim D. White") in 1994. The most well-known hominid discovery is *[Australopithecus afarensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australopithecus_afarensis "Australopithecus afarensis")* ([Lucy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_(hominid) "Lucy (hominid)")). Known locally as *Dinkinesh*, the specimen was found in the [Awash Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awash_Valley "Awash Valley") of [Afar Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_Region "Afar Region") in 1974 by [Donald Johanson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Johanson "Donald Johanson"), and is one of the most complete and best-preserved adult [Australopithecine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australopithecus "Australopithecus") fossils ever uncovered. Lucy's taxonomic name refers to the region where the discovery was made. This hominid is estimated to have lived 3.2 million years ago.

Ethiopia is also considered one of the earliest sites of the emergence of anatomically modern humans, *[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human "Human")*. The oldest of these local fossil finds, the [Omo remains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omo_remains "Omo remains"), were excavated in the southwestern [Omo Kibish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omo_Kibish_Formation "Omo Kibish Formation") area and have been dated to the [Middle Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Paleolithic "Middle Paleolithic"), around 200,000 years ago. Additionally, skeletons of *[Homo sapiens idaltu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herto_Man "Herto Man")* were found at a site in the [Middle Awash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Awash "Middle Awash") valley. Dated to approximately 160,000 years ago, they may represent an extinct subspecies of *Homo sapiens*, or the immediate ancestors of anatomically modern humans. Archaic *Homo sapiens* fossils excavated at the [Jebel Irhoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Irhoud "Jebel Irhoud") site in Morocco have since been dated to an earlier period, about 300,000 years ago, while Omo-Kibish I (Omo I) from southern Ethiopia is the oldest anatomically modern *Homo sapiens* skeleton currently known (196 ± 5 kya).

According to some anthropologists, the first [Afroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages "Afroasiatic languages")-speaking populations arrived in the region during the ensuing [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") era from the family's proposed [urheimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_homeland "Afroasiatic homeland") ("original homeland") in the [Nile Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile"), or the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East "Near East"). The majority of scholars today propose that the Afroasiatic family developed in northeast Africa because of the higher diversity of lineages in that region, a telltale sign of linguistic origin.

In 2019, archaeologists discovered a 30,000-year-old [Middle Stone Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Stone_Age "Middle Stone Age") rock shelter at the [Fincha Habera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fincha_Habera,_Ethiopia "Fincha Habera, Ethiopia") site in [Bale Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bale_Mountains "Bale Mountains") at an elevation of 3,469 m (11,381 ft) above sea level. At this high altitude, humans are susceptible both to [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxemia#Environmental_oxygen "Hypoxemia") and to extreme weather. According to a study published in the journal *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_(journal) "Science (journal)")*, this dwelling is proof of the earliest permanent human occupation at high altitude yet discovered. Thousands of animal bones, hundreds of stone tools, and ancient fireplaces were discovered, revealing a diet that featured giant [mole rats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spalacidae "Spalacidae").

Evidence of some of the earliest known stone-tipped projectile weapons (a characteristic tool of *Homo sapiens*), the stone tips of [javelins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javelin "Javelin") or throwing spears, were discovered in 2013 at the Ethiopian site of [Gademotta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gademotta "Gademotta"), which date to around 279,000 years ago. In 2019, additional Middle Stone Age projectile weapons were found at Aduma, dated 100,000–80,000 years ago, in the form of points considered likely to belong to darts delivered by spear throwers.

### Antiquity

In 980 BC, Dʿmt was established in present-day Eritrea and the northern part of Ethiopia in the [Tigray Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray_Region "Tigray Region"), and is widely believed to be the successor state to [Punt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_Punt "Land of Punt"). This polity's capital was located at [Yeha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeha "Yeha") in what is now northern Ethiopia. Most modern historians consider this civilisation to be a native Ethiopian one, although in earlier times many suggested it was [Sabaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabaeans "Sabaeans")-influenced because of the latter's hegemony of the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea").

Other scholars regard Dʿmt as the result of a union of Afroasiatic-speaking cultures of the Cushitic and Semitic branches; namely, local [Agaw peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agaw_people "Agaw people") and Sabaeans from Southern Arabia. However, Ge'ez, the ancient Semitic language of Ethiopia, is thought to have developed independently from the Sabaean language. As early as 2000 BC, other Semitic speakers were living in Ethiopia and Eritrea where Ge'ez developed. Sabaean influence is now thought to have been minor, limited to a few localities, and disappearing after a few decades or a century. It may have been a trading or military colony in alliance with the Ethiopian civilisation of Dʿmt or some other proto-[Axumite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axumite "Axumite") state.

After the fall of Dʿmt during the 4th century BC, the Ethiopian plateau came to be dominated by smaller successor kingdoms. In the 1st century AD, the Kingdom of Aksum emerged in what is now Tigray Region and Eritrea. According to the medieval *Book of Axum*, the kingdom's first capital, Mazaber, was built by Itiyopis, son of Cush. Aksum would later at times extend its rule into Yemen on the other side of the Red Sea. The Persian prophet [Mani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mani_(prophet) "Mani (prophet)") listed Axum with Rome, Persia, and China as one of the four great powers of his era, during the 3rd century. It is also believed that there was a connection between Egyptian and Ethiopian churches. There is faint evidence that the Aksumites were associated with the Queen of Sheba, via their royal inscription.

Around 316 AD, [Frumentius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frumentius "Frumentius") and his brother Edesius from [Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyre,_Lebanon "Tyre, Lebanon") accompanied their uncle on a voyage to Ethiopia. When the vessel stopped at a Red Sea port, the natives killed all the travellers except the two brothers, who were taken to the court as [slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Ethiopia "Slavery in Ethiopia"). They were given positions of trust by the monarch, and they converted members of the royal court to Christianity. Frumentius became the first bishop of Aksum. A coin dated to 324 shows that Ethiopia was the second country to officially adopt Christianity (after Armenia did so in 301), although the religion may have been at first confined to court circles; it was the first major power to do so. The Aksumites were accustomed to the Greco-Roman sphere of influence, but they embarked on significant cultural ties and trade connections between the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent") and the Roman Empire via the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road"), primarily exporting [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_trade "Ivory trade"), tortoise shell, gold and emeralds, and importing silk and spices. The country's gold production in 2015 is 9 tonnes.

### Middle Ages

The kingdom adopted the name "Ethiopia" during the reign of Ezana in the 4th century. After the conquest of Kingdom of Kush in 330, the Aksumite territory reached its peak between the 5th and 6th centuries. This period was interrupted by several incursions into the South Arabian protectorate, including [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") [Dhu Nuwas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhu_Nuwas "Dhu Nuwas") of the [Himyarite Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom "Himyarite Kingdom") and the [Aksumite–Persian wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite%E2%80%93Persian_wars "Aksumite–Persian wars"). In 575, the Aksumites [besieged and retook Sana'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_reconquest_of_Yemen "Sasanian reconquest of Yemen") following the assassination of its governor [Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_ibn_Dhi_Yazan "Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan"). The Red Sea was left to the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate "Rashidun Caliphate") in 646, and the port city of [Adulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adulis "Adulis") was plundered by Arab Muslims in the 8th century; along with irrevocable [land degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_degradation "Land degradation"), claimed [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_(general_concept) "Climate change (general concept)") and sporadic rainfall precipitation from 730 to 760, these factors likely caused the kingdom to decline in power as part of an important trade route. Aksum came to an end in 960 when [Queen Gudit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudit "Gudit") defeated the last king of Aksum. In response, the remnant of the Aksumite population shifted into the southern region and establish the Zagwe dynasty, changing its capital to [Lalibela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalibela "Lalibela"). Zagwe's rule ended when [Amhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amhara_people "Amhara people") nobleman Yekuno Amlak revolted against King [Yetbarak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yetbarak "Yetbarak") and established the Ethiopian Empire (known by the [exonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exonym "Exonym") "Abyssinia").

The Ethiopian Empire initiated territorial expansion under the leadership of Amda Seyon I. He launched campaigns against his Muslim adversaries to the east, resulting in a significant shift in the balance of power in favour of the Christians for the next two centuries. After Amda Seyon's successful eastern campaigns, most of the Muslim principalities in the Horn of Africa came under the suzerainty of the Ethiopian Empire. Stretching from [Gojjam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gojjam "Gojjam") to the Somali Coast in [Zeila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeila "Zeila"). Among these Muslim entities was the [Sultanate of Ifat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Ifat "Sultanate of Ifat"). During the reign of Emperor [Zara Yaqob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zara_Yaqob "Zara Yaqob"), the Ethiopian Empire reached its pinnacle. His rule was marked by the consolidation of territorial acquisitions from earlier rulers, the oversight of the construction of numerous churches and monasteries, the active promotion of literature and art, and the strengthening of central imperial authority. Ifat's successor, the [Adal Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adal_Sultanate "Adal Sultanate"), tried to conquer Ethiopia during the Ethiopian–Adal War, but was ultimately defeated at the 1543 [Battle of Wayna Daga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wayna_Daga "Battle of Wayna Daga").

By the 16th century, an influx of migration by ethnic [Oromo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_people "Oromo people") into northern parts of the region fragmented the empire's power. Embarking from present-day [Guji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guji_Zone "Guji Zone") and [Borena Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borena_Zone "Borena Zone"), the Oromos were largely motivated by several folkloric conceptions—beginning with *Moggaasaa* and *Liqimssa*—many of whom related to their raids. This persisted until *gada* of Meslé. According to [Abba Bahrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrey "Bahrey"), the earliest expansion occurred under Emperor [Dawit II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawit_II "Dawit II") ([*luba* Melbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luba_Melbah "Luba Melbah")), when they encroached to [Bale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bale_Province,_Ethiopia "Bale Province, Ethiopia") before invading Adal Sultanate.

During the 16th and 17th centuries, Ethiopia saw major diplomatic contact with [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal "Portugal"), which was primarily religious in nature. Beginning in 1555, Portuguese Jesuits attempted to develop Roman Catholicism as the state religion. After several failures, they sent several missionaries in 1603, including the most influential, Spanish Jesuit [Pedro Paez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_P%C3%A1ez "Pedro Páez"). Under Emperor [Susenyos I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susenyos_I "Susenyos I"), Roman Catholicism became the state religion of the Ethiopian Empire in 1622. This decision caused an uprising by the Orthodox populace.

### Early Modern Period (1632–1855)

In 1632, Emperor [Fasilides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasilides "Fasilides") halted Roman Catholic state administration, restoring [Orthodox Tewahedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Tewahedo "Orthodox Tewahedo") as the state religion. Fasilides' reign solidified imperial power, relocating the capital to [Gondar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondar "Gondar") in 1636, marking the beginning of the "[Gondarine period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondarine_period "Gondarine period")". He reclaimed lands, and exiled [Jesuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuits "Jesuits") to [Fremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fremona "Fremona"). During his rule, Fasilides constructed the iconic royal fortress, [Fasil Ghebbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasil_Ghebbi "Fasil Ghebbi"), built forty-four churches, and revived [Ethiopian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_art "Ethiopian art"). He is also credited with building seven stone bridges over the [Blue Nile River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Nile_River "Blue Nile River").

Gondar's power declined after the death of [Iyasu I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iyasu_I "Iyasu I") in 1706. Following [Iyasu II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iyasu_II "Iyasu II")'s death in 1755, Empress [Mentewab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentewab "Mentewab") brought her brother, [Ras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ras_(title) "Ras (title)") Wolde Leul, to Gondar, making him Ras Bitwaded. This led to regnal conflict between Mentewab's *Quaregnoch* and the Wollo group led by Wubit. In 1767, Ras [Mikael Sehul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikael_Sehul "Mikael Sehul"), a regent in [Tigray Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray_Province "Tigray Province"), seized Gondar, killing the child [Iyoas I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iyoas_I "Iyoas I") in 1769, the reigning emperor, and installed 70-year-old [Yohannes II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yohannes_II "Yohannes II").

Between 1769 and 1855, Ethiopia witnessed the Zemene Mesafint or "Age of Princes," a period of isolation. Emperors became figureheads, controlled by regional lords and noblemen like *Ras* Mikael Sehul, Ras [Wolde Selassie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolde_Selassie "Wolde Selassie") of Tigray, and by the [Yejju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yejju "Yejju") Oromo dynasty of the [Wara Sheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zemene_Mesafint#Wara_Sheh_rulers_(Yejju)_(1784–1855) "Zemene Mesafint"), including Ras [Gugsa of Yejju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gugsa_of_Yejju "Gugsa of Yejju"). Before the Zemene Mesafint, Emperor Iyoas I had introduced the Oromo language (*Afaan Oromo*) at court, replacing Amharic.

### Age of Imperialism (1855–1916)

Ethiopian isolationism ended following a British mission that concluded with an alliance between the two nations, but it was not until 1855 that the Amhara kingdoms of northern Ethiopia (Gondar, Gojjam, and Shewa) were briefly united after the power of the emperor was restored beginning with the reign of Tewodros II. Tewodros II began a process of consolidation, centralisation, and state-building that would be continued by succeeding emperors. This process reduced the power of regional rulers, restructured the empire's administration, and created a professional army. These changes created the basis for establishing the effective sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Ethiopian state. In 1875 and 1876, Ottoman and Egyptian forces, accompanied by many European and American advisors, twice [invaded Abyssinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian%E2%80%93Ethiopian_War "Egyptian–Ethiopian War") but were initially defeated. From 1885 to 1889 (under [Yohannes IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yohannes_IV "Yohannes IV")), Ethiopia joined the [Mahdist War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_War "Mahdist War") allied to [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland "United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland"), the Ottomans, and Egypt against the Sudanese Mahdist State. In 1887, Menelik II, king of Shewa, invaded the [Emirate of Harar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Harar "Emirate of Harar") after his victory at the [Battle of Chelenqo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chelenqo "Battle of Chelenqo"). On 10 March 1889, Yohannes IV was killed by the Sudanese Khalifah Abdullah's army whilst leading his army in the [Battle of Gallabat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gallabat "Battle of Gallabat").

Ethiopia, in roughly its current form, began under the reign of Menelik II, who was Emperor from 1889 until his death in 1913. From his base in the central province of Shewa, Menelik set out to annex territories to the south, east, and west—areas inhabited by the Oromo, [Sidama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidama_people "Sidama people"), Gurage, [Welayta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welayta_people "Welayta people"), and other peoples. He achieved this with the help of *Ras* [Gobana Dacche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gobana_Dacche "Gobana Dacche")'s Shewan Oromo militia, which occupied lands that had not been held since [Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_ibn_Ibrahim_al-Ghazi "Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi")'s war, as well as other areas that had never been under Ethiopian rule.

For his leadership, despite opposition from more traditional elements of society, Menelik II was heralded as a national hero. He had signed the [Treaty of Wuchale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Wuchale "Treaty of Wuchale") with Italy in May 1889, by which Italy would recognise Ethiopia's sovereignty so long as Italy could control an area north of Ethiopia (now part of modern Eritrea). In return, Italy was to provide Menelik with weapons and support him as emperor. The Italians used the time between the signing of the treaty and its ratification by the Italian government to expand their territorial claims. This [First Italo-Ethiopian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Italo-Ethiopian_War "First Italo-Ethiopian War") culminated in the Battle of Adwa on 1 March 1896, in which Italy's colonial forces were defeated by the Ethiopians. During this time, about a third of the population died in the [Great Ethiopian Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famines_in_Ethiopia "Famines in Ethiopia") (1888 to 1892), and the [rinderpest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1890s_African_rinderpest_epizootic "1890s African rinderpest epizootic") swept through the area, destroying much of the herd economy.

### Haile Selassie I era (1916–1974) and Italian colonial rule (1936-1941)

The early 20th century was marked by the reign of Emperor Haile Selassie (*Ras Tafari*). He came to power after [Lij Iyasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lij_Iyasu "Lij Iyasu") was deposed, and undertook [a nationwide modernisation campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernisation_under_Haile_Selassie "Modernisation under Haile Selassie") from 1916 when he was made a *Ras* and Regent (*[Inderase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inderase "Inderase")*) for the [Empress Regnant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_regnant "Queen regnant") [Zewditu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zewditu "Zewditu"), and became the *de facto* ruler of the Ethiopian Empire. Following Zewditu's death on 2 November 1930, he succeeded her as emperor. In 1931, Haile Selassie endowed Ethiopia with [its first constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1931_Constitution_of_Ethiopia "1931 Constitution of Ethiopia") in emulation of Imperial Japan's [1890 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Constitution "Meiji Constitution").

The independence of Ethiopia was interrupted by the Second Italo-Ethiopian War, beginning when it was invaded by [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy#Fascist_regime_(1922–1943) "Kingdom of Italy") in early October 1935, and by subsequent Italian rule of the country (1936–1941) after Italian victory in the war. Italy, however, never managed to secure the country in its totality, due to resistance from the [Arbegnoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbegnoch "Arbegnoch"); this made Ethiopia, along with [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia "Liberia"), the only African country to never be fully colonised. Following the entry of Italy into World War II, [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire") forces, together with the Arbegnoch, liberated Ethiopia in the course of the East African campaign in 1941. The country was placed under [British military administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupied_Enemy_Territory_Administration_(Ethiopia) "Occupied Enemy Territory Administration (Ethiopia)"), and then Ethiopia's full [sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereignty "Sovereignty") was restored with the signing of the [Anglo-Ethiopian Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Ethiopian_Agreement "Anglo-Ethiopian Agreement") in December 1944.

On 24 October 1945, Ethiopia became a founding member of the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations"). In 1952, Haile Selassie orchestrated a [federation with Eritrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_Ethiopia_and_Eritrea "Federation of Ethiopia and Eritrea"). He dissolved it in 1962 and annexed Eritrea, resulting in the [Eritrean War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean_War_of_Independence "Eritrean War of Independence"). Haile Selassie also played a leading role in the formation of the [Organisation of African Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity "Organisation of African Unity") (OAU). Opinion within Ethiopia turned against Haile Selassie, owing to the worldwide [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis "1973 oil crisis") causing a sharp increase in gasoline prices starting on 13 February 1974, leading to student and worker protests. The feudal oligarchical cabinet of [Aklilu Habte-Wold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aklilu_Habte-Wold "Aklilu Habte-Wold") was toppled, and a new government was formed with [Endelkachew Makonnen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endelkachew_Makonnen "Endelkachew Makonnen") serving as Prime Minister.

### Derg era (1974–1991)

Haile Selassie's rule ended on 12 September 1974, when he was deposed by the Derg, a committee made up of military and police officers. After the execution of [60 former government and military officials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_the_Sixty "Massacre of the Sixty"), the new [Provisional Military Administrative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Military_Administrative_Council "Provisional Military Administrative Council") abolished the monarchy in March 1975 and established Ethiopia as a [Marxist-Leninist state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist-Leninist_state "Marxist-Leninist state"). The abolition of [feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism "Feudalism"), increased [literacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy "Literacy"), [nationalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalisation "Nationalisation"), and sweeping [land reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform "Land reform") including the [resettlement and villagisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resettlement_and_villagisation_in_Ethiopia "Resettlement and villagisation in Ethiopia") from the [Ethiopian Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Highlands "Ethiopian Highlands") became priorities.

After a power struggle in 1977, [Mengistu Haile Mariam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mengistu_Haile_Mariam "Mengistu Haile Mariam") gained undisputed leadership of the Derg. In 1977, Somalia, which had previously been receiving assistance and arms from the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR "USSR"), invaded Ethiopia in the [Ogaden War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogaden_War "Ogaden War"), capturing part of the [Ogaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogaden "Ogaden") region. Ethiopia recovered it after it began receiving massive military aid from the Soviet bloc countries. By the end of the seventies, Mengistu presided over the second-largest army in all of sub-Saharan Africa, as well as a formidable [air force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Air_Force "Ethiopian Air Force") and navy.

In 1976–78, up to 500,000 were killed as a result of the [Red Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Terror_(Ethiopia) "Red Terror (Ethiopia)"), a violent [political repression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_repression "Political repression") campaign by the Derg against various opposition groups. In 1987, the Derg dissolved itself and established the [People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Democratic_Republic_of_Ethiopia "People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia") (PDRE) upon the adoption of the [1987 Constitution of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1987_Constitution_of_Ethiopia "1987 Constitution of Ethiopia"). A [1983–85 famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983%E2%80%931985_famine_in_Ethiopia "1983–1985 famine in Ethiopia") affected around 8 million people, resulting in 1 million dead. Insurrections against authoritarian rule sprang up, particularly in the northern regions of Eritrea and Tigray. The [Tigray People's Liberation Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray_People%27s_Liberation_Front "Tigray People's Liberation Front") (TPLF) merged with other ethnically based opposition movements in 1989, to form the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF).

The collapse of [Marxism–Leninism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism%E2%80%93Leninism "Marxism–Leninism") during the [revolutions of 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989 "Revolutions of 1989") coincided with the Soviet Union stopping aid to Ethiopia altogether in 1990. EPRDF forces advanced on Addis Ababa in May 1991, and Mengistu fled the country and was granted asylum in Zimbabwe.

### Federal Democratic Republic (1991–present)

In July 1991, the EPRDF convened a National Conference to establish the [Transitional Government of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitional_Government_of_Ethiopia "Transitional Government of Ethiopia") composed of an 87-member [Council of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Representatives_of_Ethiopia "Council of Representatives of Ethiopia") and guided by a national charter that functioned as a transitional constitution. In 1994, a new constitution was written that established a parliamentary republic with a bicameral legislature and a judicial system.

In April 1993, Eritrea gained independence from Ethiopia after a [national referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_Eritrean_independence_referendum "1993 Eritrean independence referendum"). In May 1998, a border dispute with Eritrea led to the [Eritrean–Ethiopian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean%E2%80%93Ethiopian_War "Eritrean–Ethiopian War"), which lasted until June 2000 and cost both countries an estimated $1 million[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] a day. This had a negative effect on Ethiopia's economy, and a [border conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean%E2%80%93Ethiopian_border_conflict "Eritrean–Ethiopian border conflict") between the two countries would continue [until 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Eritrea%E2%80%93Ethiopia_summit "2018 Eritrea–Ethiopia summit").

[Ethnic violence rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Ethiopia#Ethnic_violence "Human rights in Ethiopia") during the late 2010s and early 2020s, with various clashes and conflicts leading to millions of Ethiopians being displaced.

The federal government decided that elections for 2020 (later being [rescheduled to 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Ethiopian_general_election "2021 Ethiopian general election")) be cancelled, due to health and safety concerns about [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Ethiopia "COVID-19 pandemic in Ethiopia"). The Tigray Region's TPLF opposed this, and [proceeded to hold elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Tigray_regional_election "2020 Tigray regional election") anyway on 9 September 2020. Relations between the federal government and Tigray deteriorated rapidly, and in November 2020, Ethiopia began a military offensive in Tigray in response to [attacks on army units stationed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Command_attacks_(Ethiopia) "Northern Command attacks (Ethiopia)") there, marking the beginning of the [Tigray war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray_war "Tigray war"). By March 2022, as many as 500,000 people had died as a result of [violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_crimes_in_the_Tigray_war "War crimes in the Tigray war") and [famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_in_northern_Ethiopia_(2020%E2%80%93present) "Famine in northern Ethiopia (2020–present)"). After a number of [peace and mediation proposals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigrayan_peace_process "Tigrayan peace process") in the intervening years, Ethiopia and the Tigrayan rebel forces agreed to a [cessation of hostilities on 2 November 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93Tigray_peace_agreement "Ethiopia–Tigray peace agreement"). Coupled with [OLA insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OLA_insurgency "OLA insurgency"), the federal government relations with Fano militias, who previously allied to the government in the Tigray War, deteriorated in mid-2023, resulting in a [war in the Amhara Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Amhara "War in Amhara"). According to reports conducted by the [Ethiopian Human Rights Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Human_Rights_Commission "Ethiopian Human Rights Commission") (EHRC), mass human rights violations carried out by ENDF troops including door-to-door searches, extrajudicial killings, massacres and detentions. Notable incident includes the [Merawi massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merawi_massacre "Merawi massacre") in early 2024, which left 50 to 100 residents dead in [Merawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merawi,_Ethiopia "Merawi, Ethiopia") town in Amhara.

## Geography

At 1,104,300 square kilometres (426,372.61 sq mi), Ethiopia is the world's 26th-largest country, comparable in size to [Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivia "Bolivia"). It lies between the [3rd parallel north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_parallel_north "3rd parallel north") and the [15th parallel north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/15th_parallel_north "15th parallel north") and longitudes [33rd meridian east](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/33rd_meridian_east "33rd meridian east") and [48th meridian east](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/48th_meridian_east "48th meridian east").

The major portion of Ethiopia lies in the Horn of Africa, which is the easternmost part of the African landmass. The territories that have frontiers with Ethiopia are Eritrea to the north and then, moving in a clockwise direction, Djibouti, Somalia, Kenya, South Sudan and Sudan. Within Ethiopia is a vast highland complex of mountains and dissected plateaus divided by the [Great Rift Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Rift_Valley,_Ethiopia "Great Rift Valley, Ethiopia"), which runs generally southwest to northeast and is surrounded by lowlands, [steppes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steppe "Steppe"), or semi-desert. There is a great diversity of terrain with wide variations in climate, soils, natural vegetation and settlement patterns.

Ethiopia is an ecologically diverse country, ranging from the deserts along the eastern border to the tropical forests in the south to extensive [Afromontane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afromontane "Afromontane") in the northern and southwestern parts. [Lake Tana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Tana "Lake Tana") in the north is the source of the [Blue Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Nile "Blue Nile"). It also has many [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemism "Endemism") species, notably the [gelada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelada "Gelada"), the [walia ibex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walia_ibex "Walia ibex") and the [Ethiopian wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_wolf "Ethiopian wolf") ("Simien fox"). The wide range of altitude has given the country a variety of ecologically distinct areas, and this has helped to encourage the evolution of endemic species in ecological isolation.

The nation is a land of geographical contrasts, ranging from the vast fertile west, with its forests and numerous rivers, to the world's hottest settlement of [Dallol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol,_Ethiopia "Dallol, Ethiopia") in its north. The Ethiopian Highlands are the largest continuous mountain ranges in Africa, and the [Sof Omar Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sof_Omar_Caves "Sof Omar Caves") contains the largest cave on the continent. Ethiopia also has the second-largest number of [UNESCO World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Africa "List of World Heritage Sites in Africa") in Africa.

### Climate

The predominant climate type is tropical monsoon, with wide topographic-induced variation. The Ethiopian Highlands cover most of the country and have a climate which is generally considerably cooler than other regions at similar proximity to the Equator. Most of the country's major cities are located at elevations of around 2,000–2,500 m (6,562–8,202 ft) above sea level, including historic capitals such as Gondar and Axum. The modern capital, Addis Ababa, is situated on the foothills of [Mount Entoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Entoto "Mount Entoto") at an elevation of around 2,400 m (7,900 ft). It experiences a mild climate year round. With temperatures fairly uniform year round, the seasons in Addis Ababa are largely defined by rainfall: a dry season from October to February, a light rainy season from March to May, and a heavy rainy season from June to September. The average annual rainfall is approximately 1,200 mm (47 in).

There are on average seven hours of sunshine per day. The dry season is the sunniest time of the year, though even at the height of the rainy season in July and August there are still usually several hours per day of bright sunshine. The average annual temperature in Addis Ababa is 16 °C (60.8 °F), with daily maximum temperatures averaging 20–25 °C (68.0–77.0 °F) throughout the year, and overnight lows averaging 5–10 °C (41.0–50.0 °F).

Most major cities and tourist sites in Ethiopia lie at a similar elevation to Addis Ababa and have a comparable climate. In less elevated regions, particularly the lower lying [Ethiopian xeric grasslands and shrublands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_xeric_grasslands_and_shrublands "Ethiopian xeric grasslands and shrublands") in the east of Ethiopia, the climate can be significantly hotter and drier. Dallol, in the [Danakil Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danakil_Depression "Danakil Depression") in this eastern zone, has the world's highest average annual temperature of 34 °C (93.2 °F).

Ethiopia is vulnerable to many of the [effects of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change "Effects of climate change"). These include increases in temperature and changes in precipitation. [Climate change in Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Ethiopia "Climate change in Ethiopia") threatens food security and the economy, which is agriculture based. Many Ethiopians have been forced to leave their homes and travel as far as the Gulf, Southern Africa and [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe").

Since April 2019, the Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed has promoted [Beautifying Sheger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beautifying_Sheger "Beautifying Sheger"), a development project that aims to reduce the negative effects of climate change – among other things – in the capital city Addis Ababa. In the following May, the government held "Dine for Sheger", a fundraising event in order to cover some of the $1 billion[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] needed through the public. US$25 million was raised through the expensive event, both through the cost of attending and donations. Two Chinese railway companies under the [Belt and Road Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_and_Road_Initiative "Belt and Road Initiative") between China and Ethiopia had supplied funds to develop 12 of the total 56 kilometres.

### Biodiversity

Ethiopia is a global centre of avian diversity. More than 856 bird species have been recorded in Ethiopia, twenty of which are endemic to the country. Sixteen species are endangered or critically endangered. Many of these birds feed on butterflies, like the *[Bicyclus anynana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicyclus_anynana "Bicyclus anynana")*.[*[full citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#What_information_to_include "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*]

Historically, throughout the African continent, wildlife populations have rapidly declined due to logging, civil wars, pollution, poaching, and other human factors. A 17-year-long civil war, along with severe drought, negatively affected Ethiopia's environmental conditions, leading to even greater habitat degradation. Habitat destruction is a factor that leads to endangerment. When changes to a habitat occur rapidly, animals do not have time to adjust. Human impact threatens many species, with greater threats expected as a result of climate change induced by [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas"). With carbon dioxide emissions in 2010 of 6,494,000 tonnes, Ethiopia contributes just 0.02% to the annual human-caused release of greenhouse gases.

Ethiopia has 31 [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic "Endemic") species of mammals. Ethiopia has many species listed as critically endangered and vulnerable to global extinction. The threatened species in Ethiopia can be broken down into three categories (based on [IUCN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN "IUCN") ratings): [critically endangered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critically_endangered "Critically endangered"), [endangered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_species "Endangered species"), and [vulnerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerable_species "Vulnerable species").

Ethiopia is one of the eight fundamental and independent [centres of origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centres_of_origin "Centres of origin") for cultivated plants in the world. However, [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation "Deforestation") is a major concern for Ethiopia as studies suggest loss of forest contributes to soil erosion, loss of nutrients in the soil, loss of animal habitats, and reduction in biodiversity. At the beginning of the 20th century, around 420,000 km2 (or 35%) of Ethiopia's land was covered by trees, but recent research indicates that forest cover is now approximately 11.9% of the area. The country had a 2018 [Forest Landscape Integrity Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Landscape_Integrity_Index "Forest Landscape Integrity Index") mean score of 7.16/10, ranking it 50th globally out of 172 countries.

Ethiopia loses an estimated 1,410 km2 of natural forests each year due to firewood collection, conversion to farmland, overgrazing, and use of forest wood for building material. Between 1990 and 2005 the country lost approximately 21,000 km2 of forests. Current government programs to control deforestation consist of education, promoting reforestation programs, and providing raw materials which are alternatives to timber. In rural areas the government also provides non-timber fuel sources and access to non-forested land to promote agriculture without destroying forest habitat.

Organisations such as SOS and Farm Africa are working with the federal government and local governments to create a system of forest management.

## Government and politics

### Government

Ethiopia is a [federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism "Federalism") [parliamentary republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_republic "Parliamentary republic"), wherein the [Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Ethiopia "Prime Minister of Ethiopia") is the [head of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_government "Head of government"), and the [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Ethiopia "President of Ethiopia") is the [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state "Head of state") but with largely ceremonial powers. [Executive power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_power "Executive power") is exercised by the government and federal [legislative power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_power "Legislative power") vested in both the government and the two chambers of parliament. The [House of Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Federation "House of Federation") is the upper chamber of the bicameral legislature with 108 seats, and the lower chamber is the [House of Peoples' Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Peoples%27_Representatives "House of Peoples' Representatives") (HoPR) with 547 seats. The House of Federation is chosen by the regional councils whereas MPs of the HoPR are elected directly, in turn, they elect the president for a six-year term and the prime minister for a 5-year term.

The Ethiopian [judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary_of_Ethiopia "Judiciary of Ethiopia") consists of a dual system with two court structures: the federal and state courts. The FDRE Constitution vested federal judicial authority to the [Federal Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Supreme_Court_of_Ethiopia "Federal Supreme Court of Ethiopia") which can overturn and review decisions of subordinate federal courts; itself has regular division assigned for fundamental [errors of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_(law) "Error (law)"). In addition, the Supreme Court can perform [circuit hearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_court "Circuit court") in established five states at any states of federal levels or "area designated for its jurisdiction" if deemed "necessary for the efficient rendering of justice".

The Federal Supreme Proclamation granted three subject matter principles: laws, parties and place to federal court jurisdiction, first "cases arising under the Constitution, [federal laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_law "Federal law") and [international treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_treaties "International treaties")", second over "parties specified by federal laws".

On the basis of Article 78 of the 1994 Ethiopian Constitution, the judiciary is completely independent of the executive and the legislature. To ensure this, the President and Vice President of the Supreme Court are appointed by Parliament on the nomination of Prime Minister. Once elected, the executive power has no authority to remove them from office. Other judges are nominated by the [Federal Judicial Administration Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Judicial_Administration_Council "Federal Judicial Administration Council") (FJAC) on the basis of transparent criteria and the Prime Minister's recommendation for appointment in the HoPR. In all cases, judges cannot be removed from their duty unless they retired, violated disciplinary rules, gross incompatibility, or inefficiency to unfit due to ill health. Contrary, the majority vote of HoPR have the right to sanction removal in federal judiciary level or state council in cases of state judges. In 2015, the realities of this provision were questioned in a report prepared by [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House "Freedom House").

### Politics

Post-1995, Ethiopia's politics has been liberalised which promotes all-encompassing reforms to the country. Today, its economy is based on [mixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_economy "Mixed economy"), [market-oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy "Market economy") principles. Ethiopia has twelve semi-autonomous administrative regions that have the power to raise and spend their own revenues.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The [first multiparty election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_Ethiopian_general_election "1995 Ethiopian general election") took place in May 1995, which was won by the EPRDF. The president of the transitional government, EPRDF leader [Meles Zenawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meles_Zenawi "Meles Zenawi"), became the first Prime Minister of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, and [Negasso Gidada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negasso_Gidada "Negasso Gidada") was elected its president. Meles' government was consistently re-elected; however, these results were heavily criticised by international observers, and denounced by the opposition as fraudulent.

Meles died on 20 August 2012 in Brussels, where he was being treated for an unspecified illness. Deputy PM [Hailemariam Desalegn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hailemariam_Desalegn "Hailemariam Desalegn") was appointed as a new Prime Minister until the [2015 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_general_election,_2015 "Ethiopian general election, 2015"), and remained so afterwards with his party in control of every parliamentary seat. On 15 February 2018, Hailemariam [resigned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resignation_of_Hailemariam_Desalegn "Resignation of Hailemariam Desalegn") as PM, following [years of protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014%E2%80%932016_Oromo_protests "2014–2016 Oromo protests") and a [state of emergency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016%E2%80%932018_Ethiopian_state_of_emergency "2016–2018 Ethiopian state of emergency"). [Abiy Ahmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiy_Ahmed "Abiy Ahmed") became PM following Hailemariam's resignation. He made a historic visit to Eritrea in 2018, ending the state of conflict between the two countries, and was awarded the [Nobel Peace Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize "Nobel Peace Prize") in 2019.

According to the [Democracy Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_Index "Democracy Index") published by the United Kingdom-based [Economist Intelligence Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist_Intelligence_Unit "Economist Intelligence Unit") in late 2010, Ethiopia was an "authoritarian regime", ranking as the 118th-most democratic out of 167 countries. Ethiopia had dropped 13 places on the list since 2008, and the 2010 report attributed the drop to the government's crackdown on opposition activities, media, and civil society before the [2010 parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_general_election,_2010 "Ethiopian general election, 2010"), which the report argued had made Ethiopia a [*de facto* one-party state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant-party_system "Dominant-party system").

Accompanied by pervasive internal and intercommunal conflicts in the 21st century, the Ethiopian government resorted to an authoritarian structure, severing democratic and human rights. Freedom House, who has worked on Ethiopia since 2008, indicates that Ethiopia is "Not Free" state due to very poor [fundamental rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_rights "Fundamental rights") (political and civil liberties) recorded in both [EPRDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_People%27s_Revolutionary_Democratic_Front "Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front") and [Prosperity Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosperity_Party "Prosperity Party") regimes. Under Abiy Ahmed, Ethiopia is experiencing [democratic backsliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_Ethiopia "Democratic backsliding in Ethiopia") since 2019 marked by turbulent period of internal conflict, jailing opposition group members and limiting media freedom.

### Administrative divisions

Ethiopia has four levels of [administratively divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_division "Administrative division"): [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_Ethiopia "Regions of Ethiopia"), [zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_zones_of_Ethiopia "List of zones of Ethiopia"), [districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Districts_of_Ethiopia "Districts of Ethiopia") (*woredas*) and [wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wards_of_Ethiopia "Wards of Ethiopia") (*kebele*). The country comprises 12 regions and two city administrations under these regions. The two federal-level city administrations are Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa.

### Foreign relations

Ethiopia was historically a [trading nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_nation "Trading nation") that exported goods such as [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold "Gold"), [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory "Ivory"), exotic animals, and [incense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incense "Incense"). Modern Ethiopian foreign relations began under Emperor Tewodros II, who during his reign sought to re-establish a cohesive Ethiopian state, but was thwarted by the [British expedition of 1868](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1868_Expedition_to_Abyssinia "1868 Expedition to Abyssinia"). Since then, the country was seen as redundant by world powers until the opening of [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal "Suez Canal") due to the influence of the Mahdist War.[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]

Today, Ethiopia maintains strong relations with [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China%E2%80%93Ethiopia_relations "China–Ethiopia relations"), [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93Israel_relations "Ethiopia–Israel relations"), [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93Mexico_relations "Ethiopia–Mexico relations"), [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93Turkey_relations "Ethiopia–Turkey relations") and [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93India_relations "Ethiopia–India relations") as well as neighbouring countries. Ethiopia is a strategic partner of Global [War on Terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_on_Terrorism "War on Terrorism") and [African Growth and Opportunity Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Growth_and_Opportunity_Act "African Growth and Opportunity Act") (AGOA). Former [U.S. President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._President "U.S. President") [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama") was the first incumbent U.S. president to visit Ethiopia in July 2015; the speech he gave to the [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union "African Union") (AU) during this trip focused on combatting Islamic terrorism. [Emigration from Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigration_from_Ethiopia "Emigration from Ethiopia") is primarily directed towards Europe, including [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopians_in_Italy "Ethiopians in Italy"), the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopians_in_the_United_Kingdom "Ethiopians in the United Kingdom") and [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopians_in_Sweden "Ethiopians in Sweden"), as well as [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Canadians "Ethiopian Canadians") and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Australians "Ethiopian Australians"), while emigration to the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") is primarily to [Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabia "Saudi Arabia") and [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel"). Ethiopia is founding member of the [Non-Aligned Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement "Non-Aligned Movement"), and the [G24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_24 "Group of 24") and [G77](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_77 "Group of 77"). In 1963, the Organisation of African Unity, which later renamed itself the African Union, was founded in Addis Ababa, which today hosts the secretariat of the African Union, the African Union Commission. In addition, Ethiopia is also a member of the [Pan African Chamber of Commerce and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_African_Chamber_of_Commerce_and_Industry "Pan African Chamber of Commerce and Industry"), the [United Nations Economic Commission for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Economic_Commission_for_Africa "United Nations Economic Commission for Africa") (UNECA), the [African Standby Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Standby_Force "African Standby Force") and many of global [NGOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGO "NGO") focused on Africa.

Ethiopia's foreign relations with both [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93Sudan_relations "Ethiopia–Sudan relations") and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt%E2%80%93Ethiopia_relations "Egypt–Ethiopia relations") are somewhat fraught owing to the effects the [Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Ethiopian_Renaissance_Dam "Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam") project, which was escalated in 2020, would have on [water rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resources_law "Water resources law") in the region. Despite six upstream countries (Ethiopia, Kenya, [Uganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uganda "Uganda"), [Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwanda "Rwanda"), [Burundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burundi "Burundi"), and [Tanzania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanzania "Tanzania")) signing the [Nile Basin Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_Basin_Initiative "Nile Basin Initiative") in 2010, Egypt and Sudan rejected a water sharing treaty, citing the reduction of amount of water to the [Nile Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_Basin "Nile Basin") and the challenge it would pose to their historic connection of [water rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_rights "Water rights"). In 2020, Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed warned that "No force can stop Ethiopia from building a dam. If there is need to go to war, we could get millions readied."

Ethiopia is one of the African countries that was a founding member of [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations "League of Nations"), which served as the predecessor for the UN, since 1923. UN taskforces in Ethiopia deal primarily with humanitarian issues and development. Some of its agencies[*[which?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words "Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words")*] maintain regional ties with UNECA and the AU. The UN prioritises [sustainable development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_development "Sustainable development") in Ethiopia, including fighting poverty, sustainable economic growth, [climate change policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_policy "Climate change policy"), educational and healthcare provisions, increasing employment, and environmental protection.

### Military

The Ethiopian army's origins and military traditions date back to the earliest [history of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ethiopia "History of Ethiopia"). Due to Ethiopia's location between the Middle East and Africa, it has long been in the middle of Eastern and Western politics and has been subject to foreign invasions. In 1579, the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire")'s attempt to expand from a coastal base at [Massawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massawa "Massawa") during the [Ottoman conquest of Habesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_conquest_of_Habesh "Ottoman conquest of Habesh") was defeated. The [Army of the Ethiopian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Ethiopian_Empire "Army of the Ethiopian Empire") was also able to defeat the Egyptians in 1876 at [Gura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gura "Battle of Gura"), led by Ethiopian Emperor Yohannes IV. Ethiopia only has 3 branches inside the military, consisting of the [Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Ground_Forces "Ethiopian Ground Forces"), Airforce, and a [Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Navy "Ethiopian Navy") although it is landlocked. With an annual budget of over $1 billion,[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] it is the largest armed force in East Africa, and one of the largest in Africa.

### Law enforcement

Ethiopia has two main federal law enforcement agencies at the federal level, the [Ethiopian Federal Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Federal_Police "Ethiopian Federal Police") (EFP) and the [National Intelligence and Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Intelligence_and_Security_Service_(Ethiopia) "National Intelligence and Security Service (Ethiopia)") (NISS). The NISS, serving both as a spy agency and domestic law enforcement agency, has national jurisdiction for counterterrorism, foreign-related counterespionage activities, and protecting [national security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_security "National security"). It also has authority over international economic crimes relating to Ethiopia as a whole. It and the EFP both enforce federal laws domestically and cooperate internationally to enforce border regulations and peoples and products coming in and out in Ethiopia. The EFP mostly polices non-international and non-espionage crimes, including [civil rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_and_political_rights "Civil and political rights") matters and domestic financial crimes. At the regional level, Ethiopia has 12 regional statewide police departments and 2 federally chartered police forces, those being the Addis Ababa Police and Dire Dawa Police forces.

## Economy

Ethiopia registered the fastest economic growth under [Meles Zenawi's administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Meles_Zenawi "Premiership of Meles Zenawi").
According to the [IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF "IMF"), Ethiopia was one of the fastest-growing economies in the world, registering over 10% economic growth from 2004 through 2009. It was the fastest-growing non-oil-dependent African economy in the years 2007 and 2008. In 2015, the World Bank highlighted that Ethiopia had witnessed rapid economic growth with real domestic product (GDP) growth averaging 10.9% between 2004 and 2014.

In 2008 and 2011, Ethiopia's growth performance and considerable development gains were challenged by high [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation "Inflation") and a difficult [balance of payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_payments "Balance of payments") situation. Inflation surged to 40% in August 2011 because of loose [monetary policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy "Monetary policy"), large [civil service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service "Civil service") wage increase in early 2011, and high food prices.

In spite of fast growth in recent years, Ethiopia's GDP per capita is one of the lowest in the world, and the economy faces a number of serious structural problems. Based on the most recent data from 2019, 68.7% of the population is affected by [multidimensional poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidimensional_Poverty_Index "Multidimensional Poverty Index") and an additional 18.4% are vulnerable to it.

With a focused investment in public infrastructure and industrial parks, Ethiopia is attempting to address its structural problems by becoming a hub for light manufacturing in Africa. In 2019 a law was passed allowing expatriate Ethiopians to invest in Ethiopia's financial service industry.

Article 40 of the [Ethiopian constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_constitution "Ethiopian constitution") specifies that rights to own land belong only to "the state and the people" of Ethiopia. Citizens may lease land for up to 99 years, though they are unable to mortgage or sell. Renting out land for a maximum of twenty years is allowed, and this is expected to ensure that land goes to the most productive user.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Land distribution and administration is considered an area where corruption is institutionalised, and facilitation payments as well as bribes are often demanded when dealing with land-related issues. As there is no land ownership, infrastructural projects are most often simply done without asking the land users, which then end up being displaced and without a home or land. A lot of anger and distrust sometimes result in public protests. In addition, agricultural productivity remains low, and frequent droughts still beset the country, also leading to internal displacement.

### Energy and hydropower

Ethiopia has 14 major rivers flowing from its highlands, including the Nile. It has the largest water reserves in Africa. As of 2012[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), hydroelectric plants represented around 88.2% of the total installed electricity generating capacity. The remaining electrical power was generated from fossil fuels (8.3%) and renewable sources (3.6%).

The electrification rate for the total population in 2016 was 42%, with 85% coverage in urban areas and 26% coverage in rural areas. As of 2016[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), total electricity production was 11.15 TWh and consumption was 9.062 TWh. There were 0.166 TWh of electricity exported, 0 kWh imported, and 2.784 GW of installed generating capacity.
Ethiopia delivers roughly 81% of water volume to the Nile through the river basins of the Blue Nile, [Sobat River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobat_River "Sobat River") and [Atbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atbara "Atbara"). In 1959, Egypt and Sudan signed a bilateral treaty, the [1959 Nile Waters Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_politics_in_the_Nile_Basin "Water politics in the Nile Basin"), which gave both countries exclusive maritime rights over the Nile waters. Ever since, Egypt has discouraged almost all projects in Ethiopia that sought to use the local Nile tributaries. This had the effect of discouraging external financing of hydropower and irrigation projects in western Ethiopia, thereby impeding water resource-based economic development projects. However, Ethiopia is in the process of constructing a large 6,450 MW hydroelectric dam on the Blue Nile River. Completed in 2023, this Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam is slated to be the largest hydroelectric power station in Africa. The [Gibe III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgel_Gibe_III_Dam "Gilgel Gibe III Dam") hydroelectric project is so far the largest in the country with an installed capacity of 1,870 MW. For the year 2017–18 (2010 E.C) this hydroelectric dam generated 4,900 GWh.

### Agriculture

Agriculture constitutes around 85% of the labour force. However, the service sector represents the largest portion of the GDP. Many other economic activities depend on agriculture, including marketing, processing, and export of agricultural products. Production is overwhelmingly by small-scale farmers and enterprises, and a large part of commodity exports are provided by the small agricultural cash-crop sector. Principal crops include [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffea "Coffea"), [legumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legume "Legume"), [oilseeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapeseed "Rapeseed"), [cereals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal "Cereal"), potatoes, [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane "Sugarcane"), and vegetables. Ethiopia is also a [Vavilov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_of_origin "Centre of origin") centre of diversity for domesticated crops, including [enset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensete_ventricosum "Ensete ventricosum"), [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_bean "Coffee bean"), [okra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra "Okra"), and [teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff "Teff").

Exports are almost entirely agricultural commodities (with the exception of gold), and coffee is the largest foreign exchange earner. Ethiopia is Africa's second biggest [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize") producer.

### Exports

Ethiopia is often considered as the birthplace of [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee "Coffee") since cultivation began in the 9th century. Exports from Ethiopia in the 2009–2010 financial year totalled US$1.4 billion. Ethiopia produces more coffee than any other nation on the continent. "Coffee provides a livelihood for close to 15 million Ethiopians, 16% of the population. Farmers in the eastern part of the country, where a warming climate is already impacting production, have struggled in recent years, and many are currently reporting largely failed harvests as a result of a prolonged drought".

Ethiopia also has the fifth largest inventory of [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arado_cattle "Arado cattle"). Other main export commodities are [khat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khat "Khat"), gold, leather products, and oilseeds. Recent development of the [floriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floriculture "Floriculture") sector means Ethiopia is poised to become one of the top flower and plant exporters in the world.

Cross-border trade by pastoralists is often informal and beyond state control and regulation. In East Africa, over 95% of cross-border trade is through unofficial channels. The unofficial trade of live cattle, camels, sheep, and goats from Ethiopia sold to Somalia, Djibouti, and Kenya generates an estimated total value of US$250–300 million annually (100 times more than the official figure).

This trade helps lower food prices, increase food security, relieve border tensions, and promote regional integration. However, the unregulated and undocumented nature of this trade runs risks, such as allowing disease to spread more easily across national borders. Furthermore, the government of Ethiopia is purportedly unhappy with lost tax revenue and foreign exchange revenues. Recent initiatives have sought to document and regulate this trade.

With the private sector growing slowly, designer [fashion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion_in_Ethiopia "Fashion in Ethiopia") and leather products like bags are becoming a big export business, with Taytu becoming the first luxury designer label in the country. Additional small-scale export products include cereals, pulses, cotton, sugarcane, potatoes, and hides. With the construction of various new dams and growing hydroelectric power projects around the country, Ethiopia also plans to export electric power to its neighbours.

Most regard Ethiopia's large water resources and potential as its "white oil" and its coffee resources as "black gold".

### Transport

Two trans-African automobile routes pass through Ethiopia: the [Cairo-Cape Town Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo-Cape_Town_Highway "Cairo-Cape Town Highway") and the [N'Djamena-Djibouti Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%27Djamena-Djibouti_Highway "N'Djamena-Djibouti Highway"). Ethiopia has 926 km of electrified 1,435 mm (4 ft 8+1⁄2 in) [standard gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gauge "Standard gauge") railways, 656 km for the [Addis Ababa–Djibouti Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_Ababa%E2%80%93Djibouti_Railway "Addis Ababa–Djibouti Railway") between Addis Ababa and the [Port of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Djibouti "Port of Djibouti") (via [Awash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awash,_Ethiopia "Awash, Ethiopia")) and 270 km for the [Awash–Hara Gebeya Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awash%E2%80%93Hara_Gebeya_Railway "Awash–Hara Gebeya Railway") between Addis Ababa and the [twin cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_cities "Twin cities") of [Dessie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dessie "Dessie")/[Kombolcha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kombolcha "Kombolcha").

Ethiopia had 58 airports as of 2012[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), and 61 as of 2016[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit). Among these, the [Bole International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bole_International_Airport "Bole International Airport") in Addis Ababa and the [Aba Tenna Dejazmach Yilma International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aba_Tenna_Dejazmach_Yilma_International_Airport "Aba Tenna Dejazmach Yilma International Airport") in Dire Dawa accommodate international flights.

### Science and technology

Science and technology in Ethiopia are progressively emerging due to the lack of organised institutions. Manufacturing and service providers often place themselves in [competitive programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competitive_programming "Competitive programming") in order to advance innovative and technological solutions through in-house arenas.[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] The [Ethiopian Space Science and Technology Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Space_Science_and_Technology_Institute "Ethiopian Space Science and Technology Institute") is responsible for conducting multifaceted tasks regarding space and technology. In addition, Ethiopia also launched a 70 kg ET-RSS1 multi-spectral remote sensing satellite in December 2019. The President [Sahle-Work Zewde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahle-Work_Zewde "Sahle-Work Zewde") said that "the satellite will provide all the necessary data on changes in climate and weather-related phenomena that would be used for the country's key targets in agriculture, forestry as well as natural resources protection initiatives."[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Satellite manufacturing, assembling, integrating and testing had begun by January 2020. This would also incremented facility built by a French company funded by the [European Investment Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Investment_Bank "European Investment Bank") (EIB).[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] The use of the [Entoto Observatory Space Science Research Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entoto_Observatory_Space_Science_Research_Centre "Entoto Observatory Space Science Research Centre") (EORC) is allocated to space programmes. The Ethiopian Biotechnology Institute is responsible for environmental and climate conservation. Numerous scientists have contributed to these projects, including Kitaw Ejigu, [Mulugeta Bekele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulugeta_Bekele "Mulugeta Bekele"), [Aklilu Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aklilu_Lemma "Aklilu Lemma"), [Gebisa Ejeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gebisa_Ejeta "Gebisa Ejeta"), and [Melaku Worede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melaku_Worede "Melaku Worede").

Computer scientist [Timnit Gebru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timnit_Gebru "Timnit Gebru"), named one of *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")'*s most influential people in 2022, was born in Ethiopia.

Ethiopia is known for the use of [traditional medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_medicine "Traditional medicine") since millennia. The first epidemic that occurred in Ethiopia was in 849, causing the Aksumite Emperor Abba Yohannes to be evicted due to "God's punishment for misdeeds".[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] The first traditional medicinal remedy was claimed to be derived from this catastrophe, but the exact source is debated. Though it differs among ethnic groups, traditional medicine often implements herbs, spiritual healing, bone-setting and minor surgical procedures in treating disease.

Ethiopia is the most populous landlocked country in the world. Its total population has grown substantially in recent decades, rising from 38.1 million in 1983 to 135 M in 2025. The 2050 Ethiopia population forecast is 225 M. According to UN estimations in 2013, life expectancy had improved substantially over time, with male life expectancy reported to be 56 years and for women 60 years.

Ethiopia's population is highly diverse, containing over 80 different ethnic groups, the four largest of which are the Oromo, Amhara, [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalis "Somalis") and [Tigrayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigrayans "Tigrayans"). According to the Ethiopian national census of 2007, the Oromo are the largest ethnic group in Ethiopia, at 34.4% of the nation's population. The Amhara represent 27.0% of the country's inhabitants, while Somalis and Tigrayans represent 6.2% and 6.1% of the population respectively.

Afroasiatic-speaking communities make up the majority of the population. Among these, Semitic speakers often collectively refer to themselves as the [Habesha people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habesha_people "Habesha people"). The Arabic form of this term (*al-Ḥabasha*) is the etymological basis of "Abyssinia", the former name of Ethiopia in English and other European languages.

In 2009, Ethiopia hosted a population of refugees and asylum seekers numbering approximately 135,200. The majority of this population came from Somalia (approximately 64,300 persons), Eritrea (41,700) and Sudan (25,900). The Ethiopian government required nearly all refugees to live in refugee camps.

### Urbanisation

Population growth, migration, and urbanisation are all straining both governments' and ecosystems' capacity to provide people with basic services. Urbanisation has steadily been increasing in Ethiopia, with two periods of significantly rapid growth. First, in 1936–1941 during the Italian occupation under Mussolini's fascist government, and then from 1967 to 1975 when the populations of urban areas tripled.

In 1936, Italy annexed Ethiopia, building infrastructure to connect major cities, and a dam providing power and water. This, along with the influx of Italians and labourers, was the major cause of rapid growth during this period. The second period of growth was from 1967 to 1975, when rural populations migrated to towns seeking work and better living conditions.

This pattern slowed due to the 1975 Land Reform program instituted by the government, which provided incentives for people to stay in rural areas. As people moved from rural areas to the cities, there were fewer people to grow food for the population. The Land Reform Act was meant to increase agriculture since food production was not keeping up with population growth over the period of 1970–1983. This program encouraged the formation of peasant associations, large villages based on agriculture. The legislation did lead to an increase in food production, although there is debate over the cause; it may be related to weather conditions more than the reform. Urban populations have continued to grow with an 8.1% increase from 1975 to 2000.

At least as of 2024[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), Ethiopia is one of the most rapidly urbanising countries in the world, although its population is still largely rural.

#### Rural and urban life

Migration to urban areas is usually motivated by the hope of better lives. In peasant associations, daily life is a struggle to survive. About 16% of the population in Ethiopia lives on less than one dollar[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] per day (2008). Only 65% of rural households in Ethiopia consume the [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization")'s (WHO's) minimum standard of food per day (2,200 [kcal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie "Calorie")), with 42% of children under five years old being underweight.

Most poor families (75%) share their sleeping quarters with livestock, and 40% of children sleep on the floor, where nighttime temperatures average 5 degrees Celsius in the cold season. The average family size is six or seven, living in a 30 square metre mud and thatch hut, with less than two hectares of land to cultivate.

The peasant associations face a cycle of poverty. Since the landholdings are so small, farmers cannot allow the land to lie fallow, which reduces soil fertility. This land degradation reduces the production of fodder for livestock, which causes low milk yields. Since the community burns livestock manure as fuel, rather than plowing the nutrients back into the land, the crop production is reduced. The low productivity of agriculture leads to inadequate incomes for farmers, hunger, malnutrition and disease. These unhealthy farmers have difficulty working the land and the productivity drops further.

Although conditions are drastically better in cities, all of Ethiopia suffers from poverty and [poor sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Ethiopia "Water supply and sanitation in Ethiopia"). However, poverty in Ethiopia fell from 44% to 29.6% during 2000–2011, according to the World Bank. In the capital city of Addis Ababa, 55% of the population used to live in slums. However, a construction boom in both the private and the public sector has led to a dramatic improvement in living standards in major cities, particularly in Addis Ababa. Notably, government-built condominium housing complexes have sprung up throughout the city, benefiting close to 600,000 individuals. Sanitation is the most pressing need in the city, with most of the population lacking access to waste treatment facilities. This contributes to the spread of illness through unhealthy water.

Despite the urban living conditions, residents of Addis Ababa are more financially secure than those living in peasant associations, owing to their increased educational and economic opportunities. Unlike rural children, 69% of urban children are enrolled in primary school, and 35% are eligible to attend secondary school.[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] Addis Ababa has its own [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_Ababa_University "Addis Ababa University") as well as its own [secondary schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_school "Secondary school"). The literacy rate in Addis Ababa is 82% as of 2008.

Many [NGOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGO "NGO") are working to solve this problem; however, most are far apart, uncoordinated, and working in isolation. The Sub-Saharan Africa NGO Consortium is attempting to coordinate efforts.

### Languages

According to [Glottolog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottolog "Glottolog"), there are 109 languages spoken in Ethiopia, while *[Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnologue "Ethnologue")* lists 90 individual languages spoken in the country. Most people in the country speak Afroasiatic languages of the [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages "Cushitic languages") or [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages "Semitic languages") branches. The former includes the [Oromo language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_language "Oromo language"), spoken by the Oromo, and [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_language "Somali language"), spoken by the Somalis; the latter includes Amharic, spoken by the Amhara, and [Tigrinya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigrinya_language "Tigrinya language"), spoken by the Tigrayans. Together, these four groups make up about three-quarters of Ethiopia's population. Other Afroasiatic languages with a significant number of speakers include the Cushitic [Sidamo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidamo_language "Sidamo language"), [Afar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_language "Afar language"), [Hadiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadiyya_language "Hadiyya language") and [Agaw languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agaw_languages "Agaw languages"), as well as the Semitic [Gurage languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurage_languages "Gurage languages"), [Harari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harari_language "Harari language"), [Silt'e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silt%27e_language "Silt'e language"), and [Argobba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argobba_language "Argobba language") languages. Arabic, which also belongs to the Afroasiatic family, is likewise spoken in some areas.

English is the most widely spoken foreign language, the medium of instruction in secondary schools and all tertiary education; federal laws are also published in British English in the [Federal Negarit Gazeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Negarit_Gazeta "Federal Negarit Gazeta") including the 1995 constitution.

Amharic was once the language of primary school instruction, but has been replaced in many areas by regional languages such as Oromo, Somali or Tigrinya. All languages enjoy equal state recognition in the 1995 Constitution of Ethiopia.

#### Script

Ethiopia's principal orthography is the [Ge'ez script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%27ez_script "Ge'ez script"). Employed as an [abugida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abugida "Abugida") for several of the country's languages, it first came into usage in the 6th and 5th centuries BC as an [abjad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abjad "Abjad") to transcribe the Semitic [Ge'ez language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%27ez_language "Ge'ez language"). Ge'ez now serves as the liturgical language of both the [Ethiopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church "Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church") and [Eritrean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church "Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church") Orthodox Tewahedo Churches. During the 1980s, the Ethiopic character set was computerised. It is today part of the [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode "Unicode") standard as Ethiopic, [Ethiopic Extended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopic_Extended "Ethiopic Extended"), [Ethiopic Supplement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopic_Supplement "Ethiopic Supplement"), and [Ethiopic Extended-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopic_Extended-A "Ethiopic Extended-A").

Other [writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system "Writing system") have also been used over the years by different Ethiopian communities. The latter include [Bakri Sapalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakri_Sapalo "Bakri Sapalo")'s script for Oromo.

### Religion

According to the 2007 National Census, Christians make up 62.8% of the country's population, Muslims 33.9%, practitioners of traditional faiths 2.6%, and other religions 0.6%. The ratio of the Christian to Muslim population has largely remained stable when compared to previous censuses. Sunnis form the majority of Muslims with [non-denominational Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-denominational_Muslim "Non-denominational Muslim") being the second largest group of Muslims, and the [Shia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam "Shia Islam") are a minority. Sunnis are largely [Shafi'is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafi%27i "Shafi'i") or [Salafis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement "Salafi movement"); there are also many [Sufis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") there.

Ethiopia has close historical ties with all three of the world's major [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions"). In the 4th century, the Ethiopian empire was one of the first in the world to officially adopt Christianity as the state religion. As a result of the resolutions of the [Council of Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon "Council of Chalcedon"), in 451 the [Miaphysites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysitism "Miaphysitism"), which included the vast majority of Christians in [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") and Ethiopia, were accused of [monophysitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophysitism "Monophysitism") and designated as heretics under the common name of [Coptic Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria "Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria").

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church is part of [Oriental Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy "Oriental Orthodoxy"). It is by far the largest Christian denomination, although a number of [P'ent'ay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%27ent%27ay "P'ent'ay") ([Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism "Protestantism")) churches have recently gained ground. Since 1930, a relatively small [Ethiopian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Catholic_Church "Ethiopian Catholic Church") has existed in [full communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_communion "Full communion") with Rome, with adherents making up less than 1% of the total population.

Islam in Ethiopia dates back to the founding of the religion in 622 when a group of Muslims were counselled by [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") to escape persecution in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca "Mecca"). The disciples subsequently [migrated to Abyssinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrated_to_Abyssinia "Migrated to Abyssinia") via modern-day Eritrea, which was at the time ruled by [Ashama ibn-Abjar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashama_ibn-Abjar "Ashama ibn-Abjar"), a pious Christian emperor.

### Health

Only a minority of Ethiopians are born in hospitals, while most are born in rural households. Those who are expected to give birth at home have elderly women serve as midwives who assist with the delivery. The WHO estimates that "a majority of maternal fatalities and disabilities could be prevented if deliveries were to take place at well-equipped health centres, with adequately trained staff". [Birth rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_rate "Birth rate"), [infant mortality rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality_rate "Infant mortality rate"), and [death rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_rate "Death rate") are lower in cities than in rural areas due to better access to education, medicines, and hospitals. Life expectancy is better in cities compared to rural areas, but there have been significant improvements witnessed throughout the country as of 2016, the average Ethiopian living to be 62.2 years old, according to a [UNDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNDP "UNDP") report. Despite sanitation being a problem, use of improved water sources is also on the rise; 81% in cities compared to 11% in rural areas.

Ethiopia's main health problems are said to be communicable (contagious) diseases worsened by poor sanitation and [malnutrition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malnutrition "Malnutrition"). Over 58 M people (nearly half the population) do not have access to clean water as of 2023[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit). These problems are exacerbated by the shortage of trained doctors and nurses and health facilities. The [WHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organisation "World Health Organisation")'s 2006 World Health Report gives a figure of 1,936 physicians (for 2003), which comes to about 2.6 per 100,000.

The National Mental Health Strategy, published in 2012, introduced the development of policy designed to improve mental health care in Ethiopia. This strategy mandated that mental health be integrated into the primary health care system. However, the success of the National Mental Health Strategy has been limited. For example, the burden of depression is estimated to have increased 34.2% from 2007 to 2017. Furthermore, the prevalence of stigmatising attitudes, inadequate leadership and co-ordination of efforts, as well as a lack of mental health awareness in the general population, all remain as obstacles to successful mental health care.

In the 2024 Global Hunger Index (GHI), Ethiopia ranks 102nd out of 127 countries and has a score of 26.2, which indicates a serious level of hunger.

### Education

The current system follows school expansion schemes which are very similar to the system in the rural areas during the 1980s, with an addition of deeper regionalisation, providing rural education in students' own languages starting at the elementary level, and with more budgetary financing allocated to the education sector. Public education is free at primary levels and usually offers between age 7 and 12. The sequence of general education in Ethiopia is six years of primary school, then four years of lower secondary school followed by two years of higher secondary school.

Ethiopian education is governed by the [Ministry of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education_(Ethiopia) "Ministry of Education (Ethiopia)") and uses a 4+4+2+2 system; elementary education consists of eight years, divided into two cycles of four years, and four years of secondary education, divided into two stages of two years. National exams are conducted by the [National Educational Assessment and Examination Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Educational_Assessment_and_Examination_Agency "National Educational Assessment and Examination Agency") (NEAEA). Since 2018, there are two national exams: the [Ethiopian General Secondary Education Certificate Examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_General_Secondary_Education_Certificate_Examination "Ethiopian General Secondary Education Certificate Examination") (EGSECE), also known as the Grade 10 national exam, and the Grade 12 national exam.

As of 2022[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), there are 83 universities, 42 public universities, and more than 35 higher education institutions. Foreign students constitute 16,305 in higher education level. The overall number of tertiary students in both public and private institutions exploded by more than 2,000%, from 34,000 in 1991 to 757,000 in 2014, per UIS data. Access to education in Ethiopia has improved significantly. Approximately three million people were enrolled in primary school in 1994–95, but by 2008–09, primary enrolment had risen to 15.5 million – an increase of over 500%. In 2013–14, there was a significant boost in gross enrolment across all regions. The national GER was 104.8% for boys, 97.8% for girls and 101.3% across both sexes.

The literacy rate has increased in recent years: according to the 1994 census, the literacy rate in Ethiopia was 23.4%. In 2007 it was estimated to be 39% (male 49.1% and female 28.9%). A report by [UNDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNDP "UNDP") in 2011 showed that the literacy rate in Ethiopia was 46.7%. The same report also indicated that the female literacy rate had increased from 27 to 39% from 2004 to 2011, and the male literacy rate had increased from 49 to 59% over the same period for persons 10 years and older. By 2015, the literacy rate had further increased, to 49.1% (57.2% male and 41.1% female).

## Culture

The [culture of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Ethiopia "Culture of Ethiopia") is diverse, formed by the interaction of Semitic, Cushitic and less populous Nilo-Saharan speaking peoples since the first millennium BCE. The dominant Semitic-speaking groups, such as the Tigrayans, Amharas and [Gurage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurage_people "Gurage people"), are distinguished by their feudal traditions, hierarchical structure and agrarian life. This is rooted partly in [South Arabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Arabia "South Arabia") culture as a result of [return migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_migration "Return migration"), while the southern Cushitic (Oromo and Somali) are strong adherents to [egalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarianism "Egalitarianism") and pastoral life. Other traditions from the [Kafficho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafficho_people "Kafficho people"), Sidamo, and [Afar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_people "Afar people") peoples are also present.

### Holidays

The government of Ethiopia recognises 6 patriotic and public holidays and 9 major religious holidays. The year (Gregorian calendar) starts with [Ethiopian Christmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Christmas "Ethiopian Christmas") on 7 January. [Timkat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timkat "Timkat") ('[Epiphany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphany_(holiday) "Epiphany (holiday)")'), celebrated on 19 or 20 January, marks the baptism of [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") by [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist "John the Baptist"). Timkat is recognised by the United Nations as part of its "[Intangible Cultural Heritage Lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage_Lists "UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage Lists")" which designate important cultural and religious holidays. Three holidays are movable: [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan "Ramadan"), [Good Friday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday "Good Friday") and [Easter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter "Easter"), two of which are always in spring and one of which (Ramadan) may occur at any time of year. Other holidays include [Mawlid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mawlid "Mawlid"), which begins on the 12th or 17th day of the third month of the Islamic calendar depending on the denomination of the celebrants. The majority of Ethiopian Muslims are Sunni, thus it is celebrated on the 12th day. Other Islamic holidays include [Eid al-Fitr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eid_al-Fitr "Eid al-Fitr") and [Eid al-Adha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eid_al-Adha "Eid al-Adha"). [Meskel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meskel "Meskel") is a Christian holiday celebrating the discovery of the [True Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross "True Cross"). Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha are trans-national holidays celebrated across the Islamic world, while Meskel is also celebrated in Eritrea and by [Eritrean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean_diaspora "Eritrean diaspora") and [Ethiopian diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_diaspora "Ethiopian diaspora") communities abroad, but it is most prominent in Ethiopia.

The first public holiday of the Gregorian calendar year is [Adwa Victory Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adwa_Victory_Day "Adwa Victory Day") on 2 March, commemorating Emperor Menelik II's victory over the colonisation efforts of Italy in 1896. Others are [International Workers' Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Workers%27_Day "International Workers' Day") on the 1st of May, the [Ethiopian Patriots' Victory Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Patriots%27_Victory_Day "Ethiopian Patriots' Victory Day") for celebrating the return of Emperor Haile Selassie and thus the liberation of fascist Italian occupation on the 5th of May, and the [Downfall of the Derg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downfall_of_the_Derg_(holiday) "Downfall of the Derg (holiday)") on the 28th of May, a recent holiday promoted to public status after 1991. The most prominent public holiday is [Enkutatash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enkutatash "Enkutatash") (Ethiopian new year celebration) on 11 or 12 September. Defence Day, on the 26th of October, celebrates servicemen, and is the last holiday in the Gregorian calendar.

Various other festivals and holidays are celebrated across the country, including [Irreechaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreechaa "Irreechaa") celebrated by the Oromo community.

### Art and architecture

Ethiopian arts were largely influenced by Christian iconography throughout much of their history, such as [illuminated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscripts "Illuminated manuscripts"), [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting "Painting"), [crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_cross "Ethiopian cross"), [icons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon "Icon") and other [metalwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalwork "Metalwork") such as crowns. Most historical arts were commissioned by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the state religion for a millennium. Earlier Aksumite period art was most prominently stone carvings as evidenced in their stelae, though there is no surviving [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian "Christian") art from this era. As Christianity was introduced, its iconography was partly influenced by [Byzantine art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"). Most remaining artworks beyond the early modern period were ruined as a result of the invasion of the Adal Sultanate in the Ethiopian Highlands, but were revived by Catholic emissaries.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] The [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_art "Western art") intervention in Ethiopian art began in the 20th century, though artists maintained a traditional Ethiopian character in their work. Some notable contemporary Ethiopian artists include [Afewerk Tekle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afewerk_Tekle "Afewerk Tekle"), [Lemma Guya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemma_Guya "Lemma Guya"), [Martha Nasibù](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Nasib%C3%B9 "Martha Nasibù"), [Ale Felege Selam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ale_Felege_Selam "Ale Felege Selam"), and others.

The "Bete Medhane Alem" or "House of our Saviour" is one of the 12 churches in Lalibela built under Emperor [Lalibela I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalibela_(Emperor_of_Ethiopia) "Lalibela (Emperor of Ethiopia)"). [Emperor Lalibela I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gebre_Meskel_Lalibela "Gebre Meskel Lalibela") commissioned large portions of the Lalibela church complex, and it was named after him. This has been attributed to Christian Ethiopians' desire during medieval times, due to their blockage from Jerusalem by Muslim conquests, to replicate their own "new Jerusalem" at a national level.

Aksumite architecture continued to flourish in the 4th century CE. Aksumite stelae commonly used single blocks and rocks. The Tomb of the False Door, built for Aksumite emperors, used the monolithic style. The Lalibela civilisation was largely influenced by Aksumite architecture, but the layer of stones or wood is quite different for some dwellings.

In the Gondarine period, the architecture of Ethiopia was influenced by Baroque, Arab, Turkish and Gujarati Indian styles independently taught by Portuguese emissaries in the 16th and 17th centuries.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] One example is the imperial fortress Fasil Ghebbi, which is influenced by a mix of these styles. Medieval architecture also influenced architecture of the later 19th- and 20th-century era.

### Literature

[Ethiopian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_literature "Ethiopian literature") traces back to the Aksumite period in the 4th century, mostly religious motifs. In royal inscriptions, it employed both [Ge'ez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%CA%BDez_script "Geʽez script") and Greek, but the latter was discontinued in 350.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Unlike most Sub-Saharan African countries, Ethiopia has an ancient distinct language, Ge'ez, which dominated politics and education, In spite of the current political instability in the country endangering the cultural heritage of these works, preservation has improved in recent years.

During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), composers such as [Abba Gorgoryos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abba_Gorgoryos "Abba Gorgoryos") and [Giyorgis of Segla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giyorgis_of_Segla "Giyorgis of Segla") made lasting contributions to Ethiopian languages, such as writing the earliest dictionaries of Amharic and Ge'ez, as well as composing religious hymns and doctrinal justifications of issues in the Ethiopian Orthodox church and practices.

Ethiopian literary works in this period mostly consisted of handwritten [codexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex "Codex") ([Amharic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amharic_language "Amharic language"): ብራና, [romanised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Amharic "Romanization of Amharic"): *branna*). *Branna* are prepared by gathering parchment leaves and sewing them together. The size may vary considerably depending on the number of volumes and manner of preparation. For example, a pocket size codex is 45 cm in length, but are heavier in weight. Historians have speculated that archaic codexes existed in Ethiopia.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Today manuscripts resembling primitive codexes are still used where parchment leaves are convenient for writing.

Another notable form of writing is the protective (or magic) [scroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scroll "Scroll"). Some of these were intended for magical purposes, such as the *ketab*, which is used for magical defence. Scrolls were typically produced by *[debtera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debtera "Debtera")*, non-ordained clerical experts on [exorcism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism "Exorcism") and healings. Scrolls could range in size from the portable 30 cm scroll, to the 2 cm size,[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] which is often unrolled and hung on the walls of houses. Scrolls being the original medium for Ethiopia literature is highly disputed, as there is overwhelming evidence that Ge'ez language books were written in codex form.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Ethiopians also used accordion books (called *[sensul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensul "Sensul")*) in the late 15th and 16th century, made of folded parchment paper, with or without a cover. These books usually contain pictorial representations of the life and death of religious figures, juxtaposed with other significant texts.

[Baalu Girma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baalu_Girma "Baalu Girma") and [Haddis Alemayehu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haddis_Alemayehu "Haddis Alemayehu") have been noted as the most influential novelists in Ethiopian history. Girma has critiqued the communist government in the 80s in Ethiopia in his works. Haddis Alemayehu wrote one of the first examinations of [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(international_relations) "Realism (international relations)"), as well as a [romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_novel "Romance novel") tragedy novel, and influenced the Ethiopian intellectual community.

[Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry "Poetry") is popular in Ethiopia. Most poets recount past events, social unrests, poverty and famine. *[Qene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qene "Qene")* is the most used element of Ethiopian poetry – it is regarded as a form of Amharic poetry, though the term may also refer to any poem. The defining characteristic of *qene* is *sem-ena-werq* ([Amharic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amharic_language "Amharic language"): ሰምና ወርቅ, [romanised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Amharic "Romanization of Amharic"): *säməna wärq*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'wax and gold'), a literary device where two metaphorical words, one with an obvious meaning (wax) and the other more obscure (the gold), are paired to layer hidden meanings within the text. The most notable poets are [Tsegaye Gebre-Medhin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsegaye_Gabre-Medhin "Tsegaye Gabre-Medhin"), [Kebede Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kebede_Michael "Kebede Michael") and [Mengistu Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mengistu_Lemma "Mengistu Lemma").

Ethiopian philosophy has been superlatively prolific since ancient times in Africa, though strongly influenced by [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy") and [Patristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristic "Patristic") philosophy. The best known philosophical revival was in the early modern period, such as [Zera Yacob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zera_Yacob_(philosopher) "Zera Yacob (philosopher)") (1599–1692) and his student [Walda Heywat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walda_Heywat "Walda Heywat"), who wrote *[Hatata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatata "Hatata")* (*Inquiry*) in 1667 as an argument for the [existence of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_of_God "Existence of God").

### Music

The [music of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Ethiopia "Music of Ethiopia") is extremely diverse, with each of the country's 80 ethnic groups being associated with unique sounds. Ethiopian music uses a distinct [modal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_mode "Musical mode") that is [pentatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentatonic_scale "Pentatonic scale"), with characteristically long intervals between some notes. As with many other aspects of Ethiopian culture and tradition, tastes in music and lyrics are strongly linked with those in neighbouring Eritrea, Somalia, Djibouti, and Sudan. Traditional singing in Ethiopia presents diverse styles of [polyphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphony "Polyphony") ([heterophony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterophony "Heterophony"), [drone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drone_(music) "Drone (music)"), imitation, and [counterpoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterpoint "Counterpoint")). Traditionally, lyricism in Ethiopian songwriting is strongly associated with views of patriotism or national pride, romance, friendship, and a unique type of memoire known as *[tizita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tizita "Tizita")*.

Saint [Yared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yared "Yared"), a 6th-century Aksumite composer, is widely regarded as the father of the traditional music of Eritrea and Ethiopia, creating liturgical music of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Churches.

Modern music traces back to the reign of Emperor Haile Selassie, when 40 Armenian orphans called [Arba Lijoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arba_Lijoch "Arba Lijoch") arrived from [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") to Addis Ababa. By 1924, the band had become Ethiopia's first official orchestra, but after World War II, several similar bands emerged, such as the Imperial Bodyguard Band, Army Band, and Police Band.

In the 1960s and 1970s, traditional-style modern Ethiopian music was revived in what is known as the "[Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Golden_Age_of_music "Ethiopian Golden Age of music")". Several notable musical artists emerged thereafter, such as [Tilahun Gessesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilahun_Gessesse "Tilahun Gessesse"), [Alemayehu Eshete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemayehu_Eshete "Alemayehu Eshete"), [Bizunesh Bekele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bizunesh_Bekele "Bizunesh Bekele"), [Muluken Melesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muluken_Melesse "Muluken Melesse"), and [Mahmoud Ahmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmoud_Ahmed "Mahmoud Ahmed"). These artists also employed the traditional style called *tizita*. During the Derg regime, these artists were prohibited to perform in the country and often forced into exile in North America and Europe, causing them to incorporate jazz and funk influences. Musicians of this style include Roha Band, [Walias Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walias_Band "Walias Band"), and Ethio Stars. By this time, [Neway Debebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neway_Debebe "Neway Debebe") was critical of the Derg government.

Modern music developed further in the 1990s and 2000s. In this period, the most popular artists were [Aster Aweke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aster_Aweke "Aster Aweke"), [Gigi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigi_(singer) "Gigi (singer)") and [Teddy Afro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teddy_Afro "Teddy Afro"). Ethiopian music further modernised in the next decade. DJ [Rophnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rophnan "Rophnan") was renowned for pioneering [EDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_dance_music "Electronic dance music") in the country after releasing his debut album *Reflection* in 2018.

### Calendar

Ethiopia has several local calendars. The most widely known is the [Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar "Ethiopian calendar"), also known as the Ge'ez calendar, and written with the ancient Ge'ez script, one of the oldest alphabets still in use in the world. It is based on the older Alexandrian or [Coptic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_calendar "Coptic calendar"), which in turn derives from the [Egyptian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_calendar "Egyptian calendar"). Like the Coptic calendar, the Ethiopian calendar has twelve months of exactly 30 days each plus five or six [epagomenal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epagomenal "Epagomenal") days, which form a thirteenth month. The Ethiopian months begin on the same days as those of the Coptic calendar, but their names are in Ge'ez.

Like the [Julian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_calendar "Julian calendar"), the sixth epagomenal day—which in essence is a [leap day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_day "Leap day")—is added every four years without exception on 29 August of the Julian calendar, six months before the Julian leap day. Thus, the first day of the Ethiopian year, 1 Mäskäräm, for years between 1901 and 2099 (inclusive), is usually 11 September ([Gregorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar "Gregorian calendar")), but falls on 12 September in years before the Gregorian leap year. It is approximately seven years and three months behind the Gregorian calendar because of an alternate calculation in determining the date of the [Annunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annunciation "Annunciation") of Jesus.

Another calendrical system was developed around 300 BC by the Oromo people. A lunar-stellar calendar, this [Oromo calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_calendar "Oromo calendar") relies on astronomical observations of the moon in conjunction with seven particular stars or constellations. The Oromo months (stars/lunar phases) are *Bittottessa* (Iangulum), *Camsa* (Pleiades), *Bufa* (Aldebarran), *Waxabajjii* (Belletrix), *Obora Gudda* (Central Orion-Saiph), *Obora Dikka* (Sirius), *Birra* (full moon), *Cikawa* (gibbous moon), *Sadasaa* (quarter moon), *Abrasa* (large crescent), *Ammaji* (medium crescent), and *Gurrandala* (small crescent).

### Media

The [Ethiopian Broadcasting Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Broadcasting_Corporation "Ethiopian Broadcasting Corporation") (EBC), formerly known as ETV, is a [state-owned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_media "State media") [public service broadcaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service_broadcaster "Public service broadcaster"). Radio broadcasting began in 1935, and television service began in 1962 with the assistance of British firm Thomson and Emperor Haile Selassie. Since 2015, EBC has upgraded its studios with modernised transmission.

[Kana TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kana_TV "Kana TV") is the most popular TV channel in Ethiopia. It is mainly known for [dubbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubbing_(filmmaking) "Dubbing (filmmaking)") foreign content into Amharic. For several decades, EBC served as the main source for mass media until the late 2000s, when [EBS TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBS_TV_(Ethiopia) "EBS TV (Ethiopia)") launched as the first private television channel. Moreover, numerous private channels were founded in 2016, contributing to the growth of privately owned media companies in the country. [Fana TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fana_TV "Fana TV") has been the largest TV network since its launch in 2017.

The most widely circulated newspapers in Ethiopia are [Addis Fortune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_Fortune "Addis Fortune"), [Capital Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Ethiopia "Capital Ethiopia"), [Ethiopian Reporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Reporter "Ethiopian Reporter"), Addis Zemen (in Amharic), and [Ethiopian Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Herald "Ethiopian Herald").

The sole internet service provider is the national telecommunications firm [Ethio telecom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethio_telecom "Ethio telecom"). A large portion of users in the country access the internet through mobile devices. As of July 2016[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ethiopia&action=edit), there are around 4.29 million people who have internet access at their home as compared to a quarter of a million users a decade before that. The Ethiopian government has at times intentionally shut down internet service in the country or restricted access to certain social media sites during periods of political unrest. In August 2016, following protests and demonstrations in the Oromia Region, all access to the internet was shut down for a period of two days. In June 2017, the government shut down access to the internet for mobile users during a period that coincided with the administration of university entrance examination. Although the reason for the restriction was not confirmed by the government, the move was similar to a measure taken during the same period in 2016, after a leak of test questions.

### Cinema

The first cinema was introduced in 1898, three years after the first world film was projected. Italian minister [Federico Ciccodicola](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Federico_Ciccodicola&action=edit&redlink=1 "Federico Ciccodicola (page does not exist)") [[it](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Ciccodicola "it:Federico Ciccodicola")] offered cinematic artefacts[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] to Emperor Menelik II. The early 20th century appearance with spectacle was around 1909 and embraced by documentary or biographical films. *Au de Menilek* was the first film directed by Charles Martel. The first 16mm black-and-white film dedicated to coronation of Emperor Zewditu, then the coronation of Emperor Haile Selassie, was filmed. The 1990s saw an international boom in Ethiopian films. The most influential people in this era were [Haile Gerima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haile_Gerima "Haile Gerima"), [Salem Mekuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem_Mekuria "Salem Mekuria"), Yemane Demissie, and [Teshome Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teshome_Gabriel "Teshome Gabriel"). Films began to be filmed in the Amharic language in the 2000s. The Ethiopian films with the highest international box office grosses include *[Selanchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selanchi "Selanchi")*, *[Difret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difret "Difret")*, *[Lamb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb_(2015_Ethiopian_film) "Lamb (2015 Ethiopian film)")*, *[Prince of Love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_of_Love "Prince of Love")*, and *[Lambadina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambadina "Lambadina")*. The best-known Ethiopian actors include [Selam Tesfaye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selam_Tesfaye "Selam Tesfaye"), [Fryat Yemane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fryat_Yemane "Fryat Yemane"), [Hanan Tarik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanan_Tarik "Hanan Tarik"), [Mahder Assefa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahder_Assefa "Mahder Assefa"), [Amleset Muchie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amleset_Muchie "Amleset Muchie"), and [Ruth Negga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Negga "Ruth Negga").

One of the most prestigious film awards is the [Gumma Film Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumma_Film_Awards "Gumma Film Awards"), held in Addis Ababa. The ceremony, which was started in 2014, is broadcast on live television on some stations. Festivals including [Addis International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addis_International_Film_Festival "Addis International Film Festival") and the [Ethiopian International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_International_Film_Festival "Ethiopian International Film Festival") showcase amateur and professional filmmakers' works. They were established in 2007 and 2005 respectively.

### Cuisine

The best-known [Ethiopian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_cuisine "Ethiopian cuisine") consists of various types of thick meat [stews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stew "Stew"), known as *[wat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wat_(food) "Wat (food)")* in Ethiopian culture, and vegetable side dishes served on top of [injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera "Injera"), a large [sourdough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourdough "Sourdough") [flatbread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatbread "Flatbread") made of [teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff "Teff") flour. It is not eaten with utensils, but instead the injera is used to scoop up the entrées and side dishes. Almost universally in Ethiopia, it is common to eat from the same dish in the middle of the table with a group of people. It is also a common custom to feed others with one's own hands—a tradition referred to as *gursha*. Traditional Ethiopian cuisine employs no [pork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork "Pork"), as it is forbidden in [Ethiopian Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox "Ethiopian Orthodox") Christian and Islamic faiths; Ethiopian Orthodox Christians also [fast from meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasting_and_abstinence_in_the_Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church "Fasting and abstinence in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church") on Wednesdays, Fridays, and the days leading up to Easter and Christmas.

*[Chechebsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fit-fit#Kitcha_fit-fit "Fit-fit")*, *marqa*, *chukko*, *michirra*, and *[dhanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhanga "Dhanga")* are the most popular dishes from the Oromo people. [Kitfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitfo "Kitfo"), which originated among the Gurage people, is another of the country's most popular delicacies. In addition, *[doro wat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wat_(food)#Doro_wat "Wat (food)")* ([Amharic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amharic_language "Amharic language"): ዶሮ ወጥ) and *tsebehi derho* ([Tigrinya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigrinya_language "Tigrinya language"): ጽብሒ ድርሆ), are other popular dishes, originating from northwestern Ethiopia. *[Tihlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tihlo "Tihlo")* (ጥሕሎ), a type of [dumpling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumpling "Dumpling") prepared from roasted barley flour and originating from the [Tigray Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray_Region "Tigray Region"), is now very popular in [Amhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amhara_Region "Amhara Region") and spreading further south.

### Sport

The main sports in Ethiopia are [track and field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_and_field "Track and field") (particularly [long distance running](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_distance_running "Long distance running")) and football. Ethiopian athletes have won many [Olympic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games "Olympic Games") gold medals in track and field, most of them in long distance running. [Abebe Bikila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abebe_Bikila "Abebe Bikila") became the first athlete from a Sub-Saharan country to win an Olympic gold medal when he won the [Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marathon "Marathon") at the 1960 Rome Olympic Games in a world record time of 2:15:16.

The [Ethiopia national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia_national_football_team "Ethiopia national football team") was one of four founding members of the [Confederation of African Football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_African_Football "Confederation of African Football") and won the [Africa Cup of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_Cup_of_Nations "Africa Cup of Nations") in [1962](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1962_African_Cup_of_Nations "1962 African Cup of Nations"). Ethiopia has Sub-Saharan Africa's longest basketball tradition, as it established a [national basketball team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia_national_basketball_team "Ethiopia national basketball team") in 1949.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
