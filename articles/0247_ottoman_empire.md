# Ottoman Empire

The **Ottoman Empire**, also known historically as **Turkey** or the **Turkish Empire**, was a state that spanned much of [Southeastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Europe "Southeastern Europe"), [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia "West Asia"), and [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa") from the 14th century to the early 20th century, centred in modern-day [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey"). It also controlled parts of southeastern [Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe "Central Europe") between the early 16th and early 18th centuries.

The empire emerged from a [*beylik*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolian_beyliks "Anatolian beyliks"), or [principality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality "Principality"), founded in northwestern [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") in c. 1299 by the [Turkoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkoman_(ethnonym) "Turkoman (ethnonym)") tribal leader [Osman I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osman_I "Osman I"). His successors [conquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_wars_in_Europe "Ottoman wars in Europe") much of Anatolia and expanded into the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") by the mid-14th century, transforming their [petty kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petty_kingdom "Petty kingdom") into a transcontinental empire. The Ottomans ended the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") with the [conquest of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople") in 1453 by [Mehmed II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_II "Mehmed II"). [Further conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Mamluk_War_(1516%E2%80%931517) "Ottoman–Mamluk War (1516–1517)") by [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I "Selim I") led the Sultans to adopt the title of [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Caliphate "Ottoman Caliphate"). With its capital at [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Istanbul#Ottoman_Empire "History of Istanbul") and control over a significant portion of the [Mediterranean Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Basin "Mediterranean Basin"), the Ottoman Empire was at the centre of interactions between the Middle East and Europe for six centuries. Ruling over diverse peoples, the empire granted varying levels of autonomy to its many confessional communities, or *[millets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire) "Millet (Ottoman Empire)")*, to manage their own affairs per [Islamic law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_law "Islamic law"). During the reign of [Suleiman the Magnificent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent "Suleiman the Magnificent") in the 16th century, the Ottoman Empire became a [global power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_power "Great power").

Modern academic consensus posits that the empire began to decline after defeat in the [Second Siege of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna "Battle of Vienna") in 1683, but continued to maintain a flexible and strong economy, society and military into much of the 18th century. The Ottoman Empire fell behind technologically from the rest of Europe by the late 18th century as imperial authority fragmented. Further defeats from [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Empire "Austrian Empire") and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire "Russian Empire") culminated in the [loss of territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_and_modernization_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Decline and modernization of the Ottoman Empire"), and with [rising nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_of_nationalism_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Rise of nationalism in the Ottoman Empire") after the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution"), a number of new states emerged in the Balkans. Following [Mahmud II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_II "Mahmud II")'s reign and the *[Tanzimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanzimat "Tanzimat")* reforms over the course of the 19th century, the Ottoman state became more powerful and organised internally as a new [Ottoman identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottomanism "Ottomanism") took hold. In the [1876 revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1876_Ottoman_Revolution "1876 Ottoman Revolution"), the Ottoman Empire attempted [constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Constitutional_Era "First Constitutional Era"), before reverting to an [absolute monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_monarchy "Absolute monarchy") under [Abdul Hamid II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Hamid_II "Abdul Hamid II"), following the [Great Eastern Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Eastern_Crisis "Great Eastern Crisis").

Over the course of the late 19th century, Ottoman intellectuals known as [Young Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Turks "Young Turks") sought to liberalise and rationalise society and politics along Western lines, culminating in the [Young Turk Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Turk_Revolution "Young Turk Revolution") of 1908 led by the [Committee of Union and Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_Union_and_Progress "Committee of Union and Progress") (CUP), which reestablished [a constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Constitutional_Era "Second Constitutional Era"). However, following the disastrous [Balkan Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_Wars "Balkan Wars"), the CUP became increasingly radicalised and embraced [Turkish nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_nationalism "Turkish nationalism"), [leading to a coup d'état in 1913](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913_Ottoman_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat "1913 Ottoman coup d'état") that established a dictatorship.

In the 19th and in the start of 20th centuries, [persecution of Muslims during the Ottoman contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Muslims_during_the_Ottoman_contraction "Persecution of Muslims during the Ottoman contraction") and [in the Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Muslims#Russian_Empire "Persecution of Muslims") resulted in large-scale loss of life and [mass migration into modern-day Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhacir "Muhacir") from the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans"), [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus "Caucasus"), and [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Khanate "Crimean Khanate"). The CUP joined [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") on the side of the [Central Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Powers "Central Powers"). It struggled with internal dissent, especially the [Arab Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Revolt "Arab Revolt"), and engaged in [genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Ottoman_genocides "Late Ottoman genocides") against [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_genocide "Armenian genocide"), [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_genocide "Assyrian genocide"), and [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_genocide "Greek genocide"). In the [aftermath of World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_I "Aftermath of World War I"), the victorious [Allied Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I "Allies of World War I") occupied and [partitioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Partition of the Ottoman Empire") the Ottoman Empire, which lost its southern territories to the [United Kingdom and France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sykes%E2%80%93Picot_Agreement "Sykes–Picot Agreement"). The successful [Turkish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_War_of_Independence "Turkish War of Independence"), led by [Mustafa Kemal Atatürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Kemal_Atat%C3%BCrk "Mustafa Kemal Atatürk") against the occupying Allies, led to the [end of the Ottoman sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolition_of_the_Ottoman_sultanate "Abolition of the Ottoman sultanate") in 1922.

## Etymology

The word *Ottoman* is an historical [anglicisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicisation_(linguistics) "Anglicisation (linguistics)") of the name of [Osman I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osman_I "Osman I"), the founder of the Empire and of the ruling [House of Osman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_dynasty "Ottoman dynasty") (also known as the Ottoman dynasty). Osman's name in turn was the Turkish form of the Arabic name *[ʿUthmān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman_(name) "Uthman (name)")* (عثمان). In [Ottoman Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Turkish "Ottoman Turkish"), the empire was referred to as *Devlet-i ʿAlīye-yi ʿOsmānīye* (دولت عليه عثمانیه), lit. 'Sublime Ottoman State', or simply *Devlet-i ʿOsmānīye* (دولت عثمانيه‎), lit. 'Ottoman State'.

The Turkish word for *Ottoman* (*Osmanlı*) originally referred to the tribal followers of Osman in the 14th century. The word subsequently came to be used to refer to the empire's military-administrative elite. In contrast, the term *Turk* (*Türk*) was used to refer to the Anatolian peasant and tribal population and was seen as a disparaging term when applied to urban, educated individuals. In the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period "Early modern period"), an educated, urban-dwelling Turkish speaker who was not a member of the military-administrative class typically referred to himself neither as an *Osmanlı* nor as a *Türk*, but rather as a *Rūmī* (رومى), or "Roman", meaning an inhabitant of the territory of the former [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") in the Balkans and Anatolia. The term *Rūmī* was also used to refer to Turkish speakers by the other Muslim peoples of the empire and beyond.

In Western Europe, the names *Ottoman Empire*, *Turkish Empire* and *Turkey* were often used interchangeably, with *Turkey* being increasingly favoured both in formal and informal situations. This dichotomy was officially ended in 1920–1923, when the newly established [Ankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankara "Ankara")-based [Turkish government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_National_Assembly_of_Turkey "Grand National Assembly of Turkey") chose Turkey as the sole official name.

## History

### Rise (c. 1299–1453)

As the [Rum Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Rum "Sultanate of Rum") declined in the 13th century, [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") was a patchwork of independent Turkish principalities known as the [Anatolian Beyliks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolian_Beyliks "Anatolian Beyliks"). One of these, in the [Bithynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bithynia "Bithynia") region on the Byzantine frontier, was led by Osman I (d. 1323/4), a figure of obscure origin from whom the name Ottoman is derived. Osman's early followers were Turkish tribal groups and Byzantine renegades, not all of them Muslim. Osman extended his principality by conquering Byzantine towns along the [Sakarya River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakarya_River "Sakarya River"), and a Byzantine defeat at the [Battle of Bapheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bapheus "Battle of Bapheus") in 1302 contributed to his rise. It is not well understood how the early Ottomans came to dominate their neighbours, due to a lack of sources. The [Ghaza thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaza_thesis "Ghaza thesis") popular during the 20th century credited their success to rallying religious warriors, but the idea is no longer generally accepted, and no other hypothesis has attracted broad support.

In the century after Osman I, Ottoman rule began to extend over Anatolia and the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Balkans "History of the Balkans"). The [Byzantine–Ottoman wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars "Byzantine–Ottoman wars"), waged in Anatolia in the late 13th century before entering Europe in the mid-14th century, were followed by the [Bulgarian–Ottoman wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars "Bulgarian–Ottoman wars") and the [Serbian–Ottoman wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars "Serbian–Ottoman wars") in the mid-14th century. Osman's son, [Orhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orhan "Orhan"), captured the northwestern Anatolian city of [Bursa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bursa "Bursa") in 1326 and made it the new capital, supplanting Byzantine control in the region. The important port of [Thessaloniki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaloniki "Thessaloniki") was captured from the [Venetians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice "Republic of Venice") in 1387 and sacked. The Ottoman victory in [Kosovo in 1389](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kosovo "Battle of Kosovo") effectively marked the [end of Serbian power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Serbian_Empire "Fall of the Serbian Empire") in the region, paving the way for Ottoman expansion into Europe. The [Battle of Nicopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nicopolis "Battle of Nicopolis") for the [Bulgarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Bulgarian_Empire "Second Bulgarian Empire") [Tsardom of Vidin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsardom_of_Vidin "Tsardom of Vidin") in 1396, regarded as the last large-scale [crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades") of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), also failed to stop the Ottomans.

As the Turks expanded into the Balkans, the [conquest of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sieges_of_Constantinople#Ottoman_Sieges "Sieges of Constantinople") became a crucial objective. The Ottomans already controlled nearly all former Byzantine lands surrounding the city, but Constantinople's defenses and strategic position on the [Bosporus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosporus "Bosporus") Strait made it difficult to conquer. In 1402, the Byzantines were temporarily relieved when the [Turco-Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turco-Mongol_tradition "Turco-Mongol tradition") leader [Timur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur "Timur"), founder of the [Timurid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_Empire "Timurid Empire"), invaded Ottoman Anatolia from the east. In the [Battle of Ankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ankara "Battle of Ankara") in 1402, Timur defeated Ottoman forces and took Sultan [Bayezid I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayezid_I "Bayezid I") as prisoner, throwing the empire into disorder. The [ensuing civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Interregnum "Ottoman Interregnum") lasted from 1402 to 1413 as Bayezid's sons fought over succession. It ended when [Mehmed I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_I "Mehmed I") emerged as the sultan.

The Balkan territories lost by the Ottomans after 1402, including Thessaloniki, Macedonia, and Kosovo, were recovered by [Murad II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murad_II "Murad II") between the 1430s and 1450s. On 10 November 1444, Murad repelled the [Crusade of Varna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusade_of_Varna "Crusade of Varna") by defeating the Hungarian, Polish, and [Wallachian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallachia "Wallachia") armies under [Władysław III of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_III_of_Poland "Władysław III of Poland") and [John Hunyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hunyadi "John Hunyadi") at the [Battle of Varna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Varna "Battle of Varna"), although Albanians under [Skanderbeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skanderbeg "Skanderbeg") continued to resist. Four years later, John Hunyadi prepared another army of Hungarian and Wallachian forces to attack the Turks, but was again defeated at the [Second Battle of Kosovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kosovo_(1448) "Battle of Kosovo (1448)") in 1448.

In modern historiography, there is a direct connection between the rapid Ottoman advances and the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death") from the mid-14th century onwards. Byzantine territories were exhausted demographically and militarily due to the plague, and this facilitated Ottoman expansion. In addition, slave hunting was a main economic motivation for the Ottomans. Some 21st-century authors re-periodise conquest of the Balkans into the *akıncı phase*, which spanned 8 to 13 decades, characterised by continuous slave hunting and destruction, followed by administrative integration into the Empire.

### Expansion and peak (1453–1566)

The son of Murad II, [Mehmed the Conqueror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_the_Conqueror "Mehmed the Conqueror"), reorganised both state and military, and on 29 May 1453 conquered [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople"), ending the Byzantine Empire. Mehmed allowed the [Eastern Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") to maintain its autonomy and land in exchange for accepting Ottoman authority. Due to tension between the states of western Europe and the later Byzantine Empire, most of the Orthodox population accepted Ottoman rule, as preferable to Venetian rule. [Albanian resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_uprisings_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Albanian uprisings in the Ottoman Empire") was a major obstacle to Ottoman expansion on the Italian peninsula.

In the 15th and 16th centuries, the Ottoman Empire entered a [period of expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Growth of the Ottoman Empire"). The Empire prospered under the rule of a line of committed and effective [Sultans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Dynasty "Ottoman Dynasty"). It flourished economically due to its control of the major overland trade routes between Europe and Asia.

Sultan [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I "Selim I") (1512–1520) dramatically expanded the eastern and southern frontiers by defeating [Shah Ismail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismail_I "Ismail I") of [Safavid Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran"), in the [Battle of Chaldiran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chaldiran "Battle of Chaldiran"). Selim I established [Ottoman rule in Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt_Eyalet "Egypt Eyalet") by defeating and annexing the [Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate "Mamluk Sultanate") and created a naval presence on the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea "Red Sea"). After this Ottoman expansion, competition began between the [Portuguese Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire "Portuguese Empire") and the Ottomans to become the dominant power in the region.

[Suleiman the Magnificent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent "Suleiman the Magnificent") (1520–1566) captured [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrade "Belgrade") in 1521, conquered the southern and central parts of the [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary "Kingdom of Hungary") as part of the [Ottoman–Hungarian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Hungarian_Wars "Ottoman–Hungarian Wars"), and, after his historic victory in the [Battle of Mohács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Moh%C3%A1cs "Battle of Mohács") in 1526, he established Ottoman rule in the territory of present-day Hungary and other Central European territories. He then laid [siege to Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Vienna_(1529) "Siege of Vienna (1529)") in 1529, but failed to take the city. In 1532, he made another [attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars_in_Hungary_(1526%E2%80%931568) "Habsburg–Ottoman wars in Hungary (1526–1568)") on Vienna, but was repulsed in the [siege of Güns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_G%C3%BCns "Siege of Güns"). [Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transylvania "Transylvania"), Wallachia and, intermittently, [Moldavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldavia "Moldavia"), became tributary principalities of the Empire. In the east, the Ottoman Turks took [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad") from the Persians in 1535, establishing [Ottoman rule in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Iraq "Ottoman Iraq") and gaining naval access to the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf"). After the end of the [Ottoman–Safavid War (1532–1555)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Safavid_War_(1532%E2%80%931555) "Ottoman–Safavid War (1532–1555)"), the [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus "Caucasus") became partitioned for the first time between the Safavids and the Ottomans, a *[status quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo "Status quo")* that persisted until the [Russian conquest of the Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_the_Caucasus "Russian conquest of the Caucasus") in the 19th century. By this partitioning as signed in the [Peace of Amasya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Amasya "Peace of Amasya"), [Western Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Armenia "Western Armenia"), western [Kurdistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdistan "Kurdistan"), and [Western Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Imereti "Kingdom of Imereti") fell into Ottoman hands, while southern [Dagestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagestan "Dagestan"), [Eastern Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Armenia "Eastern Armenia"), [Eastern Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Georgia_(country) "Eastern Georgia (country)"), and [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan "Azerbaijan") remained Persian.

In 1539, a 60,000-strong Ottoman army besieged the [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_Spain "Habsburg Spain") garrison of [Castelnuovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Castelnuovo "Siege of Castelnuovo") on the [Adriatic coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriatic_coast "Adriatic coast"); the successful siege cost the Ottomans 8,000 casualties, but [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice "Republic of Venice") agreed to terms in 1540, surrendering most of its empire in the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea "Aegean Sea") and the [Morea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morea "Morea"). [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Modern_France "Early Modern France") and the Ottoman Empire, united by mutual opposition to [Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg "Habsburg") rule, became allies. The French conquests of [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Nice "Siege of Nice") (1543) and [Corsica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Corsica_(1553) "Invasion of Corsica (1553)") (1553) occurred as a joint venture between French king [Francis I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_I_of_France "Francis I of France") and Suleiman, and were commanded by the Ottoman admirals [Hayreddin Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayreddin_Barbarossa "Hayreddin Barbarossa") and [Dragut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragut "Dragut"). France supported the Ottomans with an artillery unit during the 1543 Ottoman [conquest of Esztergom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Esztergom_(1543) "Siege of Esztergom (1543)") in northern Hungary. After further advances by the Turks, the Habsburg ruler [Ferdinand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor "Ferdinand I, Holy Roman Emperor") officially recognised Ottoman ascendancy in Hungary in 1547. Suleiman died of natural causes during the [siege of Szigetvár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Szigetv%C3%A1r "Siege of Szigetvár") in 1566. Following his death, the Ottomans were said to be declining, although this has been rejected by many scholars. By the end of Suleiman's reign, the Empire spanned approximately 877,888 mi2 (2,273,720 km2), extending over three continents.

The Empire became a dominant naval force, controlling much of the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea"). The Empire was now a major part of European politics. The Ottomans became involved in multi-continental religious wars when Spain and Portugal were united under the [Iberian Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Union "Iberian Union"). The Ottomans were holders of the Caliph title, meaning they were the leaders of Muslims worldwide. The Iberians were leaders of the Christian crusaders, and so the two fought in a worldwide conflict. There were zones of operations in the Mediterranean and [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean"), where Iberians circumnavigated Africa to reach India and, on their way, wage war upon the Ottomans and their local Muslim allies. Likewise, the Iberians passed through newly-Christianised [Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America "Latin America") and [had sent expeditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_de_Salcedo "Juan de Salcedo") that traversed the Pacific to Christianise the formerly Muslim Philippines and use it as a base to attack the Muslims in the [Far East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_East "Far East"). In this case, the Ottomans [sent armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_expeditions_to_Aceh "Ottoman expeditions to Aceh") to aid its easternmost vassal and territory, the [Sultanate of Aceh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Aceh "Sultanate of Aceh") in Southeast Asia.

The [Spanish–Ottoman wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars "Spanish–Ottoman wars") took place primarily in the Mediterranean. [Barbary corsairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_corsairs "Barbary corsairs") from the North African cities of Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, which were nominally part of the Ottoman Empire, captured thousands of merchant ships and raided coastal towns in Europe, [enslaving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_slave_trade "Barbary slave trade") the people they captured.

During the 1600s, the world conflict between the Ottoman Caliphate and Iberian Union was a stalemate since [both were at similar population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_population_in_1600 "List of countries by population in 1600"), technology and economic levels. Nevertheless, the success of the Ottoman political and military establishment was compared to the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"), despite the difference in size, by the likes of contemporary Italian scholar [Francesco Sansovino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Sansovino "Francesco Sansovino") and French political philosopher [Jean Bodin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bodin "Jean Bodin").

### Stagnation and reform (1566–1827)

#### Revolts, reversals, and revivals (1566–1683)

In the second half of the 16th century, the Ottoman Empire came under increasing strain from inflation and the rapidly rising costs of warfare that were impacting both Europe and the Middle East. These pressures led to a series of crises around the year 1600, placing great strain upon the Ottoman system of government. The empire underwent a series of transformations of its political and military institutions in response to these challenges, enabling it to successfully adapt to the new conditions of the 17th century and remain powerful, both militarily and economically. Historians of the mid-20th century once characterised this period as one of stagnation and decline, but this view is now rejected by the majority of academics.

The discovery of new maritime trade routes by Western European states allowed them to avoid the Ottoman trade monopoly. The [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Portugal "Kingdom of Portugal") discovery of the [Cape of Good Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_of_Good_Hope "Cape of Good Hope") in 1488 initiated [a series of Ottoman-Portuguese naval wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_naval_expeditions_in_the_Indian_Ocean "Ottoman naval expeditions in the Indian Ocean") in the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean") throughout the 16th century. Despite the growing European presence in the Indian Ocean, Ottoman trade with the east continued to flourish. Cairo, in particular, benefitted from the rise of Yemeni coffee as a popular consumer commodity. As coffeehouses appeared in cities and towns across the empire, Cairo developed into a major centre for its trade, contributing to its continued prosperity throughout the seventeenth and much of the 18th century.

Under [Ivan IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_IV "Ivan IV") (1533–1584), the [Tsardom of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsardom_of_Russia "Tsardom of Russia") expanded into the Volga and Caspian regions at the expense of the [Tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatars "Tatars") [khanates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate "Khanate"). In 1571, the Crimean khan [Devlet I Giray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devlet_I_Giray "Devlet I Giray"), commanded by the Ottomans, [invaded Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Crimean_Wars "Russo-Crimean Wars") and [burned Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_of_Moscow_(1571) "Fire of Moscow (1571)"). The next year, the invasion was repeated but repelled at the [Battle of Molodi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Molodi "Battle of Molodi"). The Ottoman Empire and its vassal, the Crimean Khanate, continued to invade Eastern Europe in a series of [slave raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean%E2%80%93Nogai_slave_raids_in_Eastern_Europe "Crimean–Nogai slave raids in Eastern Europe"), and remained a significant power in Eastern Europe until the end of the 17th century. The Crimean cavalry became indispensable for the Ottomans' campaigns against Russia, [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish-Lithuanian_Commonwealth "Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth"), [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary_(1526%E2%80%931867) "Kingdom of Hungary (1526–1867)"), and [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran").

The Ottomans decided to conquer [Venetian Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Cyprus "Venetian Cyprus") and on 22 July 1570, Nicosia was besieged; 50,000 Christians died, and 180,000 were enslaved. On 15 September 1570, the Ottoman cavalry appeared before the last Venetian stronghold in Cyprus, Famagusta. The Venetian defenders held out for 11 months against a force that at its peak numbered 200,000 men with 145 cannons; 163,000 cannonballs struck the walls of Famagusta before it fell to the Ottomans in August 1571. The [Siege of Famagusta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Famagusta "Siege of Famagusta") claimed 50,000 Ottoman casualties. Meanwhile, the [Holy League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_League_(1571) "Holy League (1571)") consisting of mostly Spanish and Venetian fleets won a victory over the Ottoman fleet at the [Battle of Lepanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lepanto "Battle of Lepanto") (1571), off southwestern Greece; Catholic forces killed over 30,000 Turks and destroyed 200 of their ships. It was a startling, if mostly symbolic, blow to the image of Ottoman invincibility, an image which the victory of the [Knights of Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Hospitaller "Knights Hospitaller") over the Ottoman invaders in the 1565 [siege of Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Siege_of_Malta "Great Siege of Malta") had recently set about eroding. The battle was far more damaging to the Ottoman navy in sapping experienced manpower than the loss of ships, which were rapidly replaced. The Ottoman navy recovered quickly, persuading Venice to sign a peace treaty in 1573, allowing the Ottomans to expand and consolidate their position in North Africa.

By contrast, the [Habsburg frontier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Habsburg_wars "Ottoman–Habsburg wars") had settled somewhat, a stalemate caused by a stiffening of the [Habsburg defences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Frontier "Military Frontier"). The [Long Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Turkish_War "Long Turkish War") against Habsburg Austria (1593–1606) created the need for greater numbers of Ottoman infantry equipped with firearms, resulting in a relaxation of recruitment policy. This contributed to problems of indiscipline and outright rebelliousness within the corps, which were never fully solved.[*[obsolete source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AGE_MATTERS "Wikipedia:AGE MATTERS")*] Irregular sharpshooters ([Sekban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sekban "Sekban")) were also recruited, and on demobilisation turned to [brigandage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigandage "Brigandage") in the [Celali rebellions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celali_rebellions "Celali rebellions") (1590–1610), which engendered widespread anarchy in [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") in the late 16th and early 17th centuries. With the Empire's population reaching 30 million people by 1600, the shortage of land placed further pressure on the government.[*[obsolete source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AGE_MATTERS "Wikipedia:AGE MATTERS")*] In spite of these problems, the Ottoman state remained strong, and its army did not collapse or suffer crushing defeats. The only exceptions were campaigns against the [Safavid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_dynasty "Safavid dynasty") of Persia, where many of the Ottoman eastern provinces were lost, some permanently. This [1603–1618 war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Safavid_War_(1603%E2%80%931618) "Ottoman–Safavid War (1603–1618)") eventually resulted in the [Treaty of Nasuh Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Nasuh_Pasha "Treaty of Nasuh Pasha"), which ceded the entire Caucasus, except westernmost Georgia, back into the possession of [Safavid Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran"). The treaty ending the [Cretan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_War_(1645%E2%80%931669) "Cretan War (1645–1669)") cost Venice much of [Dalmatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Dalmatia "Venetian Dalmatia"), its Aegean island possessions, and [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Candia "Kingdom of Candia").

During his brief majority reign, [Murad IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murad_IV "Murad IV") (1623–1640) reasserted central authority and [recaptured Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Safavid_War_(1623%E2%80%931639) "Ottoman–Safavid War (1623–1639)") (1639) from the Safavids. The resulting [Treaty of Zuhab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Zuhab "Treaty of Zuhab") of that same year decisively divided the Caucasus and adjacent regions between the two neighbouring empires as it had already been defined in the 1555 Peace of Amasya.

The [Sultanate of Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Women "Sultanate of Women") (1533–1656) was a period during which imperial women, especially the consorts and mothers of sultans, exercised significant political influence in the Ottoman Empire. The most prominent women of this period were [Hürrem Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCrrem_Sultan "Hürrem Sultan"), [Kösem Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6sem_Sultan "Kösem Sultan") and her daughter-in-law [Turhan Hatice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turhan_Hatice "Turhan Hatice"), whose political rivalry culminated in Kösem's murder in 1651. During the [Köprülü era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6pr%C3%BCl%C3%BC_era "Köprülü era") (1656–1703), effective control of the Empire was exercised by a sequence of [grand viziers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_vizier "Grand vizier") from the Köprülü family. The Köprülü Vizierate saw renewed military success with authority restored in Transylvania, the conquest of [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete "Crete") completed in 1669, and [expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Ottoman_War_(1672%E2%80%931676) "Polish–Ottoman War (1672–1676)") into [Polish southern Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ukraine#Polish–Lithuanian_Commonwealth "History of Ukraine"), with the strongholds of [Khotyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khotyn "Khotyn"), and [Kamianets-Podilskyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamianets-Podilskyi "Kamianets-Podilskyi") and the territory of [Podolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podolia "Podolia") ceding to an Ottoman controlled [Podolia Eyalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podolia_Eyalet "Podolia Eyalet") in 1676.

This period of renewed assertiveness came to a calamitous end in 1683 when Grand Vizier [Kara Mustafa Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara_Mustafa_Pasha "Kara Mustafa Pasha") led a huge army to attempt a second Ottoman siege of [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Vienna "History of Vienna") in the [Great Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Turkish_War "Great Turkish War") of 1683–1699. The final assault being fatally delayed, the Ottoman forces were swept away by allied Habsburg, German, and Polish forces spearheaded by the Polish king [John III Sobieski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_III_Sobieski "John III Sobieski") at the [Battle of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna "Battle of Vienna"). The alliance of the [Holy League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_League_(1684) "Holy League (1684)") pressed home the advantage of the defeat at Vienna, culminating in the [Treaty of Karlowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Karlowitz "Treaty of Karlowitz") (26 January 1699), which ended the Great Turkish War. The Ottomans surrendered control of significant territories, many permanently. [Mustafa II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_II "Mustafa II") (1695–1703) led the counterattack of 1695–1696 against the Habsburgs in Hungary, but was undone at the disastrous defeat at [Zenta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Zenta "Battle of Zenta") (in modern Serbia), 11 September 1697.

#### Military defeats

Aside from the loss of the [Banat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banat "Banat") and the temporary loss of [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Belgrade "History of Belgrade") (1717–1739), the Ottoman border on the [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube "Danube") and [Sava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sava "Sava") remained stable during the 18th century. [Russian expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_of_Russia_1500%E2%80%931800 "Expansion of Russia 1500–1800"), however, presented a large and growing threat. Accordingly, King [Charles XII of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_XII_of_Sweden "Charles XII of Sweden") was welcomed as an ally in the Ottoman Empire following his defeat by the Russians at the [Battle of Poltava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Poltava "Battle of Poltava") of 1709 in central Ukraine (part of the [Great Northern War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Northern_War "Great Northern War") of 1700–1721). Charles XII persuaded the Ottoman Sultan [Ahmed III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_III "Ahmed III") to declare war on Russia, which resulted in an Ottoman victory in the [Pruth River Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pruth_River_Campaign "Pruth River Campaign") of 1710–1711, in Moldavia.

After the [Austro-Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Turkish_War_(1716%E2%80%931718) "Austro-Turkish War (1716–1718)"), the [Treaty of Passarowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Passarowitz "Treaty of Passarowitz") confirmed the loss of the Banat, Serbia, and "Little Walachia" ([Oltenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oltenia "Oltenia")) to Austria. The Treaty also revealed that the Ottoman Empire was on the defensive and unlikely to present any further aggression in Europe. The [Austro-Russian–Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Russian%E2%80%93Turkish_War_(1735%E2%80%931739) "Austro-Russian–Turkish War (1735–1739)") (1735–1739), which was ended by the [Treaty of Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Belgrade "Treaty of Belgrade") in 1739, resulted in the Ottoman recovery of northern [Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia "Bosnia"), [Habsburg Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Serbia_(1718%E2%80%931739) "Kingdom of Serbia (1718–1739)") (including Belgrade), Oltenia and the southern parts of the [Banat of Temeswar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banat_of_Temeswar "Banat of Temeswar"); but the Empire lost the port of [Azov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azov "Azov"), north of the Crimean Peninsula, to the Russians. After this treaty the Ottoman Empire was able to enjoy a generation of peace in Europe, as Austria and Russia were forced to deal with the rise of [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia "Prussia").

[Educational and technological reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_Technology_in_the_Ottoman_Empire#Education "Science and Technology in the Ottoman Empire") came about, including the establishment of higher education institutions such as the [Istanbul Technical University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Technical_University "Istanbul Technical University"). In 1734 an artillery school was established to impart Western-style artillery methods, but the Islamic clergy successfully objected under the grounds of [theodicy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodicy "Theodicy"). In 1754 the artillery school was reopened on a semi-secret basis. In 1726, [Ibrahim Muteferrika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_Muteferrika "Ibrahim Muteferrika") convinced the Grand Vizier [Nevşehirli Damat Ibrahim Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nev%C5%9Fehirli_Damat_Ibrahim_Pasha "Nevşehirli Damat Ibrahim Pasha"), the [Grand Mufti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mufti "Grand Mufti"), and the clergy on the efficiency of the printing press, and Muteferrika was later granted by Sultan Ahmed III permission to publish non-religious books (despite opposition from some [calligraphers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calligraphy "Islamic calligraphy") and religious leaders). Muteferrika's press published its first book in 1729 and, by 1743, issued 17 works in 23 volumes, each having between 500 and 1,000 copies.

In North Africa, Spain [conquered Oran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_Oran_(1732) "Spanish conquest of Oran (1732)") from the autonomous [Deylik of Algiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deylik_of_Algiers "Deylik of Algiers"). The [Bey of Oran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beylik_of_Oran "Beylik of Oran") received an army from Algiers, but it failed to recapture [Oran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oran "Oran"); the siege caused the deaths of 1,500 Spaniards, and even more Algerians. The Spanish also massacred many Muslim soldiers. In 1792, Spain abandoned Oran, selling it to the Deylik of Algiers.

In 1768 Russian-backed Ukrainian [Haidamakas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haidamaka "Haidamaka"), pursuing Polish confederates, entered [Balta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balta,_Ukraine "Balta, Ukraine"), an Ottoman-controlled town on the border of Bessarabia in Ukraine, massacred its citizens, and burned the town to the ground. This action provoked the Ottoman Empire into the [Russo-Turkish War of 1768–1774](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Turkish_War_(1768%E2%80%931774) "Russo-Turkish War (1768–1774)"). The [Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_K%C3%BC%C3%A7%C3%BCk_Kaynarca "Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca") of 1774 ended the war and provided freedom of worship for the Christian citizens of the Ottoman-controlled provinces of Wallachia and Moldavia. By the late 18th century, after a number of defeats in the wars with Russia, some people in the Ottoman Empire began to conclude that the reforms of [Peter the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_the_Great "Peter the Great") had given the Russians an edge, and the Ottomans would have to keep up with Western technology in order to avoid further defeats.

[Selim III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_III "Selim III") (1789–1807) made the first major attempts to [modernise the army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_reform_efforts "Ottoman military reform efforts"), but his reforms were hampered by the religious leadership and the [Janissary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary "Janissary") corps. Jealous of their privileges and firmly opposed to change, the Janissary [revolted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary_revolts "Janissary revolts"). Selim's efforts cost him his throne and his life, but were resolved in spectacular and bloody fashion by his successor, the dynamic [Mahmud II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_II "Mahmud II"), who [eliminated the Janissary corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auspicious_Incident "Auspicious Incident") in 1826.

The [Serbian revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_revolution "Serbian revolution") (1804–1815) marked the beginning of an era of [national awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_of_nationalism_under_the_Ottoman_Empire "Rise of nationalism under the Ottoman Empire") in the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") during the [Eastern Question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Question "Eastern Question"). In 1811, the fundamentalist Wahhabis of Arabia, led by the al-Saud family, revolted against the Ottomans. Unable to defeat the Wahhabi rebels, the Sublime Porte had [Muhammad Ali Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt "Muhammad Ali of Egypt") of [Kavala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kavala "Kavala"), the *[vali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wali "Wali")* (governor) of the [Eyalet of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt_Eyalet "Egypt Eyalet"), tasked with retaking Arabia, which ended with the destruction of the [Emirate of Diriyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Diriyah "Emirate of Diriyah") in 1818. The [suzerainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerainty "Suzerainty") of Serbia as a hereditary monarchy under its own [dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obrenovi%C4%87_dynasty "Obrenović dynasty") was acknowledged *[de jure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_jure "De jure")* in 1830. In 1821, the [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Greece "Ottoman Greece") [declared war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence "Greek War of Independence") on the Sultan. A rebellion that originated in Moldavia as a diversion was followed by the main revolution in the [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese "Peloponnese"), which, along with the northern part of the [Gulf of Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Corinth "Gulf of Corinth"), became the first parts of the Ottoman Empire to achieve independence (in 1829). In 1830, the French invaded the [Deylik of Algiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deylik_of_Algiers "Deylik of Algiers"). [The campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Algiers_in_1830 "Invasion of Algiers in 1830") took 21 days, and resulted in over 5,000 Algerian military casualties, and about 2,600 French ones. Before the French invasion the total population of Algeria was most likely between 3,000,000 and 5,000,000. By 1873, the population of Algeria (excluding several hundred thousand newly arrived French settlers) had decreased to 2,172,000. In 1831, Muhammad Ali of Egypt revolted against Sultan [Mahmud II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_II "Mahmud II") due to the latter's refusal to grant him the governorships of [Greater Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_(region) "Syria (region)") and [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete "Crete"), which the Sultan had promised him in exchange for sending military assistance to put down the [Greek revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Revolt "Greek Revolt") (1821–1829) that ultimately ended with the formal [independence of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Protocol_(1830) "London Protocol (1830)") in 1830. It was a costly enterprise for Muhammad Ali, who had lost his fleet at the [Battle of Navarino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Navarino "Battle of Navarino") in 1827. Thus began the first [Egyptian–Ottoman War (1831–1833)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian%E2%80%93Ottoman_War_(1831%E2%80%931833) "Egyptian–Ottoman War (1831–1833)"), during which the French-trained army of Muhammad Ali, under the command of his son [Ibrahim Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_Pasha_of_Egypt "Ibrahim Pasha of Egypt"), defeated the Ottoman Army as it marched into [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia"), reaching the city of [Kütahya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%BCtahya "Kütahya") within 320 km (200 mi) of the capital, Constantinople. In desperation, Sultan Mahmud II appealed to the empire's traditional arch-rival Russia for help, asking Emperor [Nicholas I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_I_of_Russia "Nicholas I of Russia") to send an expeditionary force to assist him. In return for signing the [Treaty of Hünkâr İskelesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_H%C3%BCnk%C3%A2r_%C4%B0skelesi "Treaty of Hünkâr İskelesi"), the Russians sent the expeditionary force which deterred Ibrahim Pasha from marching any further towards Constantinople. Under the terms of the [Convention of Kütahya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_of_K%C3%BCtahya "Convention of Kütahya"), signed on 5 May 1833, Muhammad Ali agreed to abandon his campaign against the Sultan, in exchange for which he was made the *vali* (governor) of the *[vilayets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilayet "Vilayet")* (provinces) of Crete, [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo "Aleppo"), [Tripoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripoli,_Lebanon "Tripoli, Lebanon"), [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus "Damascus") and [Sidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidon "Sidon") (the latter four comprising modern [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria") and [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon "Lebanon")), and given the right to collect taxes in [Adana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adana "Adana"). Had it not been for the Russian intervention, Sultan Mahmud II could have faced the risk of being overthrown and Muhammad Ali could have even become the new Sultan. These events marked the beginning of a recurring pattern where the Sublime Porte needed the help of foreign powers to protect itself.

In 1839, the [Sublime Porte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublime_Porte "Sublime Porte") attempted to take back what it lost to the *[de facto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_facto "De facto")* autonomous, but *[de jure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_jure "De jure")* still Ottoman [Eyalet of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt_Eyalet "Egypt Eyalet"), but its forces were initially defeated, which led to the [Oriental Crisis of 1840](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Crisis_of_1840 "Oriental Crisis of 1840"). Muhammad Ali had close relations with [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/July_Monarchy "July Monarchy"), and the prospect of him becoming the Sultan of Egypt was widely viewed as putting the entire [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") into the French sphere of influence. As the Sublime Porte had proved itself incapable of defeating Muhammad Ali, the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire") and [Austrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Empire "Austrian Empire") provided military assistance, and the second [Egyptian–Ottoman War (1839–1841)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian%E2%80%93Ottoman_War_(1839%E2%80%931841) "Egyptian–Ottoman War (1839–1841)") ended with Ottoman victory and the restoration of Ottoman suzerainty over Egypt Eyalet and the Levant.

By the mid-19th century, the Ottoman Empire was called the "[sick man of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sick_man_of_Europe "Sick man of Europe")". Three suzerain states – the [Principality of Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Serbia "Principality of Serbia"), [Wallachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallachia "Wallachia") and [Moldavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldavia "Moldavia") – moved towards *de jure* independence during the 1860s and 1870s.

### Decline and modernisation (1828–1908)

During the [Tanzimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanzimat "Tanzimat") period (1839–1876), the government's series of constitutional reforms led to a fairly modern [conscripted army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Conscription in the Ottoman Empire"), banking system reforms, the decriminalisation of homosexuality, the replacement of religious law with secular law, and guilds with modern factories. The Ottoman Ministry of Post was established in Istanbul in 1840. American inventor [Samuel Morse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Morse "Samuel Morse") received an Ottoman patent for the telegraph in 1847, issued by Sultan [Abdülmecid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd%C3%BClmecid_I "Abdülmecid I"), who personally tested the invention. The reformist period peaked with the Constitution, called the *[Kanûn-u Esâsî](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kan%C3%BBn-u_Es%C3%A2s%C3%AE "Kanûn-u Esâsî")*. The empire's [First Constitutional era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Constitutional_Era_(Ottoman_Empire) "First Constitutional Era (Ottoman Empire)") was short-lived. The parliament survived for only two years before the sultan suspended it.

The empire's Christian population, owing to their higher educational levels, started to pull ahead of the Muslim majority, leading to much resentment. In 1861, there were 571 primary and 94 secondary schools for Ottoman Christians, with 140,000 pupils in total, a figure that vastly exceeded the number of Muslim children in school at the time, who were further hindered by the amount of time spent learning Arabic and Islamic theology. Author Norman Stone suggests that the Arabic alphabet, in which Turkish was written [until 1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_alphabet_reform "Turkish alphabet reform"), was ill-suited to reflect the sounds of Turkish (which is a Turkic as opposed to Semitic language), which imposed further difficulty on Turkish children. In turn, Christians' higher educational levels allowed them to play a larger role in the economy, with the rise in prominence of groups such as the [Sursock family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sursock_family "Sursock family") indicative of this. In 1911, of the 654 wholesale companies in Istanbul, 528 were owned by ethnic Greeks. In many cases, Christians and Jews gained protection from European consuls and citizenship, meaning they were protected from Ottoman law and not subject to the same economic regulations as their Muslim counterparts.

The [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War "Crimean War") (1853–1856) was part of a long-running contest between the major European powers for influence over territories of the declining Ottoman Empire. The financial burden of the war led the Ottoman state to issue [foreign loans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_public_debt "Ottoman public debt") amounting to 5 million pounds sterling on 4 August 1854. The war caused an exodus of the [Crimean Tatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Tatars "Crimean Tatars"), about 200,000 of whom moved to the Ottoman Empire in continuing waves of emigration. Toward the end of the [Caucasian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasian_War "Caucasian War"), 90% of the [Circassians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassians "Circassians") were [ethnically cleansed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_cleansing_of_Circassians "Ethnic cleansing of Circassians") and exiled from their homelands in the Caucasus, fleeing to the Ottoman Empire, resulting in the settlement of 500,000 to 700,000 Circassians in the Ottoman Empire. Crimean Tatar refugees in the late 19th century played an especially notable role in seeking to modernise Ottoman education and in first promoting both [Pan-Turkism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Turkism "Pan-Turkism") and a sense of Turkish nationalism.

In this period, the Ottoman Empire spent only small amounts of public funds on education; for example, in 1860–1861 only 0.2% of the total budget was invested in education. As the Ottoman state attempted to modernise its infrastructure and army in response to outside threats, it opened itself up to a different kind of threat: that of creditors. As the historian Eugene Rogan has written, "the single greatest threat to the independence of the Middle East" in the 19th century "was not the armies of Europe but its banks". The Ottoman state, which had begun taking on debt with the Crimean War, was forced to declare bankruptcy in 1875. By 1881, the Ottoman Empire agreed to have its debt controlled by the [Ottoman Public Debt Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Public_Debt_Administration "Ottoman Public Debt Administration"), a council of European men with presidency alternating between France and Britain. The body controlled swaths of the Ottoman economy, and used its position to ensure that European capital continued to penetrate the empire, often to the detriment of local Ottoman interests.

The Ottoman [bashi-bazouks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashi-bazouk "Bashi-bazouk") suppressed the [Bulgarian uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Uprising "April Uprising") of 1876, massacring up to 100,000 people in the process. The [Russo-Turkish War (1877–1878)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Turkish_War_(1877%E2%80%931878) "Russo-Turkish War (1877–1878)") ended with a decisive victory for Russia. As a result, Ottoman holdings in Europe declined sharply: [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Bulgaria "Principality of Bulgaria") was established as an independent principality inside the Ottoman Empire; [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Principalities "United Principalities") achieved full independence; and [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Serbia "History of Serbia") and [Montenegro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegro "Montenegro") finally gained complete independence, but with smaller territories. In 1878, [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary "Austria-Hungary") unilaterally occupied the Ottoman provinces of [Bosnia-Herzegovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_Vilayet "Bosnia Vilayet") and [Novi Pazar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjak_of_Novi_Pazar "Sanjak of Novi Pazar").

British Prime Minister [Benjamin Disraeli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Disraeli "Benjamin Disraeli") advocated restoring the Ottoman territories on the Balkan Peninsula during the [Congress of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Berlin "Congress of Berlin"), and in return, Britain assumed the administration of [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") in 1878. Britain later sent troops to [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") in 1882 to put down the [Urabi Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urabi_Revolt "Urabi Revolt") (Sultan [Abdul Hamid II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Hamid_II "Abdul Hamid II") was too paranoid to mobilise his own army, fearing this would result in a coup d'état), effectively gaining control in both territories. Abdul Hamid II was so fearful of a coup that he did not allow his army to conduct war games, lest this serve as cover for a coup, but he did see the need for military mobilisation. In 1883, a German military mission under General Baron [Colmar von der Goltz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colmar_Freiherr_von_der_Goltz "Colmar Freiherr von der Goltz") arrived to train the Ottoman Army, leading to the so-called "Goltz generation" of German-trained officers, who played a notable role in the politics of the empire's last years.

From 1894 to 1896, between 100,000 and 300,000 Armenians living throughout the empire were killed in what became known as the [Hamidian massacres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamidian_massacres "Hamidian massacres").

In 1897 the population was 19 million, of whom 14 million (74%) were Muslim. An additional 20 million lived in provinces that remained under the sultan's nominal suzerainty but were entirely outside his actual power. One by one the Porte lost nominal authority. They included Egypt, Tunisia, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Bosnia-Herzegovina, and Lebanon.

As the Ottoman Empire gradually shrank, 7–9 million Muslims from its former territories in the Caucasus, [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimea "Crimea"), Balkans, and the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean "Mediterranean") islands migrated to Anatolia and [Eastern Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Thrace "Eastern Thrace"). After the Empire lost the [First Balkan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Balkan_War "First Balkan War") (1912–1913), it lost all its [Balkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_peninsula "Balkan peninsula") territories except [East Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Thrace "East Thrace") (European Turkey). This resulted in around 400,000 Muslims fleeing with the retreating Ottoman armies (with many dying from [cholera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholera "Cholera") brought by the soldiers), and 400,000 non-Muslims fled territory still under Ottoman rule. [Justin McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_McCarthy_(American_historian) "Justin McCarthy (American historian)") estimates that from 1821 to 1922, 5.5 million Muslims died in southeastern Europe, with the expulsion of 5 million. In addition the Ottoman state encouraged transnational migration, including Circassians, Bosnians, and Russian Jews resettled in the [southern Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Levant "Southern Levant"). These communities, alongside internal Arab resettlement, reshaped rural society and land use in ways that reflected broader imperial patterns of demographic engineering and agrarian expansion.

### Defeat and dissolution (1908–1922)

#### Young Turk movement

The defeat and [dissolution of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Dissolution of the Ottoman Empire") (1908–1922) began with the [Second Constitutional Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Constitutional_Era "Second Constitutional Era"), a moment of hope and promise established with the [Young Turk Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Turk_Revolution "Young Turk Revolution"). It restored the [Constitution of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Constitution of the Ottoman Empire") and brought in [multi-party politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "List of political parties in the Ottoman Empire") with a [two-stage electoral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Elections in the Ottoman Empire") ([electoral law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_electoral_law "Ottoman electoral law")) under the [Ottoman parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Assembly_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "General Assembly of the Ottoman Empire"). The constitution offered hope by freeing the empire's citizens to modernise the state's institutions, rejuvenate its strength, and enable it to hold its own against outside powers. Its guarantee of liberties promised to dissolve inter-communal tensions and transform the empire into a more harmonious place. Instead, this period became the story of the twilight struggle of the Empire.

Members of [Young Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Turks "Young Turks") movement who had once gone underground now established their parties. Among them the [Committee of Union and Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_Union_and_Progress "Committee of Union and Progress") and the [Freedom and Accord Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_and_Accord_Party "Freedom and Accord Party") were major parties. On the other end of the spectrum were ethnic parties, which included [Poale Zion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Social_Democratic_Labour_Party_in_Palestine_(Poale_Zion) "Jewish Social Democratic Labour Party in Palestine (Poale Zion)"), [Al-Fatat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fatat "Al-Fatat"), and [Armenian national movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_national_movement "Armenian national movement") organised under [Armenian Revolutionary Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Revolutionary_Federation "Armenian Revolutionary Federation"). Profiting from the civil strife, Austria-Hungary officially annexed [Bosnia and Herzegovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Bosnia and Herzegovina") in 1908. The last of the [Ottoman censuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Census in the Ottoman Empire") was performed in [1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1914_population_statistics_for_the_Ottoman_Empire "1914 population statistics for the Ottoman Empire"). Despite [military reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_reforms "Ottoman military reforms") which reconstituted the [Ottoman Modern Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Modern_Army "Ottoman Modern Army"), the Empire lost its North African territories and the Dodecanese in the [Italo-Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italo-Turkish_War "Italo-Turkish War") (1911) and almost all of its European territories in the [Balkan Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_Wars "Balkan Wars") (1912–1913). The Empire faced continuous unrest in the years leading up to [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), including the [31 March Incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/31_March_Incident "31 March Incident") and two further coups in [1912](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1912_Ottoman_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat "1912 Ottoman coup d'état") and [1913](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913_Ottoman_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat "1913 Ottoman coup d'état").

#### World War I

The Ottoman Empire entered [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") on the side of the [Central Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Powers "Central Powers") and was ultimately defeated. The Ottoman participation in the war began with the combined [German-Ottoman surprise attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea_Raid "Black Sea Raid") on the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea") coast of the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire "Russian Empire") on 29 October 1914. Following the attack, the Russian Empire (2 November 1914) and its allies [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Third_Republic "French Third Republic") (5 November 1914) and the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire") (5 November 1914) declared war on the Ottoman Empire. Also on 5 November 1914, the British government changed the status of the [Khedivate of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khedivate_of_Egypt "Khedivate of Egypt") and [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus_Convention "Cyprus Convention"), which were [de jure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_jure "De jure") Ottoman territories prior to the war, to [British protectorates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_protectorate "British protectorate").

The Ottomans successfully defended the [Dardanelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles "Dardanelles") strait during the [Gallipoli campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallipoli_campaign "Gallipoli campaign") (1915–1916) and achieved initial victories against British forces in the first two years of the [Mesopotamian campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian_campaign "Mesopotamian campaign"), such as the [Siege of Kut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Kut "Siege of Kut") (1915–1916); but the [Arab Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Revolt "Arab Revolt") (1916–1918) turned the tide against the Ottomans in the Middle East. In the [Caucasus campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus_campaign "Caucasus campaign"), however, the Russian forces had the upper hand from the beginning, especially after the [Battle of Sarikamish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sarikamish "Battle of Sarikamish") (1914–1915). Russian forces advanced into northeastern [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") and controlled the major cities there until retreating from World War I with the [Treaty of Brest-Litovsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Brest-Litovsk "Treaty of Brest-Litovsk") following the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution "Russian Revolution") in 1917.

#### Genocides

In 1915 the Ottoman government and Kurdish tribes in the region started the extermination of its ethnic Armenian population, resulting in the deaths of up to 1.5 million Armenians in the [Armenian genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_genocide "Armenian genocide"). The genocide was carried out during and after World War I and implemented in two phases: the wholesale killing of the able-bodied male population through massacre and subjection of army conscripts to forced labour, followed by the deportation of women, children, the elderly and infirm on [death marches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_march "Death march") leading to the [Syrian desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_desert "Syrian desert"). Driven forward by military escorts, the deportees were deprived of food and water and subjected to periodic robbery, [rape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rape_during_the_Armenian_Genocide "Rape during the Armenian Genocide"), and systematic massacre. Large-scale massacres were also committed against the Empire's [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_genocide "Greek genocide") and [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_genocide "Assyrian genocide") minorities as part of the same campaign of ethnic cleansing.

#### Arab Revolt

The [Arab Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Revolt "Arab Revolt") began in 1916 with British support. It turned the tide against the Ottomans on the Middle Eastern front, where they seemed to have the upper hand during the first two years of the war. On the basis of the [McMahon–Hussein Correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McMahon%E2%80%93Hussein_Correspondence "McMahon–Hussein Correspondence"), an agreement between the British government and [Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hussein_bin_Ali,_Sharif_of_Mecca "Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca"), the revolt was officially initiated at Mecca on 10 June 1916. The Arab nationalist goal was to create a single unified and independent [Arab state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_state "Arab state") stretching from [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo "Aleppo"), Syria, to [Aden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aden "Aden"), Yemen, which the British promised to recognise.

The [Sharifian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharifian_Army "Sharifian Army"), led by Hussein and the [Hashemites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hashemites "Hashemites"), with military backing from the British [Egyptian Expeditionary Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Expeditionary_Force "Egyptian Expeditionary Force"), successfully fought and expelled the Ottoman military presence from much of the [Hejaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz "Hejaz") and [Transjordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transjordan_(region) "Transjordan (region)"). The rebellion eventually took [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus "Damascus") and set up a short-lived monarchy led by [Faisal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I_of_Iraq "Faisal I of Iraq"), a son of Hussein.

Following the terms of the 1916 [Sykes–Picot Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sykes%E2%80%93Picot_Agreement "Sykes–Picot Agreement"), the British and French later partitioned the Middle East into [mandate territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations_mandate "League of Nations mandate"). There was no unified Arab state, much to Arab nationalists' anger. Palestine, Iraq, Lebanon and Syria became British and French mandates.

#### Treaty of Sèvres and Turkish War of Independence

Defeated in World War I, the Ottoman Empire signed the [Armistice of Mudros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_of_Mudros "Armistice of Mudros") on 30 October 1918. [Istanbul was occupied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Istanbul "Occupation of Istanbul") by combined British, French, Italian, and Greek forces. In May 1919, Greece also [took control of the area around Smyrna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Smyrna "Occupation of Smyrna") (now İzmir).

The [partition of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Partition of the Ottoman Empire") was finalised under the terms of the 1920 [Treaty of Sèvres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_S%C3%A8vres "Treaty of Sèvres"). This treaty, as designed in the [Conference of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conference_of_London_(1920) "Conference of London (1920)"), allowed the Sultan to retain his position and title. Anatolia's status was problematic given the occupied forces.

A nationalist opposition arose in the [Turkish national movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_national_movement "Turkish national movement"). It won the [Turkish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_War_of_Independence "Turkish War of Independence") (1919–1923) under the leadership of [Mustafa Kemal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Kemal_Atat%C3%BCrk "Mustafa Kemal Atatürk") (later given the surname "Atatürk"). The sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922, and the last sultan, [Mehmed VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_VI "Mehmed VI") (reigned 1918–1922), left the country on 17 November 1922. The [Republic of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey") was [established](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Turkey#Republic_of_Turkey "History of Turkey") in its place on 29 October 1923, in the new capital city of [Ankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankara "Ankara"). The [caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Caliphate "Ottoman Caliphate") was abolished on 3 March 1924.

## Historiographical debate on the Ottoman state

Several historians, such as British historian [Edward Gibbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Gibbon "Edward Gibbon") and the Greek historian [Dimitri Kitsikis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitri_Kitsikis "Dimitri Kitsikis"), have argued that after the fall of Constantinople, the Ottoman state took over the machinery of the Byzantine (Roman) state and that the Ottoman Empire was in essence a continuation of the Byzantine Empire under a [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people "Turkish people") [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims") guise. The American historian [Speros Vryonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speros_Vryonis "Speros Vryonis") writes that the Ottoman state centred on "a Byzantine-Balkan base with a veneer of the Turkish language and the Islamic religion". Kitsikis and the American historian [Heath Lowry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath_Lowry "Heath Lowry") posit that the early Ottoman state was a predatory confederacy open to both Byzantine Christians and Turkish Muslims whose primary goal was attaining booty and slaves, rather than spreading Islam, and that Islam only later became the empire's primary characteristic. Other historians have followed the lead of the Austrian historian [Paul Wittek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Wittek "Paul Wittek"), who emphasises the early Ottoman state's Islamic character, seeing it as a "[jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihad "Jihad") state" dedicated to expanding the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world"). Many historians led in 1937 by the Turkish historian [Mehmet Fuat Köprülü](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmet_Fuat_K%C3%B6pr%C3%BCl%C3%BC "Mehmet Fuat Köprülü") championed the [Ghaza thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaza_thesis "Ghaza thesis"), according to which the early Ottoman state was a continuation of the way of life of the nomadic [Turkic tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_peoples "Turkic peoples") who had come from East Asia to Anatolia via Central Asia and the Middle East on a much larger scale. They argued that the most important cultural influences on the Ottoman state came from [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran").

The British historian [Norman Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Stone "Norman Stone") suggests many continuities between the Byzantine and Ottoman empires, such as that the *zeugarion* tax of Byzantium became the Ottoman *[Resm-i çift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resm-i_%C3%A7ift "Resm-i çift")* tax, that the *[pronoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronoia "Pronoia")* land-holding system that linked the amount of land one owned with one's ability to raise cavalry became the Ottoman *[timar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timar "Timar")* system, and that the Ottoman land measurement the *[dönüm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%B6n%C3%BCm "Dönüm")* was the same as the Byzantine *[stremma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stremma "Stremma")*. Stone also argues that although Sunni Islam was the state religion, the Ottoman state supported and controlled the [Eastern Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church"), which in return for accepting that control became the Ottoman Empire's largest land-holder. Despite the similarities, Stone argues that a crucial difference is that the land grants under the *timar* system were not hereditary at first. Even after they became inheritable, land ownership in the Ottoman Empire remained highly insecure, and the sultan revoked land grants whenever he wished. Stone argued this insecurity in land tenure strongly discouraged *[Timariots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timariot "Timariot")* from seeking long-term development of their land, and instead led them to adopt a strategy of short-term exploitation, which had deleterious effects on the Ottoman economy.

## Government

Before the reforms of the 19th and 20th centuries, the [state organisation of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_organisation_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "State organisation of the Ottoman Empire") was a system with two main dimensions, the military administration and the civil administration. The Sultan was in the highest position in the system. The civil system was based on local administrative units based on the region's characteristics. The state had control over the clergy. Certain pre-Islamic Turkish traditions that had survived the adoption of administrative and legal practices from Islamic [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran") remained important in Ottoman administrative circles. According to Ottoman understanding, the state's primary responsibility was to defend and extend the land of the Muslims and to ensure security and harmony within its borders in the overarching context of [orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni "Sunni") Islamic practice and dynastic sovereignty.

The Ottoman Empire, or as a dynastic institution, the House of Osman, was unprecedented and unequalled in the Islamic world for its size and duration. In Europe, only the [House of Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Habsburg "House of Habsburg") had a similarly unbroken line of sovereigns (kings/emperors) from the same family who ruled for so long, and during the same period, between the late 13th and early 20th centuries. The Ottoman dynasty was Turkish in origin. On eleven occasions, the sultan was deposed (replaced by another sultan of the Ottoman dynasty, who were either the former sultan's brother, son or nephew) because he was perceived by his enemies as a threat to the state. There were only two attempts in Ottoman history to unseat the ruling Ottoman dynasty, both failures, which suggests a political system that for an extended period was able to manage its revolutions without unnecessary instability. As such, the last Ottoman sultan Mehmed VI (r. 1918–1922) was a [direct patrilineal (male-line) descendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sultans_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "List of sultans of the Ottoman Empire") of the first Ottoman sultan [Osman I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osman_I "Osman I") (d. 1323/4), which was unparalleled in both Europe (e.g., the male line of the House of Habsburg became extinct in 1740) and in the Islamic world. The primary purpose of the [Imperial Harem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Harem "Imperial Harem") was to ensure the birth of male heirs to the Ottoman throne and secure the continuation of the direct patrilineal (male-line) power of the Ottoman sultans in the future generations.

The highest position in Islam, [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate"), was claimed by the sultans starting with [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I "Selim I"), which was established as the Ottoman Caliphate. The Ottoman sultan, *[pâdişâh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padishah "Padishah")* or "lord of kings", served as the Empire's sole regent and was considered to be the embodiment of its government, though he did not always exercise complete control. The Imperial Harem was one of the most important powers of the Ottoman court. It was ruled by the [valide sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valide_sultan "Valide sultan"). On occasion, the valide sultan became involved in state politics. For a time, the women of the Harem effectively controlled the state in what was termed the "[Sultanate of Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_the_women "Sultanate of the women")". New sultans were always chosen from the sons of the previous sultan.[*[dubious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Accuracy_dispute#Disputed_statement "Wikipedia:Accuracy dispute") – [discuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Ottoman_Empire#Dubious "Talk:Ottoman Empire")*] The strong educational system of the [palace school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_school "Palace school") was geared towards eliminating the unfit potential heirs and establishing support among the ruling elite for a successor. The palace schools, which also educated the future administrators of the state, were not a single track. First, the [Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa "Madrasa") (*Medrese*) was designated for the Muslims, and educated scholars and state officials according to Islamic tradition. The financial burden of the Medrese was supported by *[vakif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waqf "Waqf")*s, allowing children of poor families to move to higher social levels and income. The second track was a free [boarding school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boarding_school "Boarding school") for the Christians, the *[Enderûn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ender%C3%BBn "Enderûn")*, which recruited 3,000 students annually from Christian boys between eight and twenty years old from one in forty families among the communities settled in [Rumelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumelia "Rumelia") or the Balkans, a process known as [Devshirme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devshirme_in_the_Ottoman_Palace_School "Devshirme in the Ottoman Palace School") (*Devşirme*). The Devshirme falls within modern definitions of [genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genocide "Genocide").

Though the sultan was the supreme monarch, the sultan's political and executive authority was delegated. The politics of the state had a number of advisors and ministers gathered around a council known as [Divan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divan "Divan"). The Divan, in the years when the Ottoman state was still a *[Beylik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bey "Bey")*, was composed of the elders of the tribe. Its composition was later modified to include military officers and local elites (such as religious and political advisors). Later still, beginning in 1320, a Grand Vizier was appointed to assume certain of the sultan's responsibilities. The Grand Vizier had considerable independence from the sultan with almost unlimited powers of appointment, dismissal, and supervision. Beginning with the late 16th century, sultans withdrew from politics and the Grand Vizier became the *de facto* head of state.

Throughout Ottoman history, there were many instances in which local governors acted independently, and even in opposition to the ruler. After the Young Turk Revolution of 1908, the Ottoman state became a constitutional monarchy. The sultan no longer had executive powers. A parliament was formed, with representatives chosen from the provinces. The representatives formed the [Imperial Government of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Government_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Imperial Government of the Ottoman Empire").

This eclectic administration was apparent even in the diplomatic correspondence of the Empire, which was initially undertaken in the [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") to the west.

The [Tughra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tughra "Tughra") were calligraphic monograms, or signatures, of the Ottoman Sultans, of which there were 35. Carved on the Sultan's seal, they bore the names of the Sultan and his father. The statement and prayer, "ever victorious", was also present in most. The earliest belonged to Orhan Gazi. The ornately stylised *Tughra* spawned a branch of Ottoman-Turkish [calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligraphy "Calligraphy").

### Law

The Ottoman legal system accepted the [religious law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_law "Religious law") over its subjects. At the same time the *[Qanun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanun_(law) "Qanun (law)")* (or *Kanun*), dynastic law, co-existed with religious law or [Sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia"). The Ottoman Empire was always organised around a system of local [jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence "Jurisprudence"). Legal administration in the Ottoman Empire was part of a larger scheme of balancing central and local authority. Ottoman power revolved crucially around the administration of the rights to land, which gave a space for the local authority to develop the needs of the local [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire) "Millet (Ottoman Empire)"). The jurisdictional complexity of the Ottoman Empire was aimed to permit the integration of culturally and religiously different groups. The Ottoman system had three court systems: one for Muslims, one for non-Muslims, involving appointed Jews and Christians ruling over their respective religious communities, and the "trade court". The entire system was regulated from above by means of the administrative *Qanun*, i.e., laws, a system based upon the Turkic *[Yassa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yassa "Yassa")* and *[Töre](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=T%C3%B6re_(law)&action=edit&redlink=1 "Töre (law) (page does not exist)")*, which were developed in the pre-Islamic era.

These court categories were not, however, wholly exclusive; for instance, the Islamic courts, which were the Empire's primary courts, could also be used to settle a trade conflict or disputes between litigants of differing religions, and Jews and Christians often went to them to obtain a more forceful ruling on an issue. The Ottoman state tended not to interfere with non-Muslim religious law systems, despite legally having a voice to do so through local governors. The Islamic *Sharia* law system had been developed from a combination of the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur%27an "Qur'an"); the [Hadīth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith "Hadith"), or words of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_in_Islam "Muhammad in Islam"); *[ijmā'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijma "Ijma")*, or consensus of the members of the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah "Ummah"); [qiyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiyas "Qiyas"), a system of analogical reasoning from earlier precedents; and local customs. Both systems were taught at the Empire's law schools, which were in [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul "Istanbul") and [Bursa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bursa "Bursa").

The Ottoman Islamic legal system was set up differently from traditional European courts. Presiding over Islamic courts was a *Qadi*, or judge. Since the closing of the *[ijtihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijtihad "Ijtihad")*, or 'Gate of Interpretation', *Qadi*s throughout the Ottoman Empire focused less on legal precedent, and more with local customs and traditions in the areas that they administered. However, the Ottoman court system lacked an appellate structure, leading to jurisdictional case strategies where plaintiffs could take their disputes from one court system to another until they achieved a ruling that was in their favour.

In the late 19th century, the Ottoman legal system saw substantial reform. This process of legal modernisation began with the [Edict of Gülhane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_G%C3%BClhane "Edict of Gülhane") of 1839. These reforms included the "fair and public trial[s] of all accused regardless of religion", the creation of a system of "separate competences, religious and civil", and the validation of testimony on non-Muslims. Specific land codes (1858), civil codes (1869–1876), and a code of civil procedure also were enacted.

These reforms were based heavily on French models, as indicated by the adoption of a three-tiered court system. Referred to as [Nizamiye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizamiye "Nizamiye"), this system was extended to the local magistrate level with the final promulgation of the [Mecelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecelle "Mecelle"), a civil code that regulated marriage, divorce, alimony, will, and other matters of personal status. In an attempt to clarify the division of judicial competences, an administrative council laid down that religious matters were to be handled by religious courts, and statute matters were to be handled by the Nizamiye courts.

### Military

The first military unit of the Ottoman State was an army that was organised by Osman I from the tribesmen inhabiting the hills of western Anatolia in the late 13th century. The military system became an intricate organisation with the advance of the Empire. The Ottoman military was a complex system of recruiting and fief-holding. The main corps of the [Ottoman Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Army "Ottoman Army") included [Janissary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary "Janissary"), [Sipahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sipahi "Sipahi"), [Akıncı](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akinji "Akinji") and [Mehterân](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_band "Ottoman military band"). The Ottoman army was once among the most advanced fighting forces in the world, being one of the first to use muskets and cannons. The Ottoman Turks began using *[falconets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falconet_(cannon) "Falconet (cannon)")*, which were short but wide cannons, during the [Siege of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(1422) "Siege of Constantinople (1422)"). The Ottoman cavalry depended on high speed and mobility rather than heavy armour, using bows and short swords on fast [Turkoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkoman_horse "Turkoman horse") and [Arabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_horse "Arabian horse") horses (progenitors of the [Thoroughbred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoroughbred#Foundation_stallions "Thoroughbred") racing horse), and often applied tactics similar to those of the [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire"), such as pretending to retreat while surrounding the enemy forces inside a crescent-shaped formation and then making the real attack. The Ottoman army continued to be an effective fighting force throughout the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, falling behind the empire's European rivals only during a long period of peace from 1740 to 1768.

The modernisation of the Ottoman Empire in the 19th century started with the military. In 1826 Sultan Mahmud II abolished the Janissary corps and established the modern Ottoman army. He named them as the [Nizam-ı Cedid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizam-%C4%B1_Cedid "Nizam-ı Cedid") (New Order). The Ottoman army was also the first institution to hire foreign experts and send its officers for training in western European countries. Consequently, the Young Turks movement began when these relatively young and newly trained men returned with their education.

The [Ottoman Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Navy "Ottoman Navy") vastly contributed to the expansion of the Empire's territories on the European continent. It initiated the conquest of North Africa, with the addition of [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algeria "Algeria") and Egypt to the Ottoman Empire in 1517. Starting with the loss of Greece in 1821 and Algeria in 1830, Ottoman naval power and control over the Empire's distant overseas territories began to decline. Sultan [Abdülaziz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd%C3%BClaziz "Abdülaziz") (reigned 1861–1876) attempted to reestablish a strong Ottoman navy, building the largest fleet after those of Britain and France. The shipyard at Barrow, England, built its first [submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine "Submarine") in 1886 for the Ottoman Empire.

However, the collapsing Ottoman economy could not sustain the fleet's strength for long. Sultan [Abdülhamid II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd%C3%BClhamid_II "Abdülhamid II") distrusted the admirals who sided with the reformist [Midhat Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midhat_Pasha "Midhat Pasha") and claimed that the large and expensive fleet was of no use against the Russians during the Russo-Turkish War. He locked most of the fleet inside the [Golden Horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Horn "Golden Horn"), where the ships decayed for the next 30 years. Following the Young Turk Revolution in 1908, the Committee of Union and Progress sought to develop a strong Ottoman naval force. The *Ottoman Navy Foundation* was established in 1910 to buy new ships through public donations.

The establishment of [Ottoman military aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Air_Force "Ottoman Air Force") dates back to between June 1909 and July 1911. The Ottoman Empire started preparing its first pilots and planes, and with the founding of the Aviation School (*Tayyare Mektebi*) in [Yeşilköy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ye%C5%9Filk%C3%B6y "Yeşilköy") on 3 July 1912, the Empire began to tutor its own flight officers. The founding of the Aviation School quickened advancement in the military aviation programme, increased the number of enlisted persons within it, and gave the new pilots an active role in the Ottoman Army and Navy. In May 1913, the world's first specialised Reconnaissance Training Program was started by the Aviation School, and the first separate reconnaissance division was established.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] In June 1914 a new military academy, the Naval Aviation School (*Bahriye Tayyare Mektebi*) was founded. With the outbreak of World War I, the modernisation process stopped abruptly. The [Ottoman Aviation Squadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Aviation_Squadrons "Ottoman Aviation Squadrons") fought on many fronts during World War I, from [Galicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galicia_(Central_Europe) "Galicia (Central Europe)") in the west to the Caucasus in the east and [Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen "Yemen") in the south.

## Administrative divisions

The Ottoman Empire was first subdivided into provinces, in the sense of fixed territorial units with governors appointed by the sultan, in the late 14th century.

The [eyalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyalet "Eyalet") (also *pashalik* or *beylerbeylik*) was the territory of office of a [beylerbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beylerbey "Beylerbey") ("lord of lords" or governor), and was further subdivided into [Sanjaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjak "Sanjak").

The [vilayets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilayets "Vilayets") were introduced with the promulgation of the "Vilayet Law" (*Teskil-i Vilayet Nizamnamesi*) in 1864, as part of the Tanzimat reforms. Unlike the previous eyalet system, the 1864 law established a hierarchy of administrative units: the vilayet, [liva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liva_(sanjak) "Liva (sanjak)")/[sanjak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjak "Sanjak")/[mutasarrifate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutasarrifate "Mutasarrifate"), [kaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaza "Kaza") and [village council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_council "Town council"), to which the 1871 Vilayet Law added the [nahiye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahiye "Nahiye").

## Economy

The Ottoman government deliberately pursued a policy for the development of Bursa, Edirne, and Istanbul, successive Ottoman capitals, into major commercial and industrial centres, considering that merchants and artisans were indispensable in creating a new metropolis. To this end, Mehmed and his successor Bayezid, also encouraged and welcomed migration of the Jews from different parts of Europe, who were settled in Istanbul and other port cities like Salonica. In many places in Europe, Jews were suffering persecution at the hands of their Christian counterparts, such as in Spain, after the conclusion of the [Reconquista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista"). The tolerance displayed by the Turks was welcomed by the immigrants.

The Ottoman economic mind was closely related to the basic concepts of state and society in the Middle East in which the ultimate goal of a state was consolidation and extension of the ruler's power, and the way to reach it was to get rich resources of revenues by making the productive classes prosperous. The ultimate aim was to increase the state revenues without damaging the prosperity of subjects to prevent the emergence of social disorder and to keep the traditional organisation of the society intact. The Ottoman economy greatly expanded during the early modern period, with particularly high growth rates during the first half of the 18th century. The empire's annual income quadrupled between 1523 and 1748, adjusted for inflation.

The organisation of the treasury and chancery were developed under the Ottoman Empire more than any other Islamic government and, until the 17th century, they were the leading organisation among all their contemporaries. This organisation developed a scribal bureaucracy (known as "men of the pen") as a distinct group, partly highly trained ulama, which developed into a professional body. The effectiveness of this professional financial body stands behind the success of many great Ottoman statesmen.

Modern Ottoman studies indicate that the change in relations between the Ottoman Turks and central Europe was caused by the opening of the new sea routes. It is possible to see the decline in the significance of the land routes to the East as Western Europe opened the ocean routes that bypassed the Middle East and the Mediterranean as parallel to the decline of the Ottoman Empire itself.[*[failed verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*] The [Anglo-Ottoman Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Ottoman_Treaty "Anglo-Ottoman Treaty"), also known as the [Treaty of Balta Liman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Balta_Liman "Treaty of Balta Liman") that opened the Ottoman markets directly to English and French competitors, can be seen as one of the staging posts along with this development.

By developing commercial centres and routes, encouraging people to extend the area of cultivated land in the country and international trade through its dominions, the state performed basic economic functions in the Empire. But in all this, the financial and political interests of the state were dominant. Within the social and political system they were living in, Ottoman administrators could not see the desirability of the dynamics and principles of the capitalist and mercantile economies developing in Western Europe.

Economic historian [Paul Bairoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bairoch "Paul Bairoch") argues that [free trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade "Free trade") contributed to [deindustrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deindustrialisation "Deindustrialisation") in the Ottoman Empire. In contrast to the [protectionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectionism "Protectionism") of China, Japan, and Spain, the Ottoman Empire had a [liberal trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_liberalism "Economic liberalism") policy, open to foreign imports. This has origins in [capitulations of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitulations_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Capitulations of the Ottoman Empire"), dating back to the first commercial treaties signed with France in 1536 and taken further with [capitulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitulation_(treaty) "Capitulation (treaty)") in 1673 and 1740, which lowered [duties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duty_(economics) "Duty (economics)") to 3% for imports and exports. The liberal Ottoman policies were praised by British economists, such as [John Ramsay McCulloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ramsay_McCulloch "John Ramsay McCulloch") in his *Dictionary of Commerce* (1834), but later criticised by British politicians such as Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli, who cited the Ottoman Empire as "an instance of the injury done by unrestrained competition" in the 1846 [Corn Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_Laws "Corn Laws") debate.

## Demographics

A population estimate for the empire of 11,692,480 for the 1520–1535 period was obtained by counting the households in Ottoman tithe registers, and multiplying this number by 5. For unclear reasons, the population in the 18th century was lower than that in the 16th century. An estimate of 7,230,660 for the first census held in 1831 is considered a serious undercount, as this census was meant only to register possible conscripts.

Censuses of Ottoman territories only began in the early 19th century. Figures from 1831 onwards are available as official census results, but the censuses did not cover the whole population. For example, the 1831 census only counted men and did not cover the whole empire. For earlier periods estimates of size and distribution of the population are based on observed demographic patterns.

However, it began to rise to reach 25–32 million by 1800, with around 10 million in the European provinces (primarily in the Balkans), 11 million in the Asiatic provinces, and around 3 million in the African provinces. Population densities were higher in the European provinces, double those in Anatolia, which in turn were triple the population densities of Iraq and [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria") and five times the population density of Arabia.

Towards the end of the empire's existence life expectancy was 49 years, compared to the mid-twenties in Serbia at the beginning of the 19th century. Epidemic diseases and famine caused major disruption and demographic changes. In 1785 around one-sixth of the Egyptian population died from the plague and Aleppo saw its population reduced by twenty percent in the 18th century. Six famines hit Egypt alone between 1687 and 1731 and the last famine to hit Anatolia was four decades later. The [1812–1819 Ottoman plague epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1812%E2%80%931819_Ottoman_plague_epidemic "1812–1819 Ottoman plague epidemic") would cost the lives of at least 300,000 individuals.

The rise of port cities saw the clustering of populations caused by the development of steamships and railroads. Urbanisation increased from 1700 to 1922, with towns and cities growing. Improvements in health and sanitation made them more attractive to live and work in. Port cities like Salonica, in Greece, saw its population rise from 55,000 in 1800 to 160,000 in 1912 and İzmir which had a population of 150,000 in 1800 grew to 300,000 by 1914. Some regions conversely had population falls—Belgrade saw its population drop from 25,000 to 8,000 mainly due to political strife.

Economic and political migrations made an impact across the empire. For example, the [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire "Russian Empire") and Austria-Habsburg annexation of the Crimean and Balkan regions respectively saw large influxes of Muslim refugees—200,000 Crimean Tatars fleeing to Dobruja. Between 1783 and 1913, approximately 5–7 million refugees arrived into the Ottoman Empire. Between the 1850s and World War I, about a million North Caucasian Muslims arrived in the Ottoman Empire as refugees. Some migrations left indelible marks such as political tension between parts of the empire (e.g., Turkey and Bulgaria), whereas centrifugal effects were noticed in other territories, simpler demographics emerging from diverse populations. Economies were also impacted by the loss of artisans, merchants, manufacturers, and agriculturists. Since the 19th century, a large proportion of Muslim peoples from the Balkans emigrated to present-day Turkey. These people are called *[Muhacir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhacir "Muhacir")*. By the time the Ottoman Empire came to an end in 1922, half of the urban population of Turkey was descended from Muslim refugees from Russia.

### Language

[Ottoman Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Turkish "Ottoman Turkish") was the official language of the Empire. It was an [Oghuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oghuz_languages "Oghuz languages") [Turkic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages "Turkic languages") highly influenced by [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language") and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"), though lower registries spoken by the common people had fewer influences from other languages compared to higher varieties used by upper classes and governmental authorities. Turkish, in its Ottoman variation, was a language of military and administration since the nascent days of the Ottomans. The Ottoman constitution of 1876 did officially cement the official imperial status of Turkish.

The Ottomans had several influential languages: Turkish, spoken by the majority of the people in Anatolia and by the majority of Muslims of the Balkans except some regions such as [Albania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Albania "History of Albania"), [Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Bosnia "History of Bosnia") and the [Megleno-Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megleno-Romanians "Megleno-Romanians")-inhabited [Nânti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A2nti "Nânti"); Persian, only spoken by the educated; Arabic, spoken mainly in Egypt, the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant"), [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Arabia "History of Arabia"), Iraq, North Africa, [Kuwait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Kuwait "History of Kuwait") and parts of the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa") and [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_language "Berber language") in North Africa. In the last two centuries, usage of these became limited, though, and specific: Persian served mainly as a literary language for the educated, while [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") was used for Islamic prayers. In the post-[Tanzimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanzimat "Tanzimat") period French became the common Western language among the educated.

Because of a low literacy rate among the public (about 2–3% until the early 19th century and just about 15% at the end of the 19th century), ordinary people had to hire [scribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribe "Scribe") as "special request-writers" (*arzuhâlci*s) to be able to communicate with the government. Some ethnic groups continued to speak within their families and neighbourhoods ([mahalles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalle "Mahalle")) with their own languages, though many non-Muslim minorities such as Greeks and Armenians only spoke Turkish. In villages where two or more populations lived together, the inhabitants often spoke each other's language. In cosmopolitan cities, people often spoke their family languages; in some cities, many of those who were not ethnic [Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people "Turkish people") spoke Turkish as a second language. However, not all cities used Turkish as a lingua franca.

### Religion

[Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam "Sunni Islam") was the prevailing *[Dīn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Din_(Arabic) "Din (Arabic)")* (customs, legal traditions, and religion) of the Ottoman Empire; the official *[Madh'hab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhhab "Madhhab")* (school of Islamic [jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence "Jurisprudence")) was *[Hanafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanafi "Hanafi").* From the early 16th century until the early 20th century, the Ottoman sultan also served as the [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate"), or politico-religious leader, of the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world"). Most of the Ottoman Sultans adhered to [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") and followed [Sufi orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufi_orders "Sufi orders"), and believed Sufism was the correct way to reach God.

Non-Muslims, particularly Christians and Jews, were present throughout the empire's history. The Ottoman imperial system was charactised by an intricate combination of official Muslim hegemony over non-Muslims and a wide degree of religious tolerance. While religious minorities were never equal under the law, they were granted recognition, protection, and limited freedoms under both Islamic and Ottoman tradition.

Until the second half of the 15th century, the majority of Ottoman subjects were Christian. Non-Muslims remained a significant and economically influential minority, albeit declining significantly by the 19th century, due largely to migration and [secession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secession "Secession"). The proportion of Muslims amounted to 60% in the 1820s, gradually increasing to 69% in the 1870s and 76% in the 1890s. By 1914, less than a fifth of the empire's population (19.1%) was non-Muslim, mostly made up of Jews and Christian Greeks, Assyrians, and Armenians.

#### Islam

[Turkic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_peoples "Turkic peoples") practiced a form of [shamanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism "Shamanism") before adopting Islam. The [Muslim conquest of Transoxiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Transoxiana "Muslim conquest of Transoxiana") under the [Abbasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate") facilitated the spread of Islam into the Turkic heartland of Central Asia. Many Turkic tribes—including the [Oghuz Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oghuz_Turks "Oghuz Turks"), who were the ancestors of both the Seljuks and the Ottomans—gradually converted to Islam and brought religion to Anatolia through their migrations beginning in the 11th century. From its founding, the Ottoman Empire officially supported the [Maturidi school of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maturidi "Maturidi") [Islamic theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_Islamic_theology "Schools of Islamic theology"), which emphasised [human reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason"), [rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality "Rationality"), the pursuit of science and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy "Islamic philosophy") (*falsafa*). The Ottomans were among the earliest and most enthusiastic adopters of the [Hanafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanafi "Hanafi") school of Islamic jurisprudence, which was comparatively more flexible and discretionary in its rulings.

The Ottoman Empire had a wide variety of Islamic sects, including [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze "Druze"), [Ismailis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma%27ilism "Isma'ilism"), [Alevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alevism "Alevism"), and [Alawites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alawites "Alawites"). [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism"), a diverse body of Islamic [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism"), found fertile ground in Ottoman lands; many Sufi religious orders (*[tariqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariqa "Tariqa")*), such as the [Bektashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bektashi_Order "Bektashi Order") and [Mevlevi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mevlevi_Order "Mevlevi Order"), were either established, or saw significant growth, throughout the empire's history. However, some heterodox Muslim groups were viewed as heretical and even ranked below Jews and Christians in terms of legal protection; Druze were frequent targets of persecution, with Ottoman authorities often citing the controversial rulings of [Ibn Taymiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Taymiyyah "Ibn Taymiyyah"), a member of the conservative [Hanbali school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanbali "Hanbali"). In 1514, Sultan Selim I ordered the massacre of 40,000 Anatolian Alevis (*[Qizilbash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qizilbash "Qizilbash")*), whom he considered a [fifth column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_column "Fifth column") for the rival [Safavid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran").

During Selim's reign, the Ottoman Empire saw an unprecedented and rapid expansion into the Middle East, particularly the [conquest of the entire Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Mamluk_War_(1516%E2%80%931517) "Ottoman–Mamluk War (1516–1517)") on the early 16th century. These conquests further solidified the Ottoman claim of being an [Islamic caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate"), although Ottoman sultans had been claiming the title of caliph since the reign of Murad I (1362–1389). The caliphate was officially transferred from the Mamluks to the Ottoman sultanate in 1517, whose members were recognised as caliphs until the [office's abolition on 3 March 1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolition_of_the_Caliphate "Abolition of the Caliphate") by the [Republic of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_National_Assembly_of_Turkey "Grand National Assembly of Turkey") (and the exile of the last caliph, [Abdülmecid II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdulmejid_II "Abdulmejid II"), to France).

#### Christianity and Judaism

In accordance with the Muslim *[dhimmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhimmi "Dhimmi")* system, the Ottoman Empire guaranteed limited freedoms to Christians, Jews, and other "[people of the book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_the_Book "People of the Book")", such as the right to worship, own property, and be exempt from the obligatory alms (*[zakat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakat "Zakat")*) required of Muslims. However, non-Muslims (or *dhimmi*) were subject to various legal restrictions, including being forbidden to carry weapons, ride on horseback, or have their homes overlook those of Muslims; likewise, they were required to pay higher taxes than Muslim subjects, including the *[jizya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jizya "Jizya"),* which was a key source of state revenue. Many Christians and Jews converted to Islam to secure full social and legal status, though most continued to practice their faith without restriction.

The Ottomans developed a unique sociopolitical system known as the [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire) "Millet (Ottoman Empire)"), which granted non-Muslim communities a large degree of political, legal, and religious autonomy; in essence, members of a millet were subjects of the empire but not subject to the Muslim faith or Islamic law. A millet could govern its own affairs, such as raising taxes and resolving internal legal disputes, with little or no interference from Ottoman authorities, so long as its members were loyal to the sultan and adhered to the rules concerning *dhimmi.* A quintessential example is the ancient Orthodox community of [Mount Athos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Athos "Mount Athos"), which was permitted to retain its autonomy and was never subject to occupation or forced conversion; even special laws were enacted to protect it from outsiders.

The [*Rum* Millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rum_Millet "Rum Millet"), which encompassed most Eastern Orthodox Christians, was governed by the Byzantine-era *[Corpus Juris Civilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Juris_Civilis "Corpus Juris Civilis")* (Code of Justinian), with the [Ecumenical Patriarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople "Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople") designated the highest religious and political authority (*millet-bashi*, or [ethnarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnarch "Ethnarch")). Likewise, [Ottoman Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "History of the Jews in the Ottoman Empire") came under the authority of the *[Haham Başı](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakham_Bashi "Hakham Bashi"),* or Ottoman [Chief Rabbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Rabbi "Chief Rabbi"), while [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians "Armenians") were under the authority of the [chief bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicos_of_All_Armenians "Catholicos of All Armenians") of the [Armenian Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church "Armenian Apostolic Church"). As the largest group of non-Muslim subjects, the Rum Millet enjoyed several special privileges in politics and commerce; however, Jews and Armenians were also well represented among the wealthy merchant class, as well as in public administration.

Some modern scholars consider the millet system to be an early example of [religious pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_pluralism "Religious pluralism"), as it accorded minority religious groups official recognition and tolerance.

### Social-political-religious structure

Beginning in the early 19th century, society, government, and religion were interrelated in a complex, overlapping way that was deemed inefficient by Atatürk, who systematically dismantled it after 1922. In Constantinople, the Sultan ruled two distinct domains: the secular government and the religious hierarchy. Religious officials formed the Ulama, who had control of religious teachings and theology, and also the Empire's judicial system, giving them a major voice in day-to-day affairs in communities across the Empire (but not including the non-Muslim millets). They were powerful enough to reject the military reforms proposed by Sultan [Selim III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_III "Selim III"). His successor Sultan [Mahmud II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_II "Mahmud II") (r. 1808–1839) first won ulama approval before proposing similar reforms. The secularisation programme brought by Atatürk ended the ulema and their institutions. The caliphate was abolished, madrasas were closed down, and the sharia courts were abolished. He replaced the Arabic alphabet with Latin letters, ended the religious school system, and gave women some political rights. Many rural traditionalists never accepted this secularisation, and by the 1990s they were reasserting a demand for a larger role for Islam.

The Janissaries were a highly formidable military unit in the early years, but as Western Europe modernised its military organisation technology, the Janissaries became a reactionary force that resisted all change. Steadily the Ottoman military power became outdated, but when the Janissaries felt their privileges were being threatened, or outsiders wanted to modernise them, or they might be superseded by the cavalrymen, they rose in rebellion. The rebellions were highly violent on both sides, but by the time the Janissaries were suppressed, it was far too late for Ottoman military power to catch up with the West. The political system was transformed by the destruction of the [Janissaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary "Janissary"), a powerful military/governmental/police force, which revolted in the [Auspicious Incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auspicious_Incident "Auspicious Incident") of 1826. Sultan Mahmud II crushed the revolt, executed the leaders and disbanded the large organisation. That set the stage for a slow process of modernisation of government functions, as the government sought, with mixed success, to adopt the main elements of Western bureaucracy and military technology.

The Janissaries had been recruited from Christians and other minorities; their abolition enabled the emergence of a Turkish elite to control the Ottoman Empire. A large number of ethnic and religious minorities were tolerated in their own separate segregated domains called millets. They were primarily [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Greeks "Ottoman Greeks"), [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Armenians in the Ottoman Empire"), or [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "History of the Jews in the Ottoman Empire"). In each locality, they governed themselves, spoke their own language, ran their own schools, cultural and religious institutions, and paid somewhat higher taxes. They had no power outside the millet. The Imperial government protected them and prevented major violent clashes between ethnic groups.

Ethnic nationalism, based on distinctive religion and language, provided a centripetal force that eventually destroyed the Ottoman Empire. In addition, Muslim ethnic groups, which were not part of the millet system, especially the Arabs and the Kurds, were outside the Turkish culture and developed their own separate nationalism. The British sponsored Arab nationalism in the First World War, promising an independent Arab state in return for Arab support. Most Arabs supported the Sultan, but those near Mecca believed in and supported the British promise.

At the local level, power was held beyond the control of the Sultan by the [ayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayan_(class) "Ayan (class)") or local notables. The ayan collected taxes, formed local armies to compete with other notables, took a reactionary attitude toward political or economic change, and often defied policies handed down by the Sultan.

After the 18th century, the Ottoman Empire was shrinking, as Russia put on heavy pressure and expanded to its south; Egypt became effectively independent in 1805, and the British later took it over, along with Cyprus. Greece became independent, and Serbia and other Balkan areas became highly restive as the force of nationalism pushed against imperialism. The French took over Algeria and Tunisia. The Europeans all thought that the empire was a sick man in rapid decline. Only the Germans seemed helpful, and their support led to the Ottoman Empire joining the central powers in 1915, with the result that they came out as one of the heaviest losers of the First World War in 1918.

## Culture

The Ottomans absorbed some of the traditions, art, and institutions of cultures in the regions they conquered and added new dimensions to them. Numerous traditions and cultural traits of previous empires (in fields such as architecture, cuisine, music, leisure, and government) were adopted by the Ottoman Turks, who developed them into new forms, resulting in a new and distinctively Ottoman cultural identity. Although the predominant literary language of the Ottoman Empire was Turkish, Persian was the preferred vehicle for the projection of an imperial image.

[Slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Slavery in the Ottoman Empire") was part of Ottoman society, with most slaves employed as domestic servants or in the case of female slaves as [concubines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concubinage_in_Islam "Concubinage in Islam") ([sexual slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_slavery "Sexual slavery")).
Agricultural slavery, such as that [in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States "Slavery in the United States"), was relatively rare.
The child of a slave was born a slave, unless the male slave owner acknowledged the child of his female slave as his, in which case the child would be automatically free by law.
Female slaves were still sold in the Empire as late as 1908.
During the 19th century the Empire came under pressure from Western European countries to outlaw the practice. Policies developed by various sultans throughout the 19th century attempted to curtail the [Ottoman slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_slave_trade "Ottoman slave trade") but slavery had centuries of religious backing and sanction and so was never abolished in the Empire.

Ottomans adopted Persian bureaucratic traditions and culture. The sultans also made an important contribution in the development of [Persian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature "Persian literature").

Language was not an obvious sign of group connection and identity in the 16th century among the rulers of the Ottoman Empire, [Safavid Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran "Safavid Iran") and [Abu'l-Khayrid Shibanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaybanids "Shaybanids") of [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"). Hence the ruling classes of all three polities were bilingual in varieties of Persian and Turkic. But in the century's final quarter, linguistic adjustments occurred in the Ottoman and Safavid realms defined by a new rigidity that favoured Ottoman Turkish and Persian, respectively.

### Education

In the Ottoman Empire, each [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire) "Millet (Ottoman Empire)") established a schooling system serving its members. Education was therefore largely divided on ethnic and religious lines: few non-Muslims attended schools for Muslim students, and vice versa. Most institutions that served all ethnic and religious groups taught in French or other languages.

Several "foreign schools" (*Frerler mektebleri*) operated by religious clergy primarily served Christians, although some Muslim students attended. Garnett described the schools for Christians and Jews as "organised upon European models", with "voluntary contributions" supporting their operation and most of them "well attended" and with "a high standard of education".

### Literature

The two primary streams of Ottoman written literature are poetry and [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose"). Poetry was by far the dominant stream. The earliest work of Ottoman historiography for example, the [*İskendernâme*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iskendername "Iskendername"), was composed by the poet [Taceddin Ahmedi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taceddin_Ahmedi "Taceddin Ahmedi"). Until the 19th century, Ottoman prose did not contain any examples of fiction: there were no counterparts to, for instance, the European [romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chivalric_romance "Chivalric romance"), short story, or novel. Analog genres did exist, though, in both [Turkish folk literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_folk_literature "Turkish folk literature") and in [Divan poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diwan_(poetry) "Diwan (poetry)").

Ottoman Divan poetry was a highly ritualised and symbolic art form. From the [Persian poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature "Persian literature") that largely inspired it, it inherited a wealth of symbols whose meanings and interrelationships—both of similitude (مراعات نظير *mura'ât-i nazîr* / تناسب *tenâsüb*) and opposition (تضاد *tezâd*) were more or less prescribed. Divan poetry was composed through the constant juxtaposition of many such images within a strict metrical framework, allowing numerous potential meanings to emerge. The vast majority of Divan poetry was [lyric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_poetry "Lyric poetry") in nature: either [gazels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazel "Gazel") (which make up the greatest part of the repertoire of the tradition), or kasîdes. But there were other common genres, especially the mesnevî, a kind of [verse romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtly_romance "Courtly romance") and thus a variety of [narrative poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_poetry "Narrative poetry"); the two most notable examples of this form are the [Leyli and Majnun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyli_and_Majnun_(Fuzuli) "Leyli and Majnun (Fuzuli)") of [Fuzuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuzuli_(poet) "Fuzuli (poet)") and the [Hüsn ü Aşk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCsn_%C3%BC_A%C5%9Fk "Hüsn ü Aşk") of [Şeyh Gâlib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eeyh_G%C3%A2lib "Şeyh Gâlib"). The [Seyahatnâme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyahatn%C3%A2me "Seyahatnâme") of [Evliya Çelebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evliya_%C3%87elebi "Evliya Çelebi") (1611–1682) is an outstanding example of travel literature.

Until the 19th century, [Ottoman prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Prose of the Ottoman Empire") did not develop to the extent that contemporary Divan poetry did. A large part of the reason was that much prose was expected to adhere to the rules of *sec* (سجع, also transliterated as *seci*), or [rhymed prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhymed_prose "Rhymed prose"), a type of writing descended from the Arabic [saj'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saj%27 "Saj'") that prescribed that between each adjective and noun in a string of words, such as a sentence, there must be a rhyme. Nevertheless, there was a tradition of prose in the literature of the time, though it was exclusively nonfictional. One apparent exception was Muhayyelât (*Fancies*) by [Giritli Ali Aziz Efendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giritli_Ali_Aziz_Efendi "Giritli Ali Aziz Efendi"), a collection of stories of the fantastic written in 1796, though not published until 1867. The first novel published in the Ottoman Empire was [Vartan Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vartan_Pasha "Vartan Pasha")'s 1851 *The Story of Akabi* (Turkish: *Akabi Hikyayesi*). It was written in Turkish but with [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language "Armenian language") script.

Due to historically close ties with France, [French literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_literature "French literature") constituted the major Western influence on Ottoman literature in the latter half of the 19th century. As a result, many of the same movements prevalent in France during this period had Ottoman equivalents; in the developing Ottoman prose tradition, for instance, the influence of [Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") can be seen during the Tanzimat period, and that of the [Realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(arts) "Realism (arts)") and [Naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(literature) "Naturalism (literature)") movements in subsequent periods; in the poetic tradition, on the other hand, the influence of the [Symbolist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_(movement) "Symbolism (movement)") and [Parnassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnassianism "Parnassianism") movements was paramount.

Many of the writers in the Tanzimat period wrote in several different genres simultaneously. This diversity was, in part, due to Tanzimat writers' wish to disseminate as much of the new literature as possible, in the hopes that it would contribute to a revitalisation of Ottoman [social structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_structure "Social structure").

### Media

The media of the Ottoman Empire was diverse, with newspapers and journals published in languages including [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_(language) "French (language)"), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_(language) "Greek (language)"), and [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_(language) "German (language)"). Many of these publications were centred in [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople"), but there were also French-language newspapers produced in [Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beirut "Beirut"), [Salonika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salonika "Salonika"), and [Smyrna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smyrna "Smyrna"). Non-Muslim ethnic minorities in the empire used French as a [lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca "Lingua franca") and used French-language publications, while some provincial newspapers were published in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"). The use of French in the media persisted until the [end of the empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Ottoman_Empire "Dissolution of the Ottoman Empire") in 1923 and for a few years thereafter in the [Republic of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey").

### Architecture

The architecture of the empire developed from earlier [Seljuk Turkish architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolian_Seljuk_architecture "Anatolian Seljuk architecture"), with influences from [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_architecture "Byzantine architecture") and [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_architecture "Iranian architecture") architecture and other architectural traditions in the Middle East. [Early Ottoman architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Ottoman_architecture "Early Ottoman architecture") experimented with multiple building types over the course of the 13th to 15th centuries, progressively evolving into the [classical Ottoman style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Ottoman_architecture "Classical Ottoman architecture") of the 16th and 17th centuries, which was also strongly influenced by the [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia "Hagia Sophia"). The most important architect of the Classical period is [Mimar Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan "Mimar Sinan"), whose major works include the [Şehzade Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eehzade_Mosque "Şehzade Mosque"), [Süleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque "Süleymaniye Mosque"), and [Selimiye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selimiye_Mosque,_Edirne "Selimiye Mosque, Edirne"). The second half of the 16th century also saw the apogee of some [types of decoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_decoration "Ottoman decoration"), most notably in the production of [Iznik tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iznik_pottery "Iznik pottery").

Beginning in the 18th century, Ottoman architecture was opened to external influences, particularly from [Baroque architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture "Baroque architecture") in Western Europe, which eventually gave rise to the [Ottoman Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Baroque_architecture "Ottoman Baroque architecture") style that emerged in the 1740s. The [Nuruosmaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuruosmaniye_Mosque "Nuruosmaniye Mosque") is one of the most important examples from this period. During the 19th century, influences from Western Europe became more prominent, brought in by architects such as those from the [Balyan family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balyan_family "Balyan family"). [Empire style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_style "Empire style") and [Neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture "Neoclassical architecture") motifs were introduced and a trend towards [eclecticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclecticism_in_architecture "Eclecticism in architecture") was evident in many types of buildings, such as the [Dolmabaçe Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmabah%C3%A7e_Palace "Dolmabahçe Palace"). The last decades of the Ottoman Empire also saw the development of a new architectural style called neo-Ottoman or Ottoman [revivalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revivalism_(architecture) "Revivalism (architecture)"), also known as the [First National Architectural Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_national_architectural_movement "First national architectural movement"), by architects such as [Mimar Kemaleddin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Kemaleddin "Mimar Kemaleddin") and [Vedat Tek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedat_Tek "Vedat Tek").

Ottoman dynastic patronage was concentrated in the historic capitals of Bursa, Edirne, and Istanbul (Constantinople), as well as in several other important administrative centres such as [Amasya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amasya "Amasya") and [Manisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manisa "Manisa"). It was in these centres that most important developments in Ottoman architecture occurred and that the most monumental Ottoman architecture can be found. Major religious monuments were typically architectural complexes, known as a *[külliye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%BClliye "Külliye")*, that had multiple components providing different services or amenities. In addition to a mosque, these could include a [madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa "Madrasa"), a [hammam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam "Hammam"), an [imaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaret "Imaret"), a [sebil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebil_(fountain) "Sebil (fountain)"), a market, a [caravanserai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai "Caravanserai"), a [primary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuttab "Kuttab"), or others. These complexes were governed and managed with the help of a *[vakıf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vak%C4%B1f "Vakıf")* agreement (Arabic *waqf*). Ottoman constructions were still abundant in Anatolia and in the Balkans (Rumelia), but in the more distant Middle Eastern and North African provinces older [Islamic architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture "Islamic architecture") styles continued to hold strong influence and were sometimes blended with Ottoman styles.

### Decorative arts

The tradition of [Ottoman miniatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_miniature "Ottoman miniature"), painted to illustrate manuscripts or used in dedicated albums, was heavily influenced by the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_miniature "Persian miniature") art form, though it also included elements of the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_art "Byzantine art") tradition of [illumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript "Illuminated manuscript") and painting. A Greek academy of painters, the *Nakkashane-i-Rum*, was established in the [Topkapi Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace "Topkapı Palace") in the 15th century, while early in the following century a similar Persian academy, the *Nakkashane-i-Irani*, was added. [Surname-i Hümayun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surname-i_H%C3%BCmayun "Surname-i Hümayun") (Imperial Festival Books) were albums that commemorated celebrations in the Ottoman Empire in pictorial and textual detail.

[Ottoman illumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_illumination "Ottoman illumination") covers non-figurative painted or drawn decorative art in books or on sheets in *[muraqqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muraqqa "Muraqqa")* or albums, as opposed to the figurative images of the [Ottoman miniature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_miniature "Ottoman miniature"). It was a part of the Ottoman Book Arts together with the Ottoman miniature (*taswir*), calligraphy (*hat*), [Islamic calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calligraphy "Islamic calligraphy"), bookbinding (*cilt*) and [paper marbling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_marbling "Paper marbling") (*ebru*). In the Ottoman Empire, [illuminated and illustrated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript "Illuminated manuscript") were commissioned by the Sultan or the administrators of the court. In Topkapi Palace, these manuscripts were created by the artists working in *Nakkashane*, the atelier of the miniature and illumination artists. Both religious and non-religious books could be illuminated. Also, sheets for albums *levha* consisted of illuminated calligraphy (*hat*) of *[tughra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tughra "Tughra")*, religious texts, verses from poems or proverbs, and purely decorative drawings.

The art of carpet [weaving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaving "Weaving") was particularly significant in the Ottoman Empire, carpets having an immense importance both as decorative furnishings, rich in religious and other symbolism and as a practical consideration, as it was customary to remove one's shoes in living quarters. The weaving of such carpets originated in the [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad") cultures of central Asia (carpets being an easily transportable form of furnishing), and eventually spread to the settled societies of Anatolia. Turks used carpets, rugs, and [kilims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilim "Kilim") not just on the floors of a room but also as a hanging on walls and doorways, where they provided additional insulation. They were also commonly donated to mosques, which often amassed large collections of them.

### Music and performing arts

[Ottoman classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_music "Ottoman music") was an important part of the education of the Ottoman elite. A number of the Ottoman sultans have accomplished musicians and composers themselves, such as [Selim III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_III "Selim III"), whose compositions are often still performed today. Ottoman classical music arose largely from a confluence of [Byzantine music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_music "Byzantine music"), [Armenian music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Armenia "Music of Armenia"), [Arabic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_music "Arabic music"), and [Persian music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_traditional_music "Persian traditional music"). Compositionally, it is organised around rhythmic units called [usul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usul_(music) "Usul (music)"), which are somewhat similar to [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(music) "Metre (music)") in Western music, and [melodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melody "Melody") units called [makam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_makam "Turkish makam"), which bear some resemblance to Western [musical modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_(music) "Mode (music)").

The [instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_instrument "Musical instrument") used are a mixture of Anatolian and Central Asian instruments (the saz, the [bağlama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba%C4%9Flama "Bağlama"), the [kemence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemenche "Kemenche")), other Middle Eastern instruments (the [ud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oud "Oud"), the [tanbur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanbur "Tanbur"), the [kanun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanun_(instrument) "Qanun (instrument)"), the [ney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ney "Ney")), and—later in the tradition—Western instruments (the violin and the piano). Because of a geographic and cultural divide between the capital and other areas, two broadly distinct styles of music arose in the Ottoman Empire: Ottoman classical music and folk music. In the provinces, several different kinds of [folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music "Folk music") were created. The most dominant regions with their distinguished musical styles are Balkan-Thracian Türküs, North-Eastern ([Laz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laz_people "Laz people")) Türküs, Aegean Türküs, Central Anatolian Türküs, Eastern Anatolian Türküs, and Caucasian Türküs. Some of the distinctive styles were: [Janissary music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_band "Ottoman military band"), [Roma music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_music "Romani music"), [Belly dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belly_dance "Belly dance"), [Turkish folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_folk_music "Turkish folk music").

The traditional [shadow play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_play "Shadow play") called [Karagöz and Hacivat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karag%C3%B6z_and_Hacivat "Karagöz and Hacivat") was widespread throughout the Ottoman Empire and featured characters representing all of the major ethnic and social groups in that culture. It was performed by a single puppet master, who voiced all of the characters, and accompanied by [tambourine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tambourine "Tambourine") (*def*). Its origins are obscure, deriving perhaps from an older Egyptian tradition, or possibly from an Asian source.

### Cuisine

[Ottoman cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_cuisine "Ottoman cuisine") is the cuisine of the capital, Constantinople ([Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul "Istanbul")), and the regional capital cities, where the melting pot of cultures created a common cuisine that most of the population regardless of ethnicity shared. This diverse cuisine was honed in the Imperial Palace's kitchens by chefs brought from certain parts of the Empire to create and experiment with different ingredients. The creations of the Ottoman Palace's kitchens filtered to the population, for instance through [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan "Ramadan") events, and through the cooking at the [Yalıs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yal%C4%B1 "Yalı") of the [Pashas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasha "Pasha"), and from there on spread to the rest of the population.

Much of the cuisine of former Ottoman territories today is descended from a shared Ottoman cuisine, especially [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_cuisine "Turkish cuisine"), and including [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_cuisine "Greek cuisine"), [Balkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_cuisine "Balkan cuisine"), [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine "Armenian cuisine"), and [Middle Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Eastern_cuisine "Middle Eastern cuisine") cuisines.

### Sports

The main sports Ottomans were engaged in were [Turkish wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrestling_in_Turkey "Wrestling in Turkey"), hunting, [Turkish archery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_archery "Turkish archery"), horseback riding, [equestrian javelin throw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jereed "Jereed"), arm wrestling, and swimming. European model sports clubs were formed with the spreading popularity of [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football "Association football") matches in 19th-century Constantinople. The leading clubs, according to timeline, were [Beşiktaş Gymnastics Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Be%C5%9Fikta%C5%9F_J.K. "Beşiktaş J.K.") (1903), [Galatasaray Sports Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galatasaray_S.K. "Galatasaray S.K.") (1905), [Fenerbahçe Sports Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenerbah%C3%A7e_S.K. "Fenerbahçe S.K.") (1907), [MKE Ankaragücü (formerly Turan Sanatkarangücü)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MKE_Ankarag%C3%BCc%C3%BC "MKE Ankaragücü") (1910) in Constantinople. Football clubs were formed in other provinces too, such as [Karşıyaka Sports Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kar%C5%9F%C4%B1yaka_S.K. "Karşıyaka S.K.") (1912), [Altay Sports Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altay_S.K. "Altay S.K.") (1914) and Turkish Fatherland Football Club (later [Ülküspor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%9Clk%C3%BCspor "Ülküspor")) (1914) of [İzmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0zmir "İzmir").

## Science and technology

Over the course of Ottoman history, the Ottomans managed to build a large collection of libraries complete with translations of books from other cultures, as well as original manuscripts. A great part of this desire for local and foreign manuscripts arose in the 15th century. [Sultan Mehmet II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_II "Mehmed II") ordered [Georgios Amiroutzes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Amiroutzes "George Amiroutzes"), a Greek scholar from [Trabzon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trabzon "Trabzon"), to translate and make available to Ottoman educational institutions the geography book of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy"). Another example is [Ali Qushji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Qushji "Ali Qushji") – an [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Astronomy in the medieval Islamic world"), [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Mathematics in the medieval Islamic world") and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Physics in the medieval Islamic world") originally from [Samarkand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarkand "Samarkand") – who became a professor in two madrasas and influenced Ottoman circles as a result of his writings and the activities of his students, even though he only spent two or three years in Constantinople before his death.

[Taqi al-Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taqi_ad-Din_Muhammad_ibn_Ma%27ruf "Taqi ad-Din Muhammad ibn Ma'ruf") built the [Constantinople observatory of Taqi ad-Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople_observatory_of_Taqi_ad-Din "Constantinople observatory of Taqi ad-Din") in 1577, where he carried out observations until 1580. He calculated the [eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity "Orbital eccentricity") of the Sun's orbit and the annual motion of the [apogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsis "Apsis"). However, the observatory's primary purpose was almost certainly [astrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology") rather than astronomical, leading to its destruction in 1580 due to the rise of a clerical faction that opposed its use for that purpose. He also experimented with [steam power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine "Steam engine") in [Ottoman Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt_Eyalet "Egypt Eyalet") in 1551, when he described a [steam jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roasting_jack "Roasting jack") driven by a rudimentary [steam turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine "Steam turbine").

[Şerafeddin Sabuncuoğlu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabuncuo%C4%9Flu_%C5%9Eerafeddin "Sabuncuoğlu Şerafeddin") was the author of the first surgical atlas and the last major [medical encyclopaedia from the Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Medicine in the medieval Islamic world"). The Ottoman Empire is credited with the invention of several surgical instruments in use such as [forceps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forceps "Forceps"), [catheters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catheter "Catheter"), [scalpels and lancets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalpel "Scalpel") as well as [pincers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pincers_(tool) "Pincers (tool)").[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

In the early 19th century, [Egypt under Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt_under_the_Muhammad_Ali_dynasty "History of Egypt under the Muhammad Ali dynasty") began using [steam engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine "Steam engine") for industrial manufacturing, with industries such as [ironworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironworks "Ironworks"), [textile manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_manufacturing "Textile manufacturing"), [paper mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_mill "Paper mill") and [hulling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husk "Husk") mills moving towards steam power. Economic historian Jean Batou argues that the necessary economic conditions existed in Egypt for the adoption of [oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil "Oil") as a potential energy source for its steam engines later in the 19th century.
