# Law

**Law** is a set of rules that are created and enforced by [governmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government "Government") or societal institutions to regulate behavior, with its precise definition a matter of longstanding debate. It has been variously described as a [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science#Law "Social science") and as the art of justice. State-enforced laws can be made by a [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature "Legislature"), resulting in [statutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute "Statute"); by the executive through [decrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree "Decree") and [regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation "Regulation"); or by judges' decisions, which form [precedent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedent "Precedent") in [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law "Common law") jurisdictions. An [autocrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocrat "Autocrat") may exercise those functions within their realm. The creation of laws themselves may be influenced by a [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution "Constitution"), written or tacit, and the [rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rights "Rights") encoded therein. The law shapes [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics "Politics"), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics"), [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History "History"), and [society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society "Society") in various ways and also serves as a mediator of relations between people.

Legal systems vary between [jurisdictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_(area) "Jurisdiction (area)"), with their differences analysed in [comparative law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_law "Comparative law"). In [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system) "Civil law (legal system)") jurisdictions, a legislature or other central body [codifies and consolidates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_(law) "Codification (law)") the law. In common law systems, judges may make [binding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legally_binding "Legally binding") case law through precedent, although on occasion this may be overturned by a higher court or the legislature. [Religious law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_law "Religious law") is in use in some religious communities and states, and has historically influenced secular law.

The scope of law can be divided into two domains: [public law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_law "Public law") concerns government and society, including [constitutional law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_law "Constitutional law"), [administrative law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_law "Administrative law"), and [criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_law "Criminal law"); [private law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_law "Private law") deals with legal disputes between parties in areas such as [contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contracts "Contracts"), [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property "Property"), [torts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torts "Torts"), [delicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delicts "Delicts") and [commercial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_law "Commercial law").
This distinction is stronger in civil law countries, particularly those with a separate system of [administrative courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_courts "Administrative courts"); by contrast, the public-private law divide is less pronounced in common law jurisdictions.

Law provides a source of scholarly inquiry into [legal history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history "Legal history"), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), [economic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_analysis_of_law "Economic analysis of law") and [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_law "Sociology of law"). Law also raises important and complex issues concerning equality, fairness, and [justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice "Justice").

## Etymology

The word *law*, attested in [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") as *lagu*, comes from the [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse "Old Norse") word *lǫg*. The singular form *lag* meant 'something laid or fixed' while its plural meant 'law'.

## Philosophy of law

> But what, after all, is a law? [...] When I say that the object of laws is always general, I mean that law considers subjects en masse and actions in the abstract, and never a particular person or action. [...] On this view, we at once see that it can no longer be asked whose business it is to make laws, since they are acts of the [general will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_will "General will"); nor whether the prince is above the law, since he is a member of the State; nor whether the law can be unjust, since no one is unjust to himself; nor how we can be both free and subject to the laws, since they are but registers of our wills.

— Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *[The Social Contract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Social_Contract "The Social Contract")*, II, 6.

The philosophy of law is commonly known as jurisprudence. Normative jurisprudence asks "what should law be?", while analytic jurisprudence asks "what is law?"

### Analytical jurisprudence

There have been several attempts to produce "a universally acceptable definition of law". In 1972, [Baron Hampstead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Lloyd,_Baron_Lloyd_of_Hampstead "Dennis Lloyd, Baron Lloyd of Hampstead") suggested that no such definition could be produced. McCoubrey and White said that the question "what is law?" has no simple answer. [Glanville Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glanville_Williams "Glanville Williams") said that the meaning of the word "law" depends on the context in which that word is used. He said that, for example, "[early customary law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_customary_law "Early customary law")" and "[municipal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_law "Municipal law")" were contexts where the word "law" had two different and irreconcilable meanings. [Thurman Arnold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thurman_Arnold "Thurman Arnold") said that it is obvious that it is impossible to define the word "law" and that it is also equally obvious that the struggle to define that word should not ever be abandoned. It is possible to take the view that there is no need to define the word "law" (e.g. "let's forget about generalities and get down to [cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law "Case law")").

One definition is that law is a system of rules and guidelines enforced by social institutions to govern behaviour. In *[The Concept of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Concept_of_Law "The Concept of Law"),* [H. L. A. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._A._Hart "H. L. A. Hart") argued that law is a "system of rules"; [John Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Austin_(legal_philosopher) "John Austin (legal philosopher)") said law was "the command of a sovereign, backed by the threat of a sanction"; [Ronald Dworkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Dworkin "Ronald Dworkin") describes law as an "interpretive concept" to achieve [justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice "Justice") in his text titled *[Law's Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law%27s_Empire "Law's Empire")*; and [Joseph Raz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Raz "Joseph Raz") argues law is an "authority" to mediate people's interests. [Oliver Wendell Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Wendell_Holmes_Jr. "Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.") defined law as "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious." In his *[Treatise on Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_Law "Treatise on Law"),* [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") argues that law is a rational ordering of things, which concern the common good, that is promulgated by whoever is charged with the care of the community. This definition has both [positivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_positivism "Legal positivism") and [naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law "Natural law") elements.

### Connection to morality and justice

Definitions of law often raise the question of the extent to which law incorporates morality. [John Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Austin_(legal_philosopher) "John Austin (legal philosopher)")'s [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism "Utilitarianism") answer was that law is "commands, backed by threat of sanctions, from a sovereign, to whom people have a habit of obedience". [Natural lawyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law "Natural law"), on the other hand, such as [Jean-Jacques Rousseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau "Jean-Jacques Rousseau"), argue that law reflects essentially moral and unchangeable laws of nature. The concept of "natural law" emerged in ancient [Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophy "Greek philosophy") concurrently and in connection with the notion of justice, and re-entered the mainstream of [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture "Western culture") through the writings of [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas"), notably his *[Treatise on Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_Law "Treatise on Law")*.

[Hugo Grotius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Grotius "Hugo Grotius"), the founder of a purely rationalistic system of natural law, argued that law arises from both a social impulse—as Aristotle had indicated—and reason. [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant") believed a moral imperative requires laws "be chosen as though they should hold as universal laws of nature". [Jeremy Bentham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham "Jeremy Bentham") and his student Austin, following [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume "David Hume"), believed that this conflated the ["is" and what "ought to be"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is-ought_problem "Is-ought problem") problem. Bentham and Austin argued for law's [positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_positivism "Legal positivism"); that real law is entirely separate from "morality". Kant was also criticised by [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche "Friedrich Nietzsche"), who rejected the principle of equality, and believed that law emanates from the [will to power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_to_power "Will to power"), and cannot be labeled as "moral" or "immoral".

In 1934, the Austrian philosopher [Hans Kelsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Kelsen "Hans Kelsen") continued the positivist tradition in his book the *[Pure Theory of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Theory_of_Law "Pure Theory of Law")*. Kelsen believed that although law is separate from morality, it is endowed with "normativity", meaning we ought to obey it. While laws are positive "is" statements (e.g., the fine for reversing on a highway *is* €500), laws tell us what we "should" do. Thus, each legal system can be hypothesised to have a ['basic norm'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_norm "Basic norm") ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"): *Grundnorm*) instructing us to obey. Kelsen's major opponent, [Carl Schmitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Schmitt "Carl Schmitt"), rejected both positivism and the idea of the [rule of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_law "Rule of law") because he did not accept the primacy of abstract normative principles over concrete political positions and decisions. Therefore, Schmitt advocated a jurisprudence of the exception ([state of emergency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_emergency "State of emergency")), which denied that legal norms could encompass all of the political experience.

Later in the 20th century, [H. L. A. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._A._Hart "H. L. A. Hart") attacked Austin for his simplifications and Kelsen for his fiction in *[The Concept of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Concept_of_Law "The Concept of Law")*. Hart argued law is a system of rules, divided into primary (rules of conduct) and secondary ones (rules addressed to officials to administer primary rules). Secondary rules are further divided into rules of adjudication (to resolve legal disputes), rules of change (to vary laws), and the rule of recognition (to identify laws as valid). Two of Hart's students continued the debate: In his book *[Law's Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law%27s_Empire "Law's Empire")*, [Ronald Dworkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Dworkin "Ronald Dworkin") attacked Hart and the positivists for their refusal to treat law as a moral issue. Dworkin argues that law is an "[interpretive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretivism_(legal) "Interpretivism (legal)") concept" that requires judges to find the best fitting and most just solution to a legal dispute, given their Anglo-American constitutional traditions. [Joseph Raz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Raz "Joseph Raz"), on the other hand, defended the positivist outlook and criticised Hart's "soft social thesis" approach in *The Authority of Law*. Raz argues that law is authority, identifiable purely through social sources and without reference to moral reasoning. In his view, any categorisation of rules beyond their role as authoritative instruments in mediation is best left to [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology "Sociology") rather than to jurisprudence.

## History

The history of law is closely linked to the development of [civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization "Civilization"). [Ancient Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") law, dating as far back as 3000 BC, was based on the concept of [Ma'at](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma%27at "Ma'at") and characterised by tradition, [rhetorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric") speech, social equality and impartiality. By the 22nd century BC, the ancient [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") ruler [Ur-Nammu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur-Nammu "Ur-Nammu") had formulated the first [law code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Ur-Nammu "Code of Ur-Nammu"), which consisted of [casuistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casuistry "Casuistry") statements ("if … then ..."). Around 1760 BC, [King Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi "Hammurabi") further developed [Babylonian law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_law "Babylonian law") by codifying and inscribing it in stone. Hammurabi placed several copies of his law code throughout the kingdom of Babylon as [stelae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stele "Stele"), for the entire public to see; this became known as the [Codex Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi "Code of Hammurabi"). The most intact copy of these stelae was discovered in the 19th century by British [Assyriologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyriologists "Assyriologists"), and has since been fully [transliterated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transliterated "Transliterated") and translated into various languages, including English, Italian, German, and French.

The [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament") dates back to 1280 BC and presents moral imperatives as recommendations for a good society. The small [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") city-state, ancient [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Athens "History of Athens"), from about the 8th century BC was the first society to be based on broad inclusion of its citizenry, excluding women and [enslaved people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_ancient_Greece "Slavery in ancient Greece"). However, Athens had no legal science or single word for "law", relying instead on the three-way distinction between divine law (*thémis*), human decree (*nómos*), and custom (*díkē*). Yet [Ancient Greek law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_law "Ancient Greek law") contained major [constitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solonian_Constitution "Solonian Constitution") innovations in the development of [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy "Athenian democracy").

[Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law "Roman law") was heavily influenced by Greek philosophy, but professional jurists developed its detailed rules and were highly sophisticated. Over the centuries between the rise and decline of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"), law was adapted to cope with the changing social situations and underwent major codification under [Theodosius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_II "Theodosius II") and [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I"). Although codes were replaced by [custom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custom_(law) "Custom (law)") and [case law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law "Case law") during the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages "Early Middle Ages"), Roman law was rediscovered around the 11th century when medieval legal scholars began to research Roman codes and adapt their concepts to the [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law_of_the_Catholic_Church "Canon law of the Catholic Church"), giving birth to the *[jus commune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jus_commune "Jus commune")*. Latin [legal maxims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_maxim "Legal maxim") (called [brocards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brocard_(law) "Brocard (law)")) were compiled for guidance. In medieval England, royal courts developed a body of [precedent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedent "Precedent") which later became the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law "Common law"). A Europe-wide [Law Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_Merchant "Law Merchant") was established so that merchants could trade under common standards of practice rather than the many splintered facets of local laws. The Law Merchant, a precursor to modern commercial law, emphasised the freedom to contract and the alienability of property. As [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism") grew in the 18th and 19th centuries, the Law Merchant was incorporated into countries' local law under new civil codes. The [Napoleonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Code "Napoleonic Code") and [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%BCrgerliches_Gesetzbuch "Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch") Codes became the most influential. In contrast to English common law, which consists of enormous tomes of case law, codes in small books are easy to export and easy for judges to apply. However, today there are signs that civil and common law are converging. EU law is codified in treaties, but develops through *de facto* precedents laid down by the [European Court of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Justice "European Court of Justice").

Ancient [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_India "Law of India") and [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_law "Chinese law") represent distinct legal traditions and have historically had independent schools of legal theory and practice. The *[Arthashastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthashastra "Arthashastra")*, probably compiled around 100 AD (although it contains older material), and the *[Manusmriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manusmriti "Manusmriti")* (c. 100–300 AD) were foundational treatises in India, and comprise texts considered authoritative legal guidance. Manu's central philosophy was tolerance and [pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralism_(political_philosophy) "Pluralism (political philosophy)"), and was cited across Southeast Asia. During the [Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquests_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent"), [sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia") was established by the Muslim sultanates and empires, most notably [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire")'s [Fatawa-e-Alamgiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatawa-e-Alamgiri "Fatawa-e-Alamgiri"), compiled by emperor [Aurangzeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurangzeb "Aurangzeb") and various scholars of Islam. In India, the [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") legal tradition, along with Islamic law, were both supplanted by common law when India [became part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj "British Raj") of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire"). Malaysia, Brunei, [Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Singapore "Law of Singapore") and [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Hong_Kong "Law of Hong Kong") also adopted the common law system. The Eastern Asian legal tradition reflects a unique blend of secular and religious influences. Japan was the first country to begin modernising its legal system along Western lines, by importing parts of the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_Civil "Code Civil"), but mostly the German Civil Code. This partly reflected Germany's status as a rising power in the late 19th century.

Similarly, [traditional Chinese law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_law "Traditional Chinese law") gave way to westernisation in the final years of the [Qing Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_Dynasty "Qing Dynasty"), in the form of six private law codes based mainly on the Japanese model of German law. Today [Taiwanese law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Republic_of_China "Law of the Republic of China") retains the closest affinity to the codifications from that period, because of the split between [Chiang Kai-shek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek "Chiang Kai-shek")'s nationalists, who fled there, and [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong "Mao Zedong")'s communists who won control of the mainland in 1949. The current legal infrastructure in the People's Republic of China was heavily influenced by [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") [Socialist law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_law "Socialist law"), which essentially prioritises [administrative law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_law "Administrative law") over private law rights. Due to rapid industrialisation, today China is undergoing a process of reform, at least in terms of economic, if not social and political, rights. A new contract code introduced in 1999 marked a shift away from administrative domination. Furthermore, after negotiations lasting fifteen years, in 2001 China joined the [World Trade Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization "World Trade Organization").

## Legal systems

In general, legal systems can be split between civil law and common law systems. Modern scholars argue that the significance of this distinction has progressively declined. The numerous [legal transplants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_transplants "Legal transplants"), typical of modern law, result in the sharing of many features traditionally associated with either common law or civil law. The third type of legal system is religious law, based on [scriptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scripture "Scripture"). The specific system by which a country is ruled is often determined by its history, its connections with other countries, or its adherence to international standards. The [sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sources_of_law "Sources of law") that jurisdictions adopt as authoritatively binding are the defining features of any legal system.

### Civil law

Civil law is the legal system used in most countries around the world today. In civil law the sources recognised as authoritative are, primarily, [legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislation "Legislation")—especially [codifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_(law) "Codification (law)") in constitutions or [statutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute "Statute") passed by government—and [custom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custom_(law) "Custom (law)"). Codifications date back millennia, with one early example being the [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_law "Babylonian law") *[Codex Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi "Code of Hammurabi")*. Modern civil law systems essentially derive from legal codes issued by [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") Emperor [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I") in the 6th century, which were rediscovered by 11th-century Italy. Roman law in the days of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic") and Empire was heavily procedural, and lacked a professional legal class. Instead a lay [magistrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magistrate "Magistrate"), *iudex*, was chosen to adjudicate. Decisions were not published systematically, so any case law that developed was obscured and almost unrecognised. Each case was to be decided afresh from the laws of the State, which mirrors the (theoretical) unimportance of judges' decisions for future cases in civil law systems today. From 529 to 534 AD, the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") Emperor [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I") codified and consolidated Roman law up until that point, so that what remained was one-twentieth of the mass of legal texts from before. This became known as the *[Corpus Juris Civilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Juris_Civilis "Corpus Juris Civilis")*. As one legal historian wrote, "Justinian consciously looked back to the golden age of Roman law and aimed to restore it to the peak it had reached three centuries before." The Justinian Code remained in force in the East until the fall of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"). Western Europe, meanwhile, relied on a mix of the [Theodosian Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Theodosianus "Codex Theodosianus") and Germanic customary law until the Justinian Code was rediscovered in the 11th century, which scholars at the [University of Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bologna "University of Bologna") used to interpret their own laws. Civil law codifications, based closely on Roman law and influenced by [religious laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_law "Religious law") such as [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law "Canon law"), continued to spread throughout Europe until the [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment"). Then, in the 19th century, both France, with the *[Code Civil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_Civil "Code Civil")*, and Germany, with the *[Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%BCrgerliches_Gesetzbuch "Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch")*, modernised their legal codes. Both these codes heavily influenced not only the law systems of the countries in continental Europe but also the [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Japan "Law of Japan") and [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea "South Korea") legal traditions. A central [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine "Doctrine") in [continental European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe "Continental Europe") legal thinking, originating in [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") [jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence "Jurisprudence"), is the concept of a *[Rechtsstaat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rechtsstaat "Rechtsstaat")*, meaning that everyone is subjected to the law, especially governments. Today, countries that have civil law systems range from Russia and Turkey to most of [Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_America "Central America") and [Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America "Latin America").

### Common law and equity

In [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law#1._Common_law_as_opposed_to_statutory_law_and_regulatory_law "Common law") legal systems, decisions by courts are explicitly acknowledged as law on equal footing with legislative [statutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_law "Statutory law") and executive [regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_and_secondary_legislation "Primary and secondary legislation"). The doctrine of precedent, or *[stare decisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stare_decisis "Stare decisis")* (Latin for "to stand by decisions"), means that decisions by higher courts bind lower courts to assure that similar cases reach similar results. In [contrast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law#2._Common_law_legal_systems_as_opposed_to_civil_law_legal_systems "Common law"), in [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system) "Civil law (legal system)") systems, legislative statutes are typically more detailed. Judicial decisions are shorter and less detailed because the adjudicator is writing only to decide a single case, rather than to set out reasoning that will guide future courts.

Common law originated from England and has been inherited by almost every country once tied to the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire") (except Malta, [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Scotland "Law of Scotland"), the U.S. state of [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_law "Louisiana law"), and the Canadian province of [Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_law "Quebec law")). In medieval England during the [Norman Conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Conquest "Norman Conquest"), the law varied from shire to shire, reflecting disparate tribal customs. The concept of a common law developed during the reign of [Henry II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II_of_England "Henry II of England") during the late 12th century, when Henry appointed judges who had the authority to create an institutionalised and unified system of law common to the country. The next major step in the evolution of the common law came when [King John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John,_King_of_England "John, King of England") was forced by his barons to agree to a document limiting his authority to pass laws. The *[Magna Carta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta "Magna Carta")* ("great charter") of 1215 also required that the King's entourage of judges hold their courts and judgments at "a certain place" rather than dispensing autocratic justice in unpredictable places about the country. A concentrated and elite group of judges acquired a dominant role in law-making under this system, and compared to its European counterparts, the English judiciary became highly centralised. In 1297, for instance, while the highest court in France had fifty-one judges, the [English Court of Common Pleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Court_of_Common_Pleas "English Court of Common Pleas") had five. This powerful and tight-knit judiciary gave rise to a systematised process of developing common law.

As time went on, many felt that the common law was overly systematised and inflexible, and an increasing number of citizens petitioned the King to override it. On the King's behalf, the [Lord Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chancellor "Lord Chancellor") started giving judgments to do what was equitable in a case. From the time of [Sir Thomas More](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_More "Thomas More"), the first [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer "Lawyer") to be appointed as Lord Chancellor, a systematic body of [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_(law) "Equity (law)") grew up alongside the rigid common law, and developed its own [Court of Chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Chancery "Court of Chancery"). At first, equity was often criticised for its erratic behavior. Over time, courts of equity developed solid [principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxims_of_equity "Maxims of equity"), especially under [Lord Eldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scott,_1st_Earl_of_Eldon "John Scott, 1st Earl of Eldon"). In the 19th century in England, and in [1937 in the U.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Hotel_Co._v._Parrish "West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish"), the two systems were [merged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law#1870_through_20th_century,_and_the_procedural_merger_of_law_and_equity "Common law").

In developing the common law, [academic writings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law#Contrasting_role_of_treatises_and_academic_writings_in_common_law_and_civil_law_systems "Common law") have always played an important role, both in collecting overarching principles from scattered case law and in advocating change. [William Blackstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blackstone "William Blackstone"), from around 1760, was the first scholar to collect, describe, and teach the common law. But merely in describing, scholars who sought explanations and underlying structures slowly changed the way the law actually worked.

### Religious law

Religious law is explicitly based on religious precepts. Examples include the [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism") [Halakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha "Halakha") and Islamic [Sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia")—both of which translate as the "path to follow". Christian [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law "Canon law") also survives in some church communities. Often, the implication of religion for law is unalterability because the word of God cannot be amended or legislated against by judges or governments. Nonetheless, most religious jurisdictions rely on further human elaboration to provide for thorough and detailed legal systems. For instance, the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran") has some law, and it acts as a source of further law through interpretation, *[Qiyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiyas "Qiyas")* (reasoning by analogy), *[Ijma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijma "Ijma")* (consensus), and [precedent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedent "Precedent"). This is mainly contained in a body of law and jurisprudence known as [Sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia") and [Fiqh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiqh "Fiqh") respectively. Another example is the [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah "Torah") or [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament"), in the [Pentateuch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentateuch "Pentateuch") or Five Books of Moses. This contains the basic code of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") law, which some Israeli communities choose to use. The [Halakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha "Halakha") is a code of Jewish law that summarizes some of the Talmud's interpretations.

Many countries are Sharia jurisdictions. [Israeli law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_law "Israeli law") allows [litigants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litigant "Litigant") to use religious laws only if they choose. Canon law is only in use by members of the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law_(Catholic_Church) "Canon law (Catholic Church)"), the [Eastern Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church"), and the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion "Anglican Communion").

#### Canon law

Canon law ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): κανών, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"): *kanon*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'a straight measuring rod; a [ruler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruler "Ruler")') is a set of ordinances and regulations made by [ecclesiastical authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_jurisdiction "Ecclesiastical jurisdiction"), for the government of a Christian organisation or church and its members. It is the internal [ecclesiastical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical "Ecclesiastical") law governing the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church"), the [Eastern Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church"), the [Oriental Orthodox Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Churches "Oriental Orthodox Churches"), and the individual national churches within the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion "Anglican Communion"). The way that such church law is [legislated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_power "Legislative power"), interpreted and at times [adjudicated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court "Court") varies widely among these three bodies of churches. In all three traditions, a [canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_(canon_law) "Canon (canon law)") was originally a rule adopted by a [church council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_council "Church council"); these canons formed the foundation of canon law.

The Catholic Church has the oldest continuously functioning legal system in the [western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world"), predating the evolution of modern European [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system) "Civil law (legal system)") and common law systems. The [1983 Code of Canon Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Code_of_Canon_Law "1983 Code of Canon Law") governs the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church "Latin Church") *[sui juris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_iuris "Sui iuris")*. The Eastern Catholic Churches, which developed different disciplines and practices, are governed by the *[Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Canons_of_the_Eastern_Churches "Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches")*. The [canon law of the Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law_of_the_Catholic_Church "Canon law of the Catholic Church") influenced the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law "Common law") during the medieval period through its preservation of [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law "Roman law") doctrine, such as the [presumption of innocence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presumption_of_innocence "Presumption of innocence").

Roman Catholic canon law is a fully developed legal system, with all the necessary elements: [courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_court "Ecclesiastical court"), [lawyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_lawyer "Canon lawyer"), judges, a fully articulated legal code, principles of legal interpretation, and coercive penalties, though it lacks civilly-binding force in most secular jurisdictions.

#### Sharia law

Until the 18th century, Sharia law was practiced throughout the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world") in a non-codified form, with the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire")'s [Mecelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecelle "Mecelle") code in the 19th century being a first attempt at [codifying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_(law) "Codification (law)") elements of Sharia law. Since the mid-1940s, efforts have been made in country after country to bring Sharia law more in line with modern conditions and conceptions. In modern times, the legal systems of many Muslim countries draw upon both civil and common law traditions as well as Islamic law and custom. The constitutions of certain Muslim states, such as Egypt and Afghanistan, recognise Islam as the state religion and obligate the legislature to adhere to Sharia. [Saudi Arabia recognises the Quran as its constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_system_of_Saudi_Arabia "Legal system of Saudi Arabia"), and is governed based on Islamic law. Iran has also witnessed a reiteration of Islamic law into [its legal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_system_of_Iran "Legal system of Iran") after 1979. During the last few decades, one of the fundamental features of the movement of [Islamic resurgence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_resurgence "Islamic resurgence") has been the call to restore the Sharia, which has generated a vast amount of literature and affected [world politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_politics "Global politics").

### Socialist law

Socialist law is the legal system in [communist states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_state "Communist state") such as the former [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") and the People's Republic of China. Academic opinion is divided on whether it is a separate system from civil law, given major deviations from civil law based on [Marxist–Leninist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist%E2%80%93Leninist "Marxist–Leninist") ideology, such as subordinating the judiciary to the executive ruling party.

## Legal methods

There are distinguished methods of legal reasoning (applying the law) and methods of interpreting (construing) the law. The former are [legal syllogism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_syllogism "Legal syllogism"), which holds sway in civil law legal systems, [analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy "Analogy"), which is present in common law legal systems, especially in the US, and argumentative theories that occur in both systems. The latter are different rules (directives) of legal interpretation, such as linguistic, teleological, and systemic interpretation, as well as more specific rules, for instance, the [Golden Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Rule "Golden Rule") or the [mischief rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mischief_rule "Mischief rule"). There are also many other arguments and cannons of interpretation which altogether make [statutory interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_interpretation "Statutory interpretation") possible.

Law professor and former [United States Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Attorney_General "United States Attorney General") [Edward H. Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_H._Levi "Edward H. Levi") noted that the "basic pattern of legal reasoning is reasoning by example"—that is, reasoning by comparing outcomes in cases resolving similar legal questions. In a U.S. Supreme Court case regarding procedural efforts taken by a debt collection company to avoid errors, Justice [Sotomayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonia_Sotomayor "Sonia Sotomayor") cautioned that "legal reasoning is not a mechanical or strictly linear process". Laws can in some cases create [unintended consequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unintended_consequences "Unintended consequences"), [perverse incentives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perverse_incentive "Perverse incentive") or [counterproductive effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterproductive_norms "Counterproductive norms").

[Jurimetrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurimetrics "Jurimetrics") is the formal application of quantitative methods, especially [probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") and [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics "Statistics"), to legal questions. The use of statistical methods in court cases and law review articles has grown massively in importance in the last few decades.

## Legal institutions

> It is a real unity of them all in one and the same person, made by covenant of every man with every man, in such manner as if every man should say to every man: I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man, or to this assembly of men, on this condition; that thou givest up, thy right to him, and authorise all his actions in like manner.

— Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, [XVII](http://oregonstate.edu/instruct/phl302/texts/hobbes/leviathan-c.html#CHAPTERXVII)

The main institutions of law in industrialised countries are independent [courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courts "Courts"), representative parliaments, an accountable executive, the military and police, [bureaucratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy "Bureaucracy") organisation, the [legal profession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_profession "Legal profession") and [civil society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_society "Civil society") itself. John Locke, in his *[Two Treatises of Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government "Two Treatises of Government")*, and [Baron de Montesquieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Secondat,_Baron_de_Montesquieu "Charles de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu") in *[The Spirit of the Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spirit_of_the_Laws "The Spirit of the Laws")*, advocated for a [separation of powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_powers "Separation of powers") between the political, legislative, and executive bodies. Their principle was that no person should be able to usurp all powers of the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(polity) "State (polity)"), in contrast to the [absolutist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_monarchy "Absolute monarchy") theory of [Thomas Hobbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes "Thomas Hobbes")' *[Leviathan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book) "Leviathan (Hobbes book)")*. [Sun Yat-sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen "Sun Yat-sen")'s [Five Power Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Constitution_of_the_Republic_of_China "Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China") for the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China "Republic of China") took the separation of powers further by having two additional branches of government—a [Control Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_Yuan "Control Yuan") for auditing oversight and an [Examination Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Examination_Yuan "Examination Yuan") to manage the employment of public officials.

[Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber") and others reshaped thinking on the extension of the state. Modern military, policing, and bureaucratic power over ordinary citizens' daily lives pose special problems for accountability that earlier writers such as Locke or Montesquieu could not have foreseen. The customs and practices of the legal profession are an important part of people's access to [justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice "Justice"), whilst civil society refers to the social institutions, communities, and partnerships that form the political basis of law.

### Judiciary

The judiciary is the system of [courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courts "Courts") that applies the law and [contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract "Contract"). Under the doctrine of the [separation of powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_powers "Separation of powers"), the judiciary generally does not make [statutory law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_law "Statutory law") (which is the responsibility of the [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature "Legislature")) or enforce law (which is the responsibility of the [executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_(government) "Executive (government)")), but rather interprets, defends, and applies the law to the facts of each case. However, in some countries the judiciary does make [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law "Common law").

In many [jurisdictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction "Jurisdiction") the judicial branch has the power of [judicial interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_interpretation "Judicial interpretation"), [judicial review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_review "Judicial review") and [constitutional review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_review "Constitutional review"). Courts with judicial review power may annul the laws and rules of the state when it finds them incompatible with other laws, such as [primary legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_and_secondary_legislation "Primary and secondary legislation"), [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution "Constitution"), [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty "Treaty") or [international law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_law "International law").

The judiciary can also be thought of as a [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolence "Nonviolence") mechanism for [conflict resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_resolution "Conflict resolution"). [Political bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_bias "Political bias"), [sentencing disparties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentencing_disparity "Sentencing disparity"), and higher [leniency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercy "Mercy") than the public reduce the [political legitimacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_legitimacy "Political legitimacy") of the judiciary. Public confidence in the judiciary varies by country according to [opinion polls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_poll "Opinion poll").

### Legislature

Prominent examples of legislatures are the [Houses of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houses_of_Parliament "Houses of Parliament") in London, the [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress "United States Congress") in Washington, D.C., the [Bundestag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundestag "Bundestag") in Berlin, the [Duma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duma "Duma") in Moscow, the [Parlamento Italiano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Italy "Parliament of Italy") in Rome and the [*Assemblée nationale*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_France "National Assembly of France") in Paris. By the principle of representative government, people vote for politicians to carry out *their* wishes. Although countries like Israel, Greece, Sweden, and China are [unicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicameralism "Unicameralism"), most countries are [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism "Bicameralism"), meaning they have two separately appointed legislative houses.

In the 'lower house', politicians are elected to represent smaller [constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituency "Constituency"). The 'upper house' is usually elected to represent states in a [federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_republic "Federal republic") system (as in Australia, Germany, or the United States) or a different voting configuration in a unitary system (as in France). In the UK, the upper house is appointed by the government as a [house of review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords "House of Lords"). One criticism of bicameral systems with two elected chambers is that the upper and lower houses may mirror one another. The traditional justification of bicameralism is that an upper chamber acts as a house of review. This can minimise arbitrariness and injustice in governmental action.

To pass legislation, a majority of the members of a legislature must [vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote "Vote") for a [bill (proposed law)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_(proposed_law) "Bill (proposed law)") in each house. Normally, there will be several readings and amendments proposed by the different political factions. If a country has an entrenched constitution, a special majority may be required for constitutional changes, making changes to the law more difficult. A government usually leads the process and is formed from Members of Parliament (e.g., in the UK or Germany). However, in a presidential system, the government is usually formed by the executive and their appointed cabinet officials (e.g., the United States or Brazil).

### Executive

The executive in a legal system serves as the centre of political [authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority "Authority") of the [State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(polity) "State (polity)"). In a [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system "Parliamentary system"), as with Britain, Italy, Germany, India, and Japan, the executive is known as the cabinet, and is composed of members of the legislature. The executive is led by the [head of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_government "Head of government"), whose office holds power under the [confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_of_no_confidence "Motion of no confidence") of the legislature. Because popular elections appoint political parties to govern, a party's leader can change between elections.

The [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state "Head of state") is separate from the executive, symbolically enacts laws, and serves as the nation's representative. Examples include the [President of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Germany "President of Germany") (appointed by [members of federal and state legislatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Convention_(Germany) "Federal Convention (Germany)")), the [Queen of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_the_United_Kingdom "Queen of the United Kingdom") (an [hereditary office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_monarchy "Hereditary monarchy")), and the [President of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Austria "President of Austria") (elected by popular vote). The other important model is the [presidential system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_system "Presidential system"), found in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States "President of the United States") and in [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Brazil "President of Brazil"). In presidential systems, the executive acts as both head of state and head of government, and has the power to appoint an unelected cabinet. Under a presidential system, the executive branch is separate from the legislature to which it is not accountable.

Although the role of the executive varies from country to country, it usually proposes most legislation and sets the government's agenda. In presidential systems, the executive often has the power to veto legislation. Most executives in both systems are responsible for [foreign relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_policy "Foreign policy"), the [law enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement "Law enforcement"), and the bureaucracy. [Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_(government) "Minister (government)") or other officials head a country's public offices, such as a [foreign ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_minister "Foreign minister") or [defence ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_minister "Defence minister"). The election of a different executive is therefore capable of revolutionising an entire country's approach to government.

### Law enforcement

[Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber") famously argued that the state is that which controls the [monopoly on the legitimate use of force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly_on_the_legitimate_use_of_force "Monopoly on the legitimate use of force"). The military and police carry out [law enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement "Law enforcement") at the request of the government or the courts. The term [failed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failed_state "Failed state") refers to states that cannot implement or enforce policies; their police and military no longer control security and order, and society moves into anarchy, the absence of government. While military organisations have existed as long as government itself, the idea of a standing police force is a relatively modern concept. For example, [Medieval England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_England "Medieval England")'s system of travelling [criminal courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_law "Criminal law"), or [assizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_Court "Assize Court"), used [show trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show_trial "Show trial") and public executions to instill fear in communities and maintain control. The first modern police were probably those in 17th-century Paris, in the court of [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV "Louis XIV"), although the Paris Prefecture of Police claim they were the world's first uniformed policemen.

### Bureaucracy

The etymology of *bureaucracy* derives from the French word for *office* (*bureau*) and the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") for word *power* (*kratos*).[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*] Like the military and police, a legal system's government servants and bodies that make up its bureaucracy carry out the directives of the executive. One of the earliest references to the concept was made by [Baron de Grimm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Melchior,_baron_von_Grimm "Friedrich Melchior, baron von Grimm"), a German author who lived in France. In 1765, he wrote:

> The real spirit of the laws in France is that bureaucracy of which the late Monsieur de Gournay used to complain so greatly; here the offices, clerks, secretaries, inspectors, and *intendants* are not appointed to benefit the public interest, indeed the public interest appears to have been established so that offices might exist.

Cynicism over "officialdom" is still common, and the workings of public servants are typically contrasted to [private enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_enterprise "Private enterprise") motivated by [profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximization_(economics) "Maximization (economics)"). In fact, private companies, especially large ones, also have bureaucracies. Negative perceptions of "[red tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_tape "Red tape")" aside, public services such as schooling, health care, policing, and public transport are considered crucial state functions, making public bureaucratic action the locus of government power.

Writing in the early 20th century, Max Weber believed that a definitive feature of a developed state had become its bureaucratic support. Weber wrote that the typical characteristics of modern bureaucracy are that officials define its mission, the scope of work is bound by rules; management is composed of career experts who manage top down, communicating through writing and binding public servants' discretion with rules.

### Legal profession

A corollary of the rule of law is the existence of a legal profession sufficiently autonomous to invoke the authority of the independent judiciary; the right to assistance of a [barrister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrister "Barrister") in a court proceeding emanates from this corollary—in England, the function of barrister or advocate is distinguished from legal counselor. As the European Court of Human Rights has stated, the law should be adequately accessible to everyone and people should be able to foresee how the law affects them.

To maintain professionalism, the [practice of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practice_of_law "Practice of law") is typically overseen by a government or an independent regulatory body, such as a [bar association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_association "Bar association"), [bar council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_council "Bar council"), or [law society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_society "Law society"). Modern lawyers achieve a distinct professional identity through specified legal procedures (e.g., successfully passing a qualifying examination). They are required by law to have a special qualification (a legal education that earns the student a [Bachelor of Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Laws "Bachelor of Laws"), a [Bachelor of Civil Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Civil_Law "Bachelor of Civil Law"), or a [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor "Juris Doctor") degree). Higher academic degrees may also be pursued. Examples include a [Master of Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Laws "Master of Laws"), a [Master of Legal Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Legal_Studies "Master of Legal Studies"), a [Bar Professional Training Course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_Professional_Training_Course "Bar Professional Training Course"), or a [Doctor of Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Laws "Doctor of Laws"). They are constituted in office by legal forms of appointment ([being admitted to the bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admission_to_the_bar "Admission to the bar")). There are few titles of respect to signify famous lawyers, such as [Esquire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esquire "Esquire"), to indicate barristers of greater dignity, and [Doctor of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_law "Doctor of law"), to indicate a person who obtained a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in Law.

Many Muslim countries have developed similar rules governing legal education and the legal profession, but some still allow lawyers trained in traditional Islamic law to practice before personal status courts. In China and other developing countries, there are not sufficient professionally trained people to staff the existing judicial systems, and, accordingly, formal standards are more relaxed.

Once accredited, a lawyer will often work in a [law firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_firm "Law firm"), in a [chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barristers%27_chambers "Barristers' chambers") as a sole practitioner, in a government post, or in a private corporation as an internal [counsel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counsel "Counsel"). In addition, a lawyer may become a [legal researcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_research "Legal research") who provides on-demand legal research through a library, a commercial service, or freelance work. Many people trained in law put their skills to use entirely outside the legal field.

Significant to the practice of law in the common law tradition is the legal research to determine the current state of the law. This usually entails exploring [case-law reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_report "Law report"), [legal periodicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_periodical "Legal periodical") and legislation. Law practice also involves drafting documents such as court [pleadings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleading "Pleading"), persuasive [briefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brief_(law) "Brief (law)"), contracts, or [wills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_(law) "Will (law)") and trusts. Negotiation and [dispute resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispute_resolution "Dispute resolution") skills (including [ADR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_dispute_resolution "Alternative dispute resolution") techniques) are also important to legal practice, depending on the field.

### Civil society

The [Classical republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_republicanism "Classical republicanism") concept of "civil society" dates back to Hobbes and Locke. Locke saw civil society as people who have "a common established law and judicature to appeal to, with authority to decide controversies between them." German philosopher [Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wilhelm_Friedrich_Hegel "Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel") distinguished the "state" from "civil society" ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"): *bürgerliche Gesellschaft*) in *[Elements of the Philosophy of Right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elements_of_the_Philosophy_of_Right "Elements of the Philosophy of Right")*.

Hegel believed that [civil society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_society "Civil society") and the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(polity) "State (polity)") were opposites, within the scheme of his dialectic theory of history. The modern dipole state–civil society was reproduced in the theories of [Alexis de Tocqueville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_de_Tocqueville "Alexis de Tocqueville") and [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx"). In post-modern theory, civil society is necessarily a source of law, by being the basis from which people form opinions and lobby for what they believe law should be. As Australian barrister and author [Geoffrey Robertson QC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Robertson_QC "Geoffrey Robertson QC") wrote of international law, "one of its primary modern sources is found in the responses of ordinary men and women, and of the non-governmental organizations which many of them support, to the human rights abuses they see on the television screen in their living rooms."

[Freedom of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech"), [freedom of association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_association "Freedom of association") and many other [individual rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individual_rights "Individual rights") allow people to gather, discuss, criticise and hold to account their governments, from which the basis of a [deliberative democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliberative_democracy "Deliberative democracy") is formed. The more people are involved with, concerned by, and capable of changing how political power is exercised over their lives, the more acceptable and [legitimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_(political) "Legitimacy (political)") the law becomes to the people. The most familiar institutions of civil society include economic markets, profit-oriented firms, families, trade unions, hospitals, universities, schools, charities, [debating clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cogers "Cogers"), non-governmental organisations, neighbourhoods, churches, and religious associations. There is no clear legal definition of civil society, and of the institutions it includes. Most institutions and bodies that compile lists of institutions (such as the [European Economic and Social Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_and_Social_Committee "European Economic and Social Committee")) exclude political parties.

## Areas of law

All legal systems deal with the same basic issues, but jurisdictions categorise and identify their legal topics in different ways. A common distinction is that between "[public law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_law "Public law")" (a term related closely to the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(law) "State (law)"), and including constitutional, administrative and criminal law), and "[private law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_law "Private law")" (which covers contract, [tort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort "Tort") and property). In [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system) "Civil law (legal system)") systems, contract and tort fall under a general [law of obligations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_obligations "Law of obligations"), while trust law is dealt with under statutory regimes or [international conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Convention_on_the_Law_Applicable_to_Trusts_and_on_their_Recognition "Hague Convention on the Law Applicable to Trusts and on their Recognition"). Although there is some variation across jurisdictions, legal subjects commonly considered core include international law, constitutional law, administrative law, criminal law, contract, tort, property law, equity and trusts, corporate law and evidence.

### International law

[International law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_law "International law"), also known as public international law and the law of nations, is the set of [rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_law "Rule of law"), norms, [legal customs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customary_law "Customary law") and standards that [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_(polity) "State (polity)") and other actors feel an obligation to, and generally do, obey in their mutual relations. In international relations, actors are simply the individuals and collective entities, such as states, [international organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_organization "International organization"), and [non-state groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-state_actor "Non-state actor"), which can make behavioral choices, whether lawful or unlawful. Rules are formal, typically written expectations that outline required behavior, while norms are informal, often unwritten guidelines about appropriate behavior that are shaped by custom and social practice. It establishes norms for states across a broad range of domains, including [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War "War") and [diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomacy "Diplomacy"), [economic relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade "Trade"), and [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights "Human rights").

### Constitutional and administrative law

Constitutional and administrative law govern the affairs of the state. Constitutional law concerns both the relationships between the executive, legislature, and judiciary and the human rights or [civil liberties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_liberties "Civil liberties") of individuals against the state. Most jurisdictions, like the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_United_States "Law of the United States") and [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_France "Law of France"), have a single codified constitution with a [bill of rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_of_rights "Bill of rights"). A few, like the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_United_Kingdom "Law of the United Kingdom"), have no such document. A "constitution" is simply those laws which constitute the [body politic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_politic "Body politic"), from [statute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute "Statute"), [case law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law "Case law"), and [convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_convention_(political_custom) "Constitutional convention (political custom)").

The fundamental constitutional principle, inspired by [John Locke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government "Two Treatises of Government"), holds that [the individual can do anything except that which is forbidden by law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everything_which_is_not_forbidden_is_allowed "Everything which is not forbidden is allowed"); the state may do nothing except that which is authorised by law. Administrative law is the chief method for people to hold state bodies to account. People (wheresoever allowed) may potentially have the prerogative to legally challenge (or sue) an agency, local council, public service, or government ministry for [judicial review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_review "Judicial review") of the offending [edict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict "Edict") (law, ordinance, policy order). Such a challenge tests the government entity's ability to exercise actionable authority under the law and whether it observed the required procedure. The first specialist administrative court was the *[Conseil d'État](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_State_(France) "Council of State (France)")* set up in 1799, as [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon") assumed power in France.

A sub-discipline of constitutional law is [election law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_law "Election law"). It, along with [Elections commissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Elections_commission&action=edit&redlink=1 "Elections commission (page does not exist)"), councils, or committees, deals with policies and procedures that facilitate elections. These rules settle disputes or enable the translation of the will of the people into functioning [democracies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy "Democracy"). Election law addresses issues who is entitled to [vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting "Voting"), [voter registration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_registration "Voter registration"), [ballot access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot_access "Ballot access"), [campaign finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_finance "Campaign finance") and [party funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party_funding "Political party funding"), [redistricting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistribution_(election) "Redistribution (election)"), [apportionment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apportionment_(politics) "Apportionment (politics)"), [electronic voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting "Electronic voting") and [voting machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine "Voting machine"), [accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessibility "Accessibility") of elections, [election systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_system "Electoral system") and formulas, [vote counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting "Vote counting"), election disputes, [referendums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Referendum "Referendum"), and issues such as [electoral fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_fraud "Electoral fraud") and [electoral silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_silence "Election silence").

### Criminal law

Criminal law, also known as penal law, pertains to crimes and punishment. It thus regulates the definition of and penalties for offences found to have a sufficiently deleterious social impact, but, in itself, makes no moral judgment on an offender nor imposes restrictions on society that physically prevent people from committing a crime in the first place. Investigating, apprehending, charging, and trying suspected offenders is regulated by the law of [criminal procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_procedure "Criminal procedure"). The [paradigm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm "Paradigm") case of a crime lies in the proof, [beyond reasonable doubt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_burden_of_proof "Legal burden of proof"), that a person is guilty of two things. First, the accused must commit an act which is deemed by society to be criminal, or *[actus reus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actus_reus "Actus reus")* (guilty act). Second, the accused must have the requisite [malicious intent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intention_(criminal_law) "Intention (criminal law)") to do a criminal act, or *[mens rea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mens_rea "Mens rea")* (guilty mind). However, for so called "[strict liability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_liability_(criminal) "Strict liability (criminal)")" crimes, an *actus reus* is enough. Criminal systems of the civil law tradition distinguish between intention in the broad sense (*dolus directus* and *dolus eventualis*), and negligence. Negligence does not carry criminal responsibility unless a particular crime provides for its punishment.

Examples of crimes include murder, assault, fraud, and theft. In exceptional circumstances defences, can apply to specific acts, such as killing in [self defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-defense_(theory) "Self-defense (theory)") or pleading [insanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insanity_defense "Insanity defense"). Another example is in the 19th-century English case of *[R v Dudley and Stephens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_v_Dudley_and_Stephens "R v Dudley and Stephens")*, which tested whether a defence of "[necessity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessity_(criminal_law) "Necessity (criminal law)")" could justify murder and cannibalism to survive a shipwreck.

Criminal law offences are viewed as offences against not just individual victims but also the community. The state, usually with the help of police, takes the lead in prosecution, which is why in common law countries cases are cited as "*The People* v ..." or "*R* (for [Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy "Monarchy") or [Regina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_regnant "Queen regnant")) v ...". Also, lay [juries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury "Jury") are often used to determine defendants' guilt on points of fact; juries cannot change legal rules. Some developed countries still condone capital punishment for certain crimes. Still, the usual punishments for a crime are [imprisonment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prison "Prison"), [fines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_(penalty) "Fine (penalty)"), state supervision (such as probation), or [community service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_service "Community service"). Modern criminal law has been affected considerably by the social sciences, especially with respect to [sentencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_(law) "Sentence (law)"), legal research, legislation, and [rehabilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehabilitation_(penology) "Rehabilitation (penology)"). On the international field, 125 countries are [members](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_Parties_to_the_Rome_Statute_of_the_International_Criminal_Court "States Parties to the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court") of the [International Criminal Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Court "International Criminal Court"), which was established to try people for [crimes against humanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimes_against_humanity "Crimes against humanity").

### Contract law

Contract law concerns enforceable promises, and can be summed up in the Latin phrase *[pacta sunt servanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacta_sunt_servanda "Pacta sunt servanda")* (agreements must be kept). In common law jurisdictions, three key elements to the creation of a contract are necessary: [offer and acceptance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offer_and_acceptance "Offer and acceptance"), [consideration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consideration "Consideration"), and the [intention to create legal relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intention_to_create_legal_relations "Intention to create legal relations").

Consideration indicates the fact that all parties to a contract have exchanged something of value. Some common law systems, including Australia, are moving away from the requirement of consideration. The idea of [estoppel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estoppel "Estoppel") or *culpa in contrahendo* can be used to create obligations during pre-contractual negotiations.

Civil law jurisdictions treat contracts differently in several respects, with a more interventionist role for the state in both their formation and enforcement. Compared to common law jurisdictions, civil law systems incorporate more mandatory terms into contracts, allow greater latitude for courts to interpret and revise contract terms and impose a stronger [duty of good faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_faith_(law) "Good faith (law)"), but are also more likely to enforce [penalty clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_clause "Penalty clause") and [specific performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_performance "Specific performance") of contracts. They also do not require consideration for a contract to be binding. In France, an ordinary contract is said to form based on a "meeting of the minds" or a "concurrence of wills". [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Germany "Law of Germany") has a distinct approach to contracts that is closely tied to property law. Their '[abstraction principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction_principle_(law) "Abstraction principle (law)")' (*Abstraktionsprinzip*) means that the personal obligation of contract forms separately from the title of property being conferred. When contracts are invalidated for some reason (e.g., a car buyer is so drunk that he lacks legal capacity to contract) the contractual obligation to pay can be invalidated separately from the proprietary title of the car. [Unjust enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unjust_enrichment "Unjust enrichment") law, rather than contract law, is then used to restore title to the rightful owner.

### Torts and delicts

Certain [civil wrongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_wrong "Civil wrong") are grouped together as [torts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort "Tort") under common law systems and [delicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delict "Delict") under civil law systems. To have acted tortiously, one must have breached a duty to another person, or infringed some pre-existing legal right. A simple example might be unintentionally hitting someone with a ball. Under the law of [negligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negligence "Negligence"), the most common form of tort, the injured party could potentially claim compensation for their injuries from the party responsible. The principles of negligence are illustrated by *[Donoghue v Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donoghue_v_Stevenson "Donoghue v Stevenson")*. A friend of Donoghue ordered an opaque bottle of [ginger beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginger_beer "Ginger beer") (intended for the consumption of Donoghue) in a café in [Paisley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paisley,_Renfrewshire "Paisley, Renfrewshire"). Having consumed half of it, Donoghue poured the remainder into a tumbler. The decomposing remains of a snail floated out. She claimed to have suffered from shock, fallen ill with gastroenteritis, and sued the manufacturer for carelessly allowing the drink to be contaminated. The [House of Lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords "House of Lords") decided that the manufacturer was liable for Mrs Donoghue's illness. [Lord Atkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Atkin "Lord Atkin") took a distinctly moral approach and said:

> The liability for negligence [...] is no doubt based upon a general public sentiment of moral wrongdoing for which the offender must pay. [...] The rule that you are to love your neighbour becomes in law, you must not injure your neighbour; and the [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer "Lawyer")'s question, Who is my neighbour? receives a restricted reply. You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour.

This became the basis for the four principles of negligence, namely that:

1. Stevenson owed Donoghue a [duty of care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duty_of_care "Duty of care") to provide safe drinks;
2. he [breached](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breach_of_duty_in_English_law "Breach of duty in English law") his duty of care;
3. the harm would not have occurred [but for](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causation_(law) "Causation (law)") his breach; and
4. his act was the [proximate cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proximate_cause "Proximate cause") of her harm.

Another example of tort might be a neighbour making excessively loud noises with machinery on his property. Under a [nuisance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuisance "Nuisance") claim, the noise could be stopped. Torts can also involve intentional acts such as [assault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assault_(tort) "Assault (tort)"), [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_(tort) "Battery (tort)") or [trespass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trespass "Trespass"). A better-known tort is [defamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slander_and_libel "Slander and libel"), which occurs, for example, when a newspaper makes unsupportable allegations that damage a politician's reputation. More infamous are economic torts, which form the basis of [labour law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_law "Labour law") in some countries by making trade unions liable for strikes, when statute does not provide immunity.

### Property law

[Property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property "Property") law governs ownership and possession. [Real property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_property "Real property"), sometimes called 'real estate', refers to ownership of land and things attached to it. [Personal property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_property "Personal property"), refers to everything else; movable objects, such as computers, cars, jewelry or intangible rights, such as [stocks and shares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_(finance) "Share (finance)"). A right *[in rem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_rem "In rem")* is a right to a specific piece of property, in contrast to a right *[in personam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_personam "In personam")*, which allows compensation for a loss but not for a particular thing. Land law forms the basis for most kinds of property law and is the most complex. It concerns [mortgages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortgage_law "Mortgage law"), [rental agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leasehold_estate "Leasehold estate"), [licences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License "License"), [covenants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covenant_(law) "Covenant (law)"), [easements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easement "Easement") and the statutory systems for land registration. Regulations on the use of personal property fall under intellectual property, [company law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_(law) "Company (law)"), [trusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_law "Trust law"), and [commercial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_law "Commercial law").

A representative example of property law is the 1722 case of *[Armory v Delamirie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armory_v_Delamirie "Armory v Delamirie")*, which applied [English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_law "English law"). A child was deprived of possession of the gemstones that had been set in a piece of jewellery, by the businessperson entrusted to appraise the piece. The court held that, under the common law view of property, the person who can show the *best* claim to a piece of property, against any contesting party, is the owner. By contrast, the classic civil law approach to property, propounded by [Friedrich Carl von Savigny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Carl_von_Savigny "Friedrich Carl von Savigny"), is that it is a right good against the world. Obligations, such as those arising from contracts and torts, are conceptualised as rights owed by individuals. The idea of property raises many further philosophical and political issues. Locke argued that our "lives, liberties and estates" are our property because we own our bodies and [mix our labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_theory_of_property "Labour theory of property") with our surroundings.

### Equity and trusts

In historical [English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_trust_law "English trust law"), the common law did not permit dividing the *ownership* from the *control* of one piece of property—but the law of equity did recognize this through an arrangement known as a trust. Trustees control property, whereas the beneficial, or equitable, ownership of trust property is held by people known as beneficiaries. Trustees owe their beneficiaries a duty to take good care of the property entrusted to them. Another example of a trustee's duty might be to invest property wisely or sell it. This is especially the case for pension funds, the most important form of trust, where investors are trustees for people's savings until retirement. But trusts can also be set up for [charitable purposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charitable_trust "Charitable trust").

Some international norms for the structure and regulation of trusts are set out in the [Hague Trust Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Trust_Convention "Hague Trust Convention") of 1985.

### Corporate law

Corporate law (also known as **company law** or **enterprise law**) is the body of law governing the formation, governance, operation, and dissolution of [incorporated entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incorporation_(business) "Incorporation (business)"). It regulates the rights and duties of shareholders, directors, officers, and other stakeholders.

### The law of evidence

The law of evidence encompasses the rules and legal principles governing the proof of facts in legal proceedings. These rules determine what evidence may or may not be considered by a legal decision-maker such as a judge or jury.

## Intersection with other fields

### Economics

In the 18th century, [Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith "Adam Smith") presented a philosophical foundation for explaining the relationship between law and economics. The discipline arose partly out of a critique of trade unions and U.S. [antitrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitrust "Antitrust") law.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The most prominent economic analyst of law[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] is [Ronald Coase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Coase "Ronald Coase"), whose first major article, *[The Nature of the Firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nature_of_the_Firm "The Nature of the Firm")* (1937), argued that the reason for the existence of firms (companies, partnerships, etc.) is the existence of [transaction costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaction_cost "Transaction cost"). [Rational individuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_economicus "Homo economicus") trade through bilateral contracts on open markets until the costs of transactions mean that using corporations to produce things is more cost-effective. His second major article, *[The Problem of Social Cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Problem_of_Social_Cost "The Problem of Social Cost")* (1960), argued that if we lived in a world without transaction costs, people would [bargain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bargaining "Bargaining") with one another to create the same allocation of resources, regardless of the way a court might rule in property disputes. He contended that law ought to be pre-emptive, and be guided by the most [efficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficiency_(economics) "Efficiency (economics)") solution.

Many members of the so-called [Chicago School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_school_(economics) "Chicago school (economics)") are generally advocates of [deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation "Deregulation") and [privatisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatisation "Privatisation"), and are hostile to state regulation or what they see as restrictions on the operation of [free markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_market "Free market").

### Sociology

The sociology of law examines the interaction between law and society, overlapping with jurisprudence, philosophy of law, social theory, and more specialised subjects such as [criminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminology "Criminology"). It is a transdisciplinary and multidisciplinary study focused on the theorisation and empirical study of legal practices and experiences as social phenomena. The institutions of [social construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructionism "Social constructionism"), [social norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_norms "Social norms"), dispute processing, and [legal culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_culture "Legal culture") are key areas for inquiry in this knowledge field. In the United States, the field is usually called law and society studies; in Europe, it is more often referred to as socio-legal studies. At first, jurists and legal philosophers were suspicious of the sociology of law. Kelsen attacked one of its founders, [Eugen Ehrlich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugen_Ehrlich "Eugen Ehrlich"), who sought to clarify the differences and connections between positive law, which lawyers learn and apply, and other forms of 'law' or social norms that regulate everyday life, thereby generally preventing conflicts from reaching lawyers and courts. Contemporary research in the sociology of law is concerned with the way that law develops outside discrete state jurisdictions, being produced through social interaction in social arenas, and acquiring a diversity of sources of authority in national and transnational communal networks.

Around 1900, [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber") defined his "scientific" approach to law, identifying the "legal rational form" as a type of domination, not attributable to personal authority but to the authority of abstract norms. Formal legal rationality was his term for the key characteristic of the coherent and calculable law that was a precondition for modern political developments and the modern bureaucratic state. Weber saw this law as having developed in parallel with the growth of capitalism. Another sociologist, [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim"), wrote in his classic work *The Division of Labour in Society* that as society becomes more complex, the body of civil law concerned primarily with restitution and compensation grows at the expense of criminal laws and penal sanctions. Other notable early legal sociologists included [Hugo Sinzheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Sinzheimer "Hugo Sinzheimer"), [Theodor Geiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Geiger "Theodor Geiger"), [Georges Gurvitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Gurvitch "Georges Gurvitch") and [Leon Petrażycki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Petra%C5%BCycki "Leon Petrażycki") in Europe, and [William Graham Sumner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Graham_Sumner "William Graham Sumner") in the U.S.
