# Medicine

**Medicine** is the [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science "Science") and [practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praxis_(process) "Praxis (process)") of caring for [patients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patient "Patient"), managing the [diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diagnosis "Medical diagnosis"), [prognosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prognosis "Prognosis"), [prevention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_medicine "Preventive medicine"), [treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapy "Therapy") and [palliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palliation "Palliation") of their [injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injury "Injury") or [disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease "Disease"), while [promoting their health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_promotion "Health promotion"). Medicine encompasses a variety of [health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care "Health care") practices which evolved to maintain and restore [health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health "Health") through the prevention and treatment of [illness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illness "Illness") and [infection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infection "Infection"). Contemporary medicine applies [biomedical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_sciences "Biomedical sciences"), [biomedical research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_research "Biomedical research"), [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_genetics "Medical genetics"), and [medical technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_technology "Medical technology") to diagnose, treat, and prevent injury and disease, typically through various [pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical "Pharmaceutical") or [surgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgery "Surgery"), but also through therapies such as [psychotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychotherapy "Psychotherapy"), [external splints and traction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splint_(medicine) "Splint (medicine)"), [medical devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_device "Medical device"), [biologics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biologic_medical_product "Biologic medical product"), and [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_(medicine) "Radiation (medicine)"), amongst others.

Medicine has been practiced since [prehistoric times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_medicine "Prehistoric medicine"), and for most of this time it was an [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art "Art") (an area of creativity and skill), frequently having connections to the [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious "Religious") and [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical "Philosophical") beliefs of local culture. For example, a [medicine man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_man "Medicine man") would apply [herbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbs "Herbs") and say [prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer "Prayer") for healing, or an ancient philosopher and [physician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician") would apply [bloodletting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloodletting "Bloodletting") according to the theories of [humorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humorism "Humorism"), or [the four humors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_four_humors "The four humors"). In recent centuries, since the [advent of modern science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science "History of science"), most medicine has become a combination of art and science (both [basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_science "Fundamental science") and [applied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science "Applied science"), under the [umbrella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbrella_term "Umbrella term") of **medical science**). For example, while stitching technique for [sutures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgical_suture "Surgical suture") is an art learned through practice, science provides knowledge of what happens at the [cellular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytology "Cytology") and [molecular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_medicine "Molecular medicine") level in the tissues being stitched.

Prescientific forms of medicine, now known as [traditional medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_medicine "Traditional medicine") or *folk medicine*, remain commonly used in the absence of scientific medicine and are thus called [alternative medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_medicine "Alternative medicine"). Alternative treatments outside of scientific medicine with ethical, safety and efficacy concerns are termed [quackery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quackery "Quackery") or being based on [fringe science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fringe_science "Fringe science").

## Etymology

Medicine ([UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"): [/ˈmɛdsɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:En-uk-medicine.ogg "File:En-uk-medicine.ogg"), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English"): [/ˈmɛdɪsɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:En-us-medicine.ogg "File:En-us-medicine.ogg")) is the science and practice of the [diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagnosis "Diagnosis"), [prognosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prognosis "Prognosis"), [treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapy "Therapy"), and [prevention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_medicine "Preventive medicine") of [disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease "Disease"). The word "[medicine](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/medicine "wikt:medicine")" is derived from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") *[medicus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/medicus#Latin "wikt:medicus")*, meaning "a physician". The word "[physic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/physic "wikt:physic")" itself, from which "[physician](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/physician "wikt:physician")" derives, was the old word for what is now called a medicine, and also the field of medicine.

## Clinical practice

Medical availability and clinical practice vary across the world due to regional differences in [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture "Culture") and [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology "Technology"). Modern scientific medicine is highly developed and widespread in the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world"), whereas in some low-resource regions—including parts of Africa, the Pacific Islands of Oceania, Southeast Asia, and Latin America—populations often rely more heavily on [traditional medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_medicine "Traditional medicine"). These practices consist of decentralized training structures and vary widely in empirical support; consequently, they may lack formal regulatory oversight or robust evidence for clinical efficacy.

In the [developed world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_world "Developed world"), [evidence-based medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence-based_medicine "Evidence-based medicine") (EBM) is not universally applied in clinical practice; for example, a 2007 survey of literature reviews found that about 49% of medical interventions lacked sufficient evidence to support either benefit or harm. However, medical practitioners who apply an intervention with uncertain efficacy without adequate justification, transparency, or patient consent may violate the [bioethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioethical "Bioethical") principle of [non-maleficence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-maleficence "Non-maleficence"), a core tenet of biomedical ethics historically associated with the [Hippocratic Oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Oath "Hippocratic Oath"), which emphasizes a primary duty to "first, do no harm."

In modern clinical practice, [physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicians "Physicians") and [mid-level practitioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-level_practitioners "Mid-level practitioners") such as [physician assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician_assistants "Physician assistants") personally assess patients to [diagnose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diagnosis "Medical diagnosis"), prognose, treat, and prevent disease using clinical judgment. An initial medical encounter with a patient typically begins with a review of the patient's [medical history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_history "Medical history") and [medical record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_record "Medical record"), followed by a medical interview and a [physical examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_examination "Physical examination"). Basic diagnostic [medical devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_device "Medical device") (e.g., [stethoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stethoscope "Stethoscope"), [tongue depressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongue_depressor "Tongue depressor")) are typically used. After examining for [signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_sign "Medical sign") and interviewing for [symptoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symptoms "Symptoms"), the doctor may order [medical tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_test "Medical test") (e.g., [blood tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_test "Blood test")), take a [biopsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopsy "Biopsy"), or prescribe [pharmaceutical drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical_drug "Pharmaceutical drug") or other therapies. [Differential diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_diagnosis "Differential diagnosis") methods help to rule out conditions based on the information provided. During the encounter, properly informing the patient of all relevant facts is an important part of the relationship and the development of trust within the context of the [doctor-patient relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor-patient_relationship "Doctor-patient relationship"). The medical encounter is then documented in the medical record, which is a legal document in many jurisdictions. Follow-up encounters may be shorter but follow the same general procedure, and specialists follow a similar process. The diagnosis and treatment may take only a few minutes or a few weeks, depending on the complexity of the issue.

The components of the medical interview and encounter are:

* [Chief complaint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_complaint "Chief complaint") (CC): the reason for the current medical visit. These are the *[symptoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symptom "Symptom")*. They are in the patient's own words and are recorded along with the duration of each one. Also called *chief concern* or *presenting complaint*.
* Current activity: occupation, hobbies, what the patient actually does professionally and non-professionally.
* [Family history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_history_(medicine) "Family history (medicine)") (FH): listing of diseases in the family that may impact the patient. A [family tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_tree "Family tree") is sometimes used.
* [History of present illness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_present_illness "History of the present illness") (HPI): the chronological order of events of symptoms and further clarification of each symptom. Distinguishable from history of previous illness, often called past medical history (PMH). [Medical history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_history "Medical history") comprises HPI and PMH.
* [Medications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medication "Medication") (Rx): what drugs the patient takes including [prescribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_prescription "Medical prescription"), [over-the-counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-counter_drug "Over-the-counter drug"), and [home remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_remedies "Home remedies"), as well as alternative and [herbal medicines or remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbalism "Herbalism"). [Allergies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergy "Allergy") are also recorded.
* [Past medical history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Past_medical_history "Past medical history") (PMH/PMHx): concurrent medical problems, past hospitalizations and operations, injuries, past [infectious diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_disease "Infectious disease") or [vaccinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccination "Vaccination"), history of known allergies.
* [Review of systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Review_of_systems "Review of systems") (ROS) or *systems inquiry*: a set of additional questions to ask, which may be missed on HPI: a general enquiry (have you noticed any [weight loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight_loss "Weight loss"), change in sleep quality, fevers, lumps and bumps? etc.), followed by questions on the body's main organ systems ([heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_heart "Human heart"), [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_lung "Human lung"), [digestive tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestive_tract "Digestive tract"), [urinary tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urinary_system "Urinary system"), etc.).
* [Social history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_history_(medicine) "Social history (medicine)") (SH): birthplace, residences, marital history, social and economic status, habits (including [diet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diet_(nutrition) "Diet (nutrition)"), medications, [tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_smoking "Tobacco smoking"), alcohol).

The physical examination is the examination of the patient for medical signs of disease that are objective and observable, in contrast to symptoms that are volunteered by the patient and are not necessarily objectively observable. The healthcare provider uses sight, hearing, touch, and sometimes smell (e.g., in infection, [uremia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uremia "Uremia"), [diabetic ketoacidosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetic_ketoacidosis "Diabetic ketoacidosis")). Four actions are the basis of physical examination: [inspection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspection_(medicine) "Inspection (medicine)"), [palpation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palpation "Palpation") (feel), [percussion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percussion_(medicine) "Percussion (medicine)") (tap to determine resonance characteristics), and [auscultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auscultation "Auscultation") (listen), generally in that order, although auscultation occurs prior to percussion and palpation for abdominal assessments.

The clinical examination involves the study of:

* [Abdomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdomen "Abdomen") and [rectum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectum "Rectum")
* [Cardiovascular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular "Cardiovascular") ([heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart "Heart") and [blood vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_vessel "Blood vessel"))
* General appearance of the patient and specific indicators of disease (nutritional status, presence of jaundice, pallor or [clubbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nail_clubbing "Nail clubbing"))
* [Genitalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genitalia "Genitalia") (and pregnancy if the patient is or could be pregnant)
* [Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head "Head"), [eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye "Eye"), [ear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear "Ear"), [nose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nose "Nose"), and [throat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throat "Throat") ([HEENT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HEENT_examination "HEENT examination"))
* [Musculoskeletal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musculoskeletal "Musculoskeletal") (including spine and extremities)
* [Neurological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurological "Neurological") (consciousness, awareness, [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain "Brain"), [vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_system "Visual system"), [cranial nerves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranial_nerves "Cranial nerves"), spinal cord and [peripheral nerves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_nervous_system "Peripheral nervous system"))
* [Psychiatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric "Psychiatric") (orientation, [mental state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_state_examination "Mental state examination"), mood, evidence of abnormal perception or thought).
* [Respiratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory "Respiratory") (large airways and [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_lung "Human lung"))
* [Skin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_skin "Human skin")
* Vital signs including height, weight, body temperature, [blood pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_pressure "Blood pressure"), [pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse "Pulse"), respiration rate, and hemoglobin [oxygen saturation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_saturation "Oxygen saturation")

It is to likely focus on areas of interest highlighted in the medical history and may not include everything listed above.

The treatment plan may include ordering additional [medical laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_laboratory "Medical laboratory") tests and [medical imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_imaging "Medical imaging") studies, starting therapy, referral to a [specialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_specialty "Medical specialty"), or watchful observation. A follow-up may be advised. Depending upon the [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance "Health insurance") plan and the [managed care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Managed_care "Managed care") system, various forms of "[utilization review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilization_review "Utilization review")", such as prior authorization of tests, may place barriers on accessing expensive services.

The [medical decision-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Decision_Making_(journal) "Medical Decision Making (journal)") (MDM) process includes the analysis and synthesis of all the above data to come up with a list of possible diagnoses (the differential diagnoses), along with an idea of what needs to be done to obtain a definitive diagnosis that would explain the patient's problem.

On subsequent visits, the process may be repeated in an abbreviated manner to obtain any new history, symptoms, physical findings, lab or imaging results, or specialist [consultations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consultation_(doctor) "Consultation (doctor)").

## Institutions

Contemporary medicine is, in general, conducted within [health care systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_system "Health care system"). Legal, [credentialing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credential "Credential"), and financing frameworks are established by individual governments, augmented on occasion by international organizations, such as churches. The characteristics of any given health care system have a significant impact on the way medical care is provided.

From ancient times, Christian emphasis on practical charity gave rise to the development of systematic nursing and hospitals, and the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") today remains the largest non-government provider of medical services in the world. Advanced industrial countries (with the exception of the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States")) and many developing countries provide medical services through a system of [universal health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care "Universal health care") that aims to guarantee care for all through a [single-payer health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-payer_health_care "Single-payer health care") system or compulsory private or cooperative health insurance. This is intended to ensure that the entire population has access to medical care on the basis of need rather than ability to pay. Delivery may be via private medical practices, state-owned hospitals and clinics, or charities, most commonly a combination of all three.

Most [tribal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal "Tribal") societies provide no guarantee of healthcare for the population as a whole. In such societies, healthcare is available to those who can afford to pay for it, have self-insured it (either directly or as part of an employment contract), or may be covered by care financed directly by the government or tribe.

Transparency of information is another factor defining a delivery system. Access to information on conditions, treatments, quality, and pricing greatly affects the choice of patients/consumers and, therefore, the incentives of medical professionals. While the US healthcare system has come under fire for its lack of openness, new legislation may encourage greater openness. There is a perceived tension between the demand for transparency and concerns over patient confidentiality and the commercial misuse of medical data. Advocates argue that openness helps patients make informed decisions, while critics warn it can expose sensitive records and invite exploitation by insurers, employers, or marketers. Balancing accountability with privacy remains a central challenge.

The [health professionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_professional "Health professional") who provide care in medicine comprise multiple [professions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profession "Profession"), such as [medics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine "Doctor of Medicine"), [nurses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurses "Nurses"), [physiotherapists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiotherapists "Physiotherapists"), and [psychologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychologist "Psychologist"). These professions will have their own [ethical standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_ethics "Professional ethics"), professional education, and bodies. The medical profession has been conceptualized from a [sociological perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_sociology#The_Medical_Profession "Medical sociology").

### Delivery

Provision of medical care is classified into primary, secondary, and tertiary care categories.

[Primary care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_care "Primary care") medical services are provided by [physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician"), [physician assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician_assistant "Physician assistant"), [nurse practitioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse_practitioner "Nurse practitioner"), or other health professionals who have first contact with a patient seeking medical treatment or care. These occur in physician offices, medical practices, [clinics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinic "Clinic"), [nursing homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing_home "Nursing home"), schools, patients' homes, and in other places that are typically geographically close to where patients live, work or study. About 90% of medical visits can be satisfactorily and effectively dealt with by primary care provider(s). Primary care visits might include treatment of minor, acute or chronic illnesses, [preventive care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_care "Preventive care"), and [health education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_education "Health education"). Primary care is directed to the health of entire populations and thus providers care for patients of all ages and sexes.

[Secondary care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_care "Secondary care") medical services are provided by [medical specialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_specialist "Medical specialist") in their offices, practices or clinics, or at local community hospitals, to patients referred by the primary care provider who first diagnosed or treated the patient. 'Referrals' are made of those patients who required the particular expertise of, or specific procedures performed by, specialists. Secondary care services include both [ambulatory care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambulatory_care "Ambulatory care") and [inpatient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inpatient "Inpatient") services, [emergency departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_department "Emergency department"), some [intensive care medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_care_medicine "Intensive care medicine"), some surgeries and related services, [physical therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_therapy "Physical therapy"), [labor and delivery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childbirth "Childbirth"), [endoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoscopy "Endoscopy") units, diagnostic laboratory and medical imaging services, [hospice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospice_care "Hospice care") centers, and others depending on the health services systems within which the care is being delivered. Some primary care providers may also take care of hospitalized patients and deliver babies in a secondary care setting.

[Tertiary care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_care "Tertiary care") medical services are provided by specialist teams of providers in larger, more specialised hospitals or regional medical centers, which are equipped with diagnostic and treatment facilities not typically available at local (often smaller) hospitals. This allows for the treatment and care of patients with more complex or urgent or serious medical conditions, which in turn may require more expertise (including multi-disciplinary teams) and resources (facilities, staff, bed days) to effectively treat. Tertiary care may include that provided at [burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burn_(injury) "Burn (injury)") treatment or [trauma centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma_center "Trauma center"), advanced [neonatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neonatology "Neonatology") unit services, [organ transplants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplant "Organ transplant"), high-risk pregnancy and child delivery, [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_therapy "Radiation therapy") [oncology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oncology "Oncology"), and very many other forms of specialist and intensive care.

Modern medical care also depends on the keeping and use of information, including about a particular patient—still kept in many health care settings on paper 'medical records', but increasingly nowadays by [electronic means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_health_record "Electronic health record").

In low-income countries, modern healthcare is often too expensive for the average person. International healthcare policy researchers have advocated that "user fees" be removed in these areas to ensure access; however, even with removal of patient fee obligations, significant costs and barriers remain for the poor and the sick in accessing sufficient care.

[Separation of prescribing and dispensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_prescribing_and_dispensing "Separation of prescribing and dispensing") is a practice in medicine and pharmacy in which the physician who provides a [medical prescription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_prescription "Medical prescription") is different from the [pharmacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacist "Pharmacist") who provides the [prescription drug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescription_drug "Prescription drug") to the patient. In the Western world there are centuries of tradition and practice differentiating pharmacists from physicians, and two quite separate professions developed. In many Asian countries, on the other hand, it is traditional for physicians to also deliver drugs directly to patients, at least in some cases. This model is also being used increasingly in the west: especially for simply-treated conditions (eg, those needing general antibiotics), in remote locations, with vulnerable communities of patients, and in small or integrated medical facilities.

## Branches

Working together as an [interdisciplinary team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary_team "Interdisciplinary team"), many [health professionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_profession "Health profession") besides medical practitioners are involved in the delivery of modern health care. Examples include: [nurses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse "Nurse"), [emergency medical technicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_medical_technician "Emergency medical technician") and [paramedics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramedics "Paramedics"), laboratory scientists, [pharmacists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacists "Pharmacists"), [podiatrists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podiatrists "Podiatrists"), [physiotherapists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiotherapists "Physiotherapists"), [respiratory therapists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_therapists "Respiratory therapists"), [speech therapists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_therapist "Speech therapist"), [occupational therapists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_therapists "Occupational therapists"), [radiographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiographers "Radiographers"), [dietitians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietitian "Dietitian"), and [bioengineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioengineer "Bioengineer"), [medical physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_physicist "Medical physicist"), [surgeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgeons "Surgeons"), [surgeon's assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgeon%27s_assistant "Surgeon's assistant"), [surgical technologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgical_technologist "Surgical technologist").

The scope and sciences underpinning human medicine overlap many other fields. A patient admitted to the hospital is usually under the care of a specific team based on their main presenting problem, e.g., the [cardiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiology "Cardiology") team, who then may interact with other specialties, e.g., [surgical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgical "Surgical"), [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology "Radiology"), to help diagnose or treat the main problem or any subsequent complications/developments.

Physicians have many specializations and [sub-specializations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspecialization "Subspecialization") in certain branches of medicine, which are listed below. There are variations from country to country regarding which specialties certain subspecialties are in.

The main branches of medicine are:

* Basic sciences of medicine; this is what every physician is educated in, and some return to in [biomedical research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_research#Preclinical_research "Biomedical research").
* [Interdisciplinary fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary_sub-specialties_of_medicine "Interdisciplinary sub-specialties of medicine"), where different medical specialties are mixed to function in certain occasions.

### Basic sciences

* *[Anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy "Anatomy")* is the study of the physical structure of [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism"). In contrast to *macroscopic* or *[gross anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_anatomy "Gross anatomy")*, *[cytology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytology "Cytology")* and *[histology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histology "Histology")* are concerned with microscopic structures.
* *[Biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry")* is the study of the chemistry taking place in living organisms, especially the structure and function of their chemical components.
* *[Biomechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomechanics "Biomechanics")* is the study of the structure and function of biological systems by means of the methods of [Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics "Mechanics").
* *[Biophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics "Biophysics")* is an interdisciplinary science that uses the methods of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry") to study biological systems.
* *[Biostatistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biostatistics "Biostatistics")* is the application of [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics "Statistics") to biological fields in the broadest sense. A knowledge of biostatistics is essential in the planning, evaluation, and interpretation of medical research. It is also fundamental to [epidemiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology "Epidemiology") and evidence-based medicine.
* *[Cytology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_biology "Cell biology")* is the microscopic study of individual [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)").

* *[Embryology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryology "Embryology")* is the study of the early development of organisms.
* *[Endocrinology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocrinology "Endocrinology")* is the study of [hormones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hormones "Hormones") and their effect throughout the body of animals.
* *[Epidemiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology "Epidemiology")* is the study of the demographics of disease processes, and includes, but is not limited to, the study of [epidemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemics "Epidemics").
* *[Genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics")* is the study of genes, and their role in [biological inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_inheritance "Biological inheritance").
* *[Gynecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gynecology "Gynecology")* is the study of female reproductive system.
* *[Histology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histology "Histology")* is the study of the structures of [biological tissues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_tissue "Biological tissue") by light [microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopy "Microscopy"), [electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscope "Electron microscope") and [immunohistochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunohistochemistry "Immunohistochemistry").
* *[Immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology")* is the study of the [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system "Immune system"), which includes the innate and adaptive immune system in humans, for example.
* *[Lifestyle medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifestyle_medicine "Lifestyle medicine")* is the study of the [chronic conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_conditions "Chronic conditions"), and how to prevent, treat and reverse them.
* *[Medical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_physics "Medical physics")* is the study of the applications of physics principles in medicine.
* *[Microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbiology "Microbiology")* is the study of cellular, multicellular, and acellular [microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganism "Microorganism") such as [protozoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa "Protozoa"), [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterium "Bacterium"), [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungus "Fungus"), [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus "Virus"), [prions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prion "Prion"), and [viroids.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viroid "Viroid")
* *[Molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology")* is the study of molecular underpinnings of the process of [replication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_replication "DNA replication"), [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(genetics) "Transcription (genetics)") and [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_(biology) "Translation (biology)") of the genetic material.
* *[Neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience")* includes those disciplines of science that are related to the study of the [nervous system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system "Nervous system"). A main focus of neuroscience is the [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology") and [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology "Physiology") of the human brain and [spinal cord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinal_cord "Spinal cord"). Some related clinical specialties include [neurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurology "Neurology"), [neurosurgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurosurgery "Neurosurgery") and [psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatry "Psychiatry").
* *[Nutrition science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrition_science "Nutrition science")* (theoretical focus) and *[dietetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietetics "Dietetics")* (practical focus) is the study of the relationship of food and drink to health and disease, especially in determining an optimal diet. Medical nutrition therapy is done by dietitians and is prescribed for [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes "Diabetes"), [cardiovascular diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease "Cardiovascular disease"), weight and eating [disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_illness "Mental illness"), [allergies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergies "Allergies"), [malnutrition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malnutrition "Malnutrition"), and [neoplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplasia "Neoplasia") diseases.
* *[Pathology as a science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathology_as_a_science "Pathology as a science")* is the study of [disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease "Disease") – the causes, course, progression and resolution thereof.
* *[Pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology")* is the study of [drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drugs "Drugs") and their actions.
* *[Photobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photobiology "Photobiology")* is the study of the interactions between [non-ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-ionizing_radiation "Non-ionizing radiation") and living organisms.
* *[Physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology "Physiology")* is the study of the normal functioning of the body and the underlying regulatory mechanisms.
* *[Radiobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiobiology "Radiobiology")* is the study of the interactions between [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation "Ionizing radiation") and living organisms.
* *[Toxicology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicology "Toxicology")* is the study of hazardous effects of drugs and [poisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison "Poison").

### Specialties

In the broadest meaning of "medicine", there are many different specialties. In the UK, most specialities have their own body or college, which has its own entrance examination. These are collectively known as the Royal Colleges, although not all currently use the term "Royal". The development of a speciality is often driven by new technology (such as the development of effective anaesthetics) or ways of working (such as emergency departments); the new specialty leads to the formation of a unifying body of doctors and the prestige of administering their own examination.

Within medical circles, specialities usually fit into one of two broad categories: "Medicine" and "Surgery". "Medicine" refers to the practice of non-operative medicine, and most of its subspecialties require preliminary training in Internal Medicine. In the UK, this was traditionally evidenced by passing the examination for the Membership of the [Royal College of Physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Physicians "Royal College of Physicians") (MRCP) or the equivalent college in Scotland or Ireland. "Surgery" refers to the practice of operative medicine, and most subspecialties in this area require preliminary training in General Surgery, which in the UK leads to membership of the [Royal College of Surgeons of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Surgeons_of_England "Royal College of Surgeons of England") (MRCS). At present, some specialties of medicine do not fit easily into either of these categories, such as radiology, pathology, or anesthesia. Most of these have branched from one or other of the two camps above; for example anaesthesia developed first as a [faculty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_(teaching_staff) "Faculty (teaching staff)") of the Royal College of Surgeons (for which MRCS/FRCS would have been required) before becoming the [Royal College of Anaesthetists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Anaesthetists "Royal College of Anaesthetists") and membership of the college is attained by sitting for the examination of the Fellowship of the Royal College of Anesthetists (FRCA).

#### Surgical specialty

*Surgery* is an ancient medical specialty that uses operative manual and instrumental techniques on a patient to investigate or treat a [pathological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathological "Pathological") condition such as disease or [injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injury "Injury"), to help improve bodily function or appearance or to repair unwanted ruptured areas (for example, [a perforated ear drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perforated_eardrum "Perforated eardrum")). Surgeons must also manage pre-operative, post-operative, and potential surgical candidates on the hospital wards. In some centers, [anesthesiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anesthesiology "Anesthesiology") is part of the division of surgery (for historical and logistical reasons), although it is not a surgical discipline. Other medical specialties may employ surgical procedures, such as [ophthalmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophthalmology "Ophthalmology") and [dermatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermatology "Dermatology"), but are not considered surgical sub-specialties per se.

Surgical training in the U.S. requires a minimum of five years of residency after medical school. Sub-specialties of surgery often require seven or more years. In addition, fellowships can last an additional one to three years. Because post-residency fellowships can be competitive, many trainees devote two additional years to research. Thus in some cases surgical training will not finish until more than a decade after medical school. Furthermore, surgical training can be very difficult and time-consuming.

* [Plastic surgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_surgery "Plastic surgery") – may also be pursued through a separate plastic surgery residency track

#### Internal medicine specialty

**Internal medicine** is the [medical specialty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_specialty "Medical specialty") dealing with the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of adult diseases. According to some sources, an emphasis on internal structures is implied. In North America, specialists in internal medicine are commonly called "internists". Elsewhere, especially in [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations "Commonwealth of Nations") nations, such specialists are often called [physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician"). These terms, *internist* or *physician* (in the narrow sense, common outside North America), generally exclude practitioners of gynecology and obstetrics, pathology, psychiatry, and especially surgery and its subspecialities.

Because their patients are often seriously ill or require complex investigations, internists do much of their work in hospitals. Formerly, many internists were not subspecialized; such *general physicians* would see any complex nonsurgical problem; this style of practice has become much less common. In modern urban practice, most internists are subspecialists: that is, they generally limit their medical practice to problems of one organ system or to one particular area of medical knowledge. For example, [gastroenterologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastroenterology "Gastroenterology") and [nephrologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nephrology "Nephrology") specialize respectively in diseases of the gut and the kidneys.

In the Commonwealth of Nations and some other countries, specialist [pediatricians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pediatrics "Pediatrics") and [geriatricians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geriatrics "Geriatrics") are also described as *specialist physicians* (or internists) who have subspecialized by age of patient rather than by organ system. Elsewhere, especially in North America, general pediatrics is often a form of [primary care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_care_physician "Primary care physician").

Training in internal medicine (as opposed to surgical training), varies considerably across the world: see the articles on *[medical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_education "Medical education")* for more details. In North America, it requires at least three years of residency training after medical school, which can then be followed by a one- to three-year fellowship in the subspecialties listed above. In general, resident work hours in medicine are less than those in surgery, averaging about 60 hours per week in the US. This difference does not apply in the UK where all doctors are now required by law to work less than 48 hours per week on average.

#### Diagnostic specialties

* *[Clinical laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_laboratory "Clinical laboratory") sciences* are the clinical diagnostic services that apply laboratory techniques to diagnosis and management of patients. In the United States, these services are supervised by a pathologist. The personnel that work in these [medical laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_laboratory "Medical laboratory") departments are technically trained staff who do not hold medical degrees, but who usually hold an undergraduate [medical technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_technology "Medical technology") degree, who actually perform the [tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_test "Medical test"), [assays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assay "Assay"), and procedures needed for providing the specific services. Subspecialties include [transfusion medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfusion_medicine "Transfusion medicine"), [cellular pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_pathology "Cellular pathology"), [clinical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_chemistry "Clinical chemistry"), [hematology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematology "Hematology"), [clinical microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_microbiology "Clinical microbiology"), and [clinical immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_immunology "Clinical immunology").
* *[Clinical neurophysiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_neurophysiology "Clinical neurophysiology")* is concerned with testing the physiology or function of the central and peripheral aspects of the nervous system. These kinds of tests can be divided into recordings of: (1) spontaneous or continuously running electrical activity, or (2) stimulus evoked responses. Subspecialties include [electroencephalography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalography "Electroencephalography"), [electromyography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromyography "Electromyography"), [evoked potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evoked_potential "Evoked potential"), [nerve conduction study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerve_conduction_study "Nerve conduction study"), and [polysomnography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysomnography "Polysomnography"). Sometimes these tests are performed by techs without a medical degree, but the interpretation of these tests is done by a medical professional.
* *Diagnostic [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology "Radiology")* is concerned with imaging of the body, e.g. by [x-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray"), x-ray [computed tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computed_tomography "Computed tomography"), [ultrasonography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonography "Ultrasonography"), and [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance "Nuclear magnetic resonance") [tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomography "Tomography"). Interventional radiologists can access areas in the body under imaging for an intervention or diagnostic sampling.
* *[Nuclear medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_medicine "Nuclear medicine")* is concerned with studying human organ systems by administering radiolabelled substances (radiopharmaceuticals) to the body, which can then be imaged outside the body by a [gamma camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_camera "Gamma camera") or a PET scanner. Each radiopharmaceutical consists of two parts: a tracer that is specific for the function under study (e.g., neurotransmitter pathway, metabolic pathway, blood flow, or other), and a radionuclide (usually either a gamma-emitter or a positron emitter). There is a degree of overlap between nuclear medicine and radiology, as evidenced by the emergence of combined devices such as the PET/CT scanner.
* *[Pathology as a medical specialty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathology_as_a_medical_specialty "Pathology as a medical specialty")* is the branch of medicine that deals with the study of diseases and the morphologic, physiologic changes produced by them. As a diagnostic specialty, pathology can be considered the basis of modern scientific medical knowledge and plays a large role in [evidence-based medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence-based_medicine "Evidence-based medicine"). Many modern molecular tests such as [flow cytometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_cytometry "Flow cytometry"), [polymerase chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymerase_chain_reaction "Polymerase chain reaction") (PCR), [immunohistochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunohistochemistry "Immunohistochemistry"), [cytogenetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytogenetics "Cytogenetics"), gene rearrangements studies, and [fluorescent in situ hybridization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescent_in_situ_hybridization "Fluorescent in situ hybridization") (FISH) fall within the territory of pathology.

#### Other major specialties

The following are some major medical specialties that do not directly fit into any of the above-mentioned groups:

* *[Anesthesiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anesthesiology "Anesthesiology")* (also known as *anaesthetics*): concerned with the perioperative management of the surgical patient. The anesthesiologist's role during surgery is to prevent derangement in the vital organs' (i.e. brain, heart, kidneys) functions and postoperative pain. Outside of the operating room, the anesthesiology physician also serves the same function in the labor and delivery ward, and some are specialized in critical medicine.
* *[Emergency medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_medicine "Emergency medicine")* is concerned with the diagnosis and treatment of acute or life-threatening conditions, including [trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_trauma "Physical trauma"), surgical, medical, pediatric, and psychiatric emergencies.
* *[Family medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_medicine "Family medicine")*, *[family practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_practice "Family practice")*, *[general practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_practice "General practice")* or *primary care* is, in many countries, the first port-of-call for patients with non-emergency medical problems. Family physicians often provide services across a broad range of settings including office based practices, emergency department coverage, inpatient care, and nursing home care.

* *[Medical genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_genetics "Medical genetics")* is concerned with the diagnosis and management of hereditary disorders.
* *[Neurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurology "Neurology")* is concerned with diseases of the nervous system. In the UK, neurology is a subspecialty of general medicine.
* *[Obstetrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obstetrics "Obstetrics") and [gynecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gynecology "Gynecology")* (often abbreviated as *[OB/GYN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OB/GYN "OB/GYN")* (American English) or *Obs & Gynae* (British English)) are concerned respectively with childbirth and the female reproductive and associated organs. [Reproductive medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_medicine "Reproductive medicine") and [fertility medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_medicine "Fertility medicine") are generally practiced by gynecological specialists.
* *[Pediatrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pediatrics "Pediatrics")* (AE) or *paediatrics* (BE) is devoted to the care of infants, children, and adolescents. Like internal medicine, there are many pediatric subspecialties for specific age ranges, organ systems, disease classes, and sites of care delivery.
* *[Pharmaceutical medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical_medicine "Pharmaceutical medicine")* is the medical scientific discipline concerned with the discovery, development, evaluation, registration, monitoring and medical aspects of marketing of medicines for the benefit of patients and public health.
* *[Physical medicine and rehabilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_medicine_and_rehabilitation "Physical medicine and rehabilitation")* (or *physiatry*) is concerned with functional improvement after injury, illness, or [congenital disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congenital_disorder "Congenital disorder").
* *[Podiatric medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podiatric_medicine "Podiatric medicine")* is the study of, diagnosis, and medical and surgical treatment of disorders of the foot, ankle, lower limb, hip and lower back.
* *[Preventive medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_medicine "Preventive medicine")* is the branch of medicine concerned with preventing disease.
  + *[Community health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_health "Community health")* or *[public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health "Public health")* is an aspect of health services concerned with threats to the overall health of a community based on [population health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_health "Population health") analysis.
* *[Psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatry "Psychiatry")* is the branch of medicine concerned with the [bio-psycho-social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopsychosocial "Biopsychosocial") study of the [etiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiology "Etiology"), diagnosis, treatment and prevention of [cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive "Cognitive"), [perceptual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual "Perceptual"), [emotional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional "Emotional") and [behavioral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral "Behavioral") disorders. Related fields include [psychotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychotherapy "Psychotherapy") and [clinical psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_psychology "Clinical psychology").

### Interdisciplinary fields

Some interdisciplinary sub-specialties of medicine include:

* *[Addiction medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addiction_medicine "Addiction medicine")* deals with the treatment of addiction.
* *[Aerospace medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_medicine "Aerospace medicine")* deals with medical problems related to flying and [space travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight "Human spaceflight").
* *[Biomedical Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_Engineering "Biomedical Engineering")* is a field dealing with the application of [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering "Engineering") principles to medical practice.
* *[Clinical pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_pharmacology "Clinical pharmacology")* is concerned with how systems of [therapeutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutics "Therapeutics") interact with patients.
* *[Conservation medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_medicine "Conservation medicine")* studies the relationship between human and non-human animal health, and environmental conditions. Also known as ecological medicine, [environmental medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_medicine "Environmental medicine"), or [medical geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_geology "Medical geology").
* *[Disaster medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disaster_medicine "Disaster medicine")* deals with medical aspects of emergency preparedness, disaster mitigation and management.
* *[Diving medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_medicine "Diving medicine")* (or [hyperbaric medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbaric_medicine "Hyperbaric medicine")) is the prevention and treatment of diving-related problems.
* *[Evolutionary medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_medicine "Evolutionary medicine")* is a perspective on medicine derived through applying [evolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution") theory.
* *[Forensic medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forensic_medicine "Forensic medicine")* deals with medical questions in [legal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal "Legal") context, such as determination of the time and cause of death, type of weapon used to inflict trauma, reconstruction of the facial features using remains of deceased (skull) thus aiding identification.
* *[Gender-based medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-based_medicine "Gender-based medicine")* studies the biological and physiological differences between the human sexes and how that affects differences in disease.
* *[Health informatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_informatics "Health informatics")* is a relatively recent field that deal with the application of computers and [information technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology "Information technology") to medicine.
* *[Hospice and Palliative Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospice_and_Palliative_Medicine "Hospice and Palliative Medicine")* is a relatively modern branch of clinical medicine that deals with pain and symptom relief and emotional support in patients with [terminal illnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_illness "Terminal illness") including cancer and [heart failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_failure "Heart failure").
* *[Hospital medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_medicine "Hospital medicine")* is the general medical care of hospitalized patients. Physicians whose primary professional focus is hospital medicine are called [hospitalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospitalist "Hospitalist") in the United States and [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada "Canada"). The term Most Responsible Physician (MRP) or attending physician is also used interchangeably to describe this role.
* *[Laser medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_medicine "Laser medicine")* involves the use of lasers in the diagnostics or treatment of various conditions.
* Many other [health science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_science "Health science") fields, e.g. [dietetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietetics "Dietetics")
* *[Medical ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_ethics "Medical ethics")* deals with [ethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethic "Ethic") and [moral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral "Moral") principles that apply values and judgments to the practice of medicine.
* *[Medical humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_humanities "Medical humanities")* includes the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities "Humanities") ([literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature "Literature"), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), history and religion), [social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science") ([anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology"), [cultural studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_studies "Cultural studies"), [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology"), [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology "Sociology")), and the arts ([literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature "Literature"), theater, film, and [visual arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_arts "Visual arts")) and their application to [medical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_education "Medical education") and practice.
* *[Nosokinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nosokinetics "Nosokinetics")* is the science/subject of measuring and modelling the process of care in health and social care systems.
* *[Nosology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nosology "Nosology")* is the classification of diseases for various purposes.
* *[Occupational medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_medicine "Occupational medicine")* is the provision of health advice to organizations and individuals to ensure that the highest standards of health and safety at work can be achieved and maintained.
* *[Pain management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pain_management "Pain management")* (also called *[pain medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pain_medicine "Pain medicine")*, or *algiatry*) is the medical discipline concerned with the relief of pain.
* *[Pharmacogenomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacogenomics "Pharmacogenomics")* is a form of *individualized medicine*.
* *[Podiatric medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podiatry "Podiatry")* is the study of, diagnosis, and medical treatment of disorders of the foot, ankle, lower limb, hip and lower back.
* *[Sexual medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_medicine "Sexual medicine")* is concerned with diagnosing, assessing and treating all disorders related to sexuality.
* *[Sports medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_medicine "Sports medicine")* deals with the treatment and prevention and rehabilitation of sports/exercise injuries such as [muscle spasms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle_spasm "Muscle spasm"), [muscle tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle_tear "Muscle tear"), injuries to ligaments (ligament tears or ruptures) and their repair in [athletes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sportsperson "Sportsperson"), [amateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur "Amateur") and [professional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional "Professional").
* *[Therapeutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutics "Therapeutics")* is the field, more commonly referenced in earlier periods of history, of the various remedies that can be used to treat disease and promote health.
* *[Travel medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_medicine "Travel medicine")* or *emporiatrics* deals with health problems of international travelers or travelers across highly different environments.
* *[Tropical medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_medicine "Tropical medicine")* deals with the prevention and treatment of tropical diseases. It is studied separately in temperate climates where those diseases are quite unfamiliar to medical practitioners and their local clinical needs.
* *[Urgent care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urgent_care "Urgent care")* focuses on delivery of unscheduled, walk-in care outside of the hospital emergency department for injuries and illnesses that are not severe enough to require care in an emergency department. In some jurisdictions this function is combined with the emergency department.
* [Veterinary medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veterinary_medicine "Veterinary medicine"); [veterinarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veterinarians "Veterinarians") apply similar techniques as physicians to the care of non-human animals.
* *[Wilderness medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilderness_medicine_(practice) "Wilderness medicine (practice)")* entails the practice of medicine in the wild, where conventional medical facilities may not be available.

## Education and legal controls

Medical education and training varies around the world. It typically involves entry level education at a university [medical school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_school "Medical school"), followed by a period of supervised practice or [internship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_intern "Medical intern"), or [residency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residency_(medicine) "Residency (medicine)"). This can be followed by postgraduate vocational training. A variety of teaching methods have been employed in medical education, still itself a focus of active research. In Canada and the United States of America, a Doctor of Medicine degree, often abbreviated M.D., or a [Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteopathic_medicine_in_the_United_States "Osteopathic medicine in the United States") degree, often abbreviated as D.O. and unique to the United States, must be completed in and delivered from a recognized university.

Since knowledge, techniques, and medical technology continue to evolve at a rapid rate, many regulatory authorities require [continuing medical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuing_medical_education "Continuing medical education"). Medical practitioners upgrade their knowledge in various ways, including [medical journals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_journal "Medical journal"), seminars, conferences, and online programs. A database of objectives covering medical knowledge, as suggested by national societies across the United States, can be searched at <http://data.medobjectives.marian.edu/> [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20181004185930/http://data.medobjectives.marian.edu/) 4 October 2018 at the [Wayback Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine "Wayback Machine").

In most countries, it is a legal requirement for a medical doctor to be licensed or registered. In general, this entails a medical degree from a university and accreditation by a medical board or an equivalent national organization, which may ask the applicant to pass exams. This restricts the considerable legal authority of the medical profession to physicians that are trained and qualified by national standards. It is also intended as an assurance to patients and as a safeguard against [charlatans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlatan "Charlatan") that practice inadequate medicine for personal gain. While the laws generally require medical doctors to be trained in "evidence based", Western, or [Hippocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic "Hippocratic") Medicine, they are not intended to discourage different paradigms of health.

In the European Union, the profession of doctor of medicine is regulated. A profession is said to be regulated when access and exercise is subject to the possession of a specific professional qualification. The regulated professions database contains a list of regulated professions for doctor of medicine in the EU member states, EEA countries and Switzerland. This list is covered by the [Directive 2005/36/EC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_2005/36/EC "Directive 2005/36/EC").

Doctors who are negligent or intentionally harmful in their care of patients can face charges of [medical malpractice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_malpractice "Medical malpractice") and be subject to civil, criminal, or professional sanctions.

## Medical ethics

Medical ethics is a system of moral principles that apply values and judgments to the practice of medicine. As a scholarly discipline, medical ethics encompasses its practical application in clinical settings as well as work on its history, philosophy, theology, and sociology. Six of the values that commonly apply to medical ethics discussions are:

* [autonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomy#Medicine "Autonomy") – the patient has the right to refuse or choose their treatment. ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): *Voluntas aegroti suprema lex*.)
* [beneficence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beneficence_(ethics) "Beneficence (ethics)") – a practitioner should act in the best interest of the patient. (Latin: *Salus aegroti suprema lex*.)
* [justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_(ethics) "Justice (ethics)") – concerns the distribution of scarce health resources, and the decision of who gets what treatment (fairness and equality).
* [non-maleficence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primum_non_nocere "Primum non nocere") – "first, do no harm" (Latin: *primum non-nocere*).
* [respect for persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respect_for_persons "Respect for persons") – the patient (and the person treating the patient) have the right to be treated with dignity.
* [truthfulness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth "Truth") and [honesty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honesty "Honesty") – the concept of [informed consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informed_consent "Informed consent") has increased in importance since the historical events of the [Doctors' Trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctors%27_Trial "Doctors' Trial") of the Nuremberg trials, [Tuskegee syphilis experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_syphilis_experiment "Tuskegee syphilis experiment"), and others.

Such values do not give answers as to how to handle a particular situation, but provide a useful framework for understanding conflicts. When moral values are in conflict, the result may be an ethical [dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilemma "Dilemma") or crisis. Sometimes, no good solution to a dilemma in medical ethics exists, and occasionally, the values of the medical community (i.e., the hospital and its staff) conflict with the values of the individual patient, family, or larger non-medical community. Conflicts can also arise between health care providers or among family members. For example, some argue that the principles of autonomy and beneficence clash when patients refuse [blood transfusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_transfusions "Blood transfusions"), considering them life-saving; and truth-telling was not emphasized to a large extent before the HIV era.

## History

### Ancient world

[Prehistoric medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_medicine "Prehistoric medicine") incorporated plants ([herbalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbalism "Herbalism")), animal parts, and minerals. In many cases these materials were used ritually as magical substances by priests, [shamans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamans "Shamans"), or [medicine men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_man "Medicine man"). Well-known spiritual systems include [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism "Animism") (the notion of inanimate objects having spirits), [spiritualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritualism_(beliefs) "Spiritualism (beliefs)") (an appeal to gods or communion with ancestor spirits); [shamanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism "Shamanism") (the vesting of an individual with mystic powers); and [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination "Divination") (magically obtaining the truth). The field of [medical anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_anthropology "Medical anthropology") examines the ways in which culture and society are organized around or impacted by issues of health, health care and related issues.

The earliest known medical texts in the world were found in the ancient [Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria") city of [Ebla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebla "Ebla") and date back to 2500 BCE. Other early records on medicine have been discovered from [ancient Egyptian medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_medicine "Ancient Egyptian medicine"), [Babylonian Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_Medicine "Babylonian Medicine"), [Ayurvedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayurveda "Ayurveda") medicine (in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent")), [classical Chinese medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Chinese_medicine "Classical Chinese medicine") ([Alternative medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_medicine "Alternative medicine")) predecessor to the modern [traditional Chinese medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine "Traditional Chinese medicine")), and [ancient Greek medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_medicine "Ancient Greek medicine") and [Roman medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_in_ancient_Rome "Medicine in ancient Rome").

In Egypt, [Imhotep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imhotep "Imhotep") (3rd millennium BCE) is the first physician in history known by name. The oldest [Egyptian medical text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_medical_papyri "Egyptian medical papyri") is the *[Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahun_Gynaecological_Papyrus "Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus")* from around 2000 BCE, which describes gynaecological diseases. The *[Edwin Smith Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Smith_Papyrus "Edwin Smith Papyrus")* dating back to 1600 BCE is an early work on surgery, while the *[Ebers Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebers_Papyrus "Ebers Papyrus")* dating back to 1500 BCE is akin to a textbook on medicine.

In China, archaeological evidence of medicine in Chinese dates back to the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") [Shang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_dynasty "Shang dynasty"), based on seeds for herbalism and tools presumed to have been used for surgery. The *[Huangdi Neijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huangdi_Neijing "Huangdi Neijing")*, the progenitor of Chinese medicine, is a medical text written beginning in the 2nd century BCE and compiled in the 3rd century.

In India, the oldest known surgical text, the [Sushruta Samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sushruta_Samhita "Sushruta Samhita") written by the surgeon [Sushruta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sushruta "Sushruta"), described numerous surgical operations, including the earliest forms of [plastic surgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_surgery "Plastic surgery") as well as methods of sterilization for surgical instruments. The earliest records of dedicated hospitals come from Mihintale in [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") where evidence of dedicated medicinal treatment facilities for patients are found.

In Greece, the ancient Greek physician [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates "Hippocrates"), the "father of modern medicine", laid the foundation for a rational approach to medicine. Hippocrates introduced the [Hippocratic Oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Oath "Hippocratic Oath") for physicians, which is still relevant and in use today, and was the first to categorize illnesses as [acute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_(medical) "Acute (medical)"), [chronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_(medicine) "Chronic (medicine)"), [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_(epidemiology) "Endemic (epidemiology)") and epidemic, and use terms such as, "exacerbation, [relapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relapse "Relapse"), resolution, crisis, [paroxysm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroxysm "Paroxysm"), peak, and [convalescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convalescence "Convalescence")". The Greek physician [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen "Galen") was also one of the greatest surgeons of the ancient world and performed many audacious operations, including brain and eye surgeries. After the fall of the [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire") and the onset of the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages "Early Middle Ages"), the Greek tradition of medicine went into decline in Western Europe, although it continued uninterrupted in the [Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Empire "Eastern Roman Empire").

Most of our knowledge of ancient [Hebrew medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_medicine "Hebrew medicine") during the [1st millennium BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_millennium_BC "1st millennium BC") comes from the [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah "Torah"), i.e. the Five Books of [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses"), which contain various health related laws and rituals. The Hebrew contribution to the development of modern medicine started in the [Byzantine Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Era "Byzantine Era"), with the physician [Asaph the Jew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asaph_the_Jew "Asaph the Jew").

### Middle Ages

The concept of hospital as institution to offer medical care and possibility of a cure for the patients due to the ideals of Christian charity, rather than just merely a place to die, appeared in the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire").

Although the concept of [uroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uroscopy "Uroscopy") was known to Galen, he did not see the importance of using it to localize the disease. It was under the Byzantines with physicians such of [Theophilus Protospatharius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophilus_Protospatharius "Theophilus Protospatharius") that they realized the potential in uroscopy to determine disease in a time when no microscope or stethoscope existed. That practice eventually spread to the rest of Europe.

After 750 CE, the Muslim world had the works of Hippocrates, Galen and Sushruta translated into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"), and [Islamic physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_medicine "Islamic medicine") engaged in some significant medical research. Notable Islamic medical pioneers include the Persian [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath"), [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna"), who, along with Imhotep and Hippocrates, has also been called the "father of medicine". He wrote *[The Canon of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canon_of_Medicine "The Canon of Medicine")* which became a standard medical text at many medieval European [universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University "University"), considered one of the most famous books in the history of medicine. Others include [Abulcasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Qasim_al-Zahrawi "Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi"), [Avenzoar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Zuhr "Ibn Zuhr"), [Ibn al-Nafis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Nafis "Ibn al-Nafis"), and [Averroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes "Averroes"). [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians "Persians") physician [Rhazes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Zakar%C4%ABya_R%C4%81zi "Muhammad ibn Zakarīya Rāzi") was one of the first to question the Greek theory of [humorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humorism "Humorism"), which nevertheless remained influential in both medieval Western and medieval Islamic medicine. Some volumes of Rhazes's work *Al-Mansuri*, namely "On Surgery" and "A General Book on Therapy", became part of the medical curriculum in European universities. Additionally, he has been described as a doctor's doctor, the father of pediatrics, and a pioneer of ophthalmology. For example, he was the first to recognize the reaction of the eye's pupil to light. The Persian [Bimaristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimaristan "Bimaristan") hospitals were an early example of [public hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_hospital "Public hospital").

In Europe, [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne "Charlemagne") decreed that a hospital should be attached to each cathedral and monastery and the historian [Geoffrey Blainey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Blainey "Geoffrey Blainey") likened the [activities of the Catholic Church in health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_and_health_care "Catholic Church and health care") during the Middle Ages to an early version of a welfare state: "It conducted hospitals for the old and orphanages for the young; hospices for the sick of all ages; places for the lepers; and hostels or inns where pilgrims could buy a cheap bed and meal". It supplied food to the population during famine and distributed food to the poor. This welfare system the church funded through collecting taxes on a large scale and possessing large farmlands and estates. The [Benedictine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine "Benedictine") order was noted for setting up hospitals and infirmaries in their monasteries, growing medical herbs and becoming the chief medical care givers of their districts, as at the great [Abbey of Cluny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_Cluny "Abbey of Cluny"). The Church also established a network of [cathedral schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_schools "Cathedral schools") and universities where medicine was studied. The [Schola Medica Salernitana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schola_Medica_Salernitana "Schola Medica Salernitana") in Salerno, looking to the learning of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks "Greeks") and [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab "Arab") physicians, grew to be the finest medical school in medieval Europe.

However, the fourteenth and fifteenth century [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death") devastated both the Middle East and Europe, and it has even been argued that Western Europe was generally more effective in recovering from the pandemic than the Middle East. In the early modern period, important early figures in medicine and anatomy emerged in Europe, including [Gabriele Falloppio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriele_Falloppio "Gabriele Falloppio") and [William Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harvey "William Harvey").

The major shift in medical thinking was the gradual rejection, especially during the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death") in the 14th and 15th centuries, of what may be called the "traditional authority" approach to science and medicine. This was the notion that because some prominent person in the past said something must be so, then that was the way it was, and anything one observed to the contrary was an anomaly (which was paralleled by a similar shift in European society in general – see [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus "Nicolaus Copernicus")'s rejection of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy")'s theories on astronomy). Physicians like [Vesalius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesalius "Vesalius") improved upon or disproved some of the theories from the past. The main tomes used both by medicine students and expert physicians were *[De materia medica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_materia_medica "De materia medica")* and *[Pharmacopoeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorsch_Pharmacopoeia "Lorsch Pharmacopoeia")*.

[Andreas Vesalius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Vesalius "Andreas Vesalius") was the author of *[De humani corporis fabrica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_humani_corporis_fabrica "De humani corporis fabrica")*, an important book on [human anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_anatomy "Human anatomy"). Bacteria and microorganisms were first observed with a microscope by [Antonie van Leeuwenhoek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek "Antonie van Leeuwenhoek") in 1676, initiating the scientific field [microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbiology "Microbiology"). Independently from Ibn al-Nafis, [Michael Servetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Servetus "Michael Servetus") rediscovered the [pulmonary circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulmonary_circulation "Pulmonary circulation"), but this discovery did not reach the public because it was written down for the first time in the "Manuscript of Paris" in 1546, and later published in the theological work for which he paid with his life in 1553. Later this was described by [Renaldus Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaldus_Columbus "Renaldus Columbus") and [Andrea Cesalpino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Cesalpino "Andrea Cesalpino"). [Herman Boerhaave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Boerhaave "Herman Boerhaave") is sometimes referred to as a "father of physiology" due to his exemplary teaching in Leiden and textbook 'Institutiones medicae' (1708). [Pierre Fauchard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Fauchard "Pierre Fauchard") has been called "the father of modern [dentistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dentistry "Dentistry")".

### Modern

Veterinary medicine was, for the first time, truly separated from human medicine in 1761, when the French veterinarian [Claude Bourgelat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Bourgelat "Claude Bourgelat") founded the world's first veterinary school in Lyon, France. Before this, medical doctors treated both humans and other animals.

Modern scientific [biomedical research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_research "Biomedical research") (where results are testable and [reproducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducible "Reproducible")) began to replace early Western traditions based on herbalism, the Greek "four humours" and other such pre-modern notions. The modern era really began with [Edward Jenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jenner "Edward Jenner")'s discovery of the [smallpox vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox_vaccine "Smallpox vaccine") at the end of the 18th century (inspired by the method of [variolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation "Variolation") originated in ancient China), [Robert Koch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Koch "Robert Koch")'s discoveries around 1880 of the transmission of disease by bacteria, and then the discovery of [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic "Antibiotic") around 1900.

The post-18th century [modernity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernity "Modernity") period brought more groundbreaking researchers from Europe. From [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") and Austria, doctors [Rudolf Virchow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Virchow "Rudolf Virchow"), [Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Conrad_R%C3%B6ntgen "Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen"), [Karl Landsteiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Landsteiner "Karl Landsteiner") and [Otto Loewi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Loewi "Otto Loewi") made notable contributions. In the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom"), [Alexander Fleming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Fleming "Alexander Fleming"), [Joseph Lister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lister,_1st_Baron_Lister "Joseph Lister, 1st Baron Lister"), [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") and [Florence Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale "Florence Nightingale") are considered important. [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain "Spain") doctor [Santiago Ramón y Cajal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_Ram%C3%B3n_y_Cajal "Santiago Ramón y Cajal") is considered the father of modern [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience").

From New Zealand and Australia came [Maurice Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkins "Maurice Wilkins"), [Howard Florey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Florey "Howard Florey"), and [Frank Macfarlane Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Macfarlane_Burnet "Frank Macfarlane Burnet").

Others that did significant work include [William Williams Keen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Williams_Keen "William Williams Keen"), [William Coley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Coley "William Coley"), [James D. Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson "James D. Watson") (United States); [Salvador Luria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Luria "Salvador Luria") (Italy); [Alexandre Yersin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Yersin "Alexandre Yersin") (Switzerland); [Kitasato Shibasaburō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitasato_Shibasabur%C5%8D "Kitasato Shibasaburō") (Japan); [Jean-Martin Charcot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Martin_Charcot "Jean-Martin Charcot"), [Claude Bernard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Bernard "Claude Bernard"), [Paul Broca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Broca "Paul Broca") (France); [Adolfo Lutz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolfo_Lutz "Adolfo Lutz") (Brazil); [Nikolai Korotkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Korotkov "Nikolai Korotkov") (Russia); [Sir William Osler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Osler "William Osler") (Canada); and [Harvey Cushing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Cushing "Harvey Cushing") (United States).

As science and technology developed, medicine became more reliant upon [medications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medication "Medication"). Throughout history and in Europe right until the late 18th century, not only plant products were used as medicine, but also animal (including human) body parts and fluids. [Pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology") developed in part from herbalism and some drugs are still derived from plants ([atropine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atropine "Atropine"), [ephedrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephedrine "Ephedrine"), [warfarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfarin "Warfarin"), [aspirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirin "Aspirin"), [digoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digoxin "Digoxin"), [*vinca* alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinca_alkaloid "Vinca alkaloid"), [taxol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxo "Taxo"), [hyoscine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyoscine_hydrobromide "Hyoscine hydrobromide"), etc.). [Vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine "Vaccine") were discovered by Edward Jenner and [Louis Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pasteur "Louis Pasteur").

The first antibiotic was [arsphenamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsphenamine "Arsphenamine") (Salvarsan) discovered by [Paul Ehrlich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ehrlich "Paul Ehrlich") in 1908 after he observed that bacteria took up toxic dyes that human cells did not. The first major class of antibiotics was the [sulfa drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfonamide_(medicine) "Sulfonamide (medicine)"), derived by German chemists originally from [azo dyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azo_dye "Azo dye").

Pharmacology has become increasingly sophisticated; modern [biotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology "Biotechnology") allows drugs targeted towards specific physiological processes to be developed, sometimes designed for compatibility with the body to reduce [side-effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_effect "Side effect"). [Genomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomics "Genomics") and knowledge of [human genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_genetics "Human genetics") and [human evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_evolution "Human evolution") is having increasingly significant influence on medicine, as the causative [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") of most monogenic [genetic disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_disorder "Genetic disorder") have now been identified, and the development of techniques in [molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology"), [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution"), and [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") are influencing medical technology, practice and decision-making.

Evidence-based medicine is a contemporary movement to establish the most effective [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms "Algorithms") of practice (ways of doing things) through the use of [systematic reviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_review "Systematic review") and [meta-analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-analysis "Meta-analysis"). The movement is facilitated by modern global [information science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_science "Information science"), which allows as much of the available evidence as possible to be collected and analyzed according to standard protocols that are then disseminated to healthcare providers. The [Cochrane Collaboration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochrane_Collaboration "Cochrane Collaboration") leads this movement. A 2001 review of 160 Cochrane systematic reviews revealed that, according to two readers, 21.3% of the reviews concluded insufficient evidence, 20% concluded evidence of no effect, and 22.5% concluded positive effect.

## Quality, efficiency, and access

Evidence-based medicine, prevention of [medical error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_error "Medical error") (and other "[iatrogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iatrogenesis "Iatrogenesis")"), and avoidance of [unnecessary health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unnecessary_health_care "Unnecessary health care") are a priority in modern medical systems. These topics generate significant political and public policy attention, particularly in the United States where healthcare is regarded as excessively costly but [population health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_health "Population health") metrics lag similar nations.

[Health spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_economics#Health_spending "Health economics") varies by country, which results in differences in access to care and [access to medicines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_to_medicines "Access to medicines"). [Health care rationing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_rationing "Health care rationing") varies by country. Most developed countries provide health care to all citizens, with a few exceptions such as the United States where restrictions on [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance "Health insurance") coverage may limit affordability.

## Telemedicine

Telemedicine (also Telehealth) refers to preventive, promotive, and curative care delivery, including remote clinical services, such as diagnosis, monitoring, administration and provider education. The main categories of telehealth:

* [Telenursing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telenursing "Telenursing") is experiencing significant growth globally due to factors such as the need to reduce healthcare costs, an increasing aging and chronically ill population, and expanded healthcare coverage to distant, rural, small, or sparsely populated regions. Telenursing can help address nurse shortages, reduce travel time and distances, and minimize hospital admissions.
* [Telepalliative care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palliative_care "Palliative care") is a remote approach to optimising quality of life and relieving suffering for people with serious, complex and often fatal illnesses. The [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization") (WHO) recommends integrating palliative care as early as possible for any chronic and fatal illness. Telepalliative care typically utilizes telecommunication technologies like video conferencing, messaging for follow-ups, and digital symptom assessments through questionnaires that generate alerts for healthcare professionals.
* [Telepharmacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telepharmacy "Telepharmacy") involves delivering pharmaceutical care via telecommunications to patients in locations where direct contact with a pharmacist may not be possible or difficult.
* [Telepsychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telepsychiatry "Telepsychiatry") (telemental health) uses telecommunications technology to provide remote psychiatric care for individuals with mental health conditions.
* [Telepsychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telepsychology "Telepsychology") is the use of communication technology for the remote administration of psychological tests and psychotherapy.
* [Teleneurotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotherapy "Neurotherapy") utilizes computers and communications technology to deliver neurotherapy remotely. Research indicates that systematic physical stimuli from standard electronic devices, such as tablets with headphones, may treat injured nervous systems online by modulating neuronal plasticity. Evidence suggests that teleneurotherapy could enhance neurological treatment if it incorporates the therapeutic effect of a systematic abiotic impact of physical forces with key parameters of mother-fetus interaction. Recent research has shown therapeutic effects when implementing the APIN method in the online treatment of patients with various neurological conditions.
* Telenutrition refers to the use of video conferencing or telephony to provide online consultations by nutritionists or dieticians.
* [Telerehabilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telerehabilitation "Telerehabilitation") (or e-rehabilitation) is the delivery of rehabilitation services over telecommunication networks and the Internet. Most services fall into two categories: clinical assessment (evaluating the patient's functional abilities in their environment) and clinical therapy.
