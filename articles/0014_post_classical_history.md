# Post-classical history

In [world history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history "Human history"), **post-classical history** refers to the period from about 500 CE to about 1450 or 1500 CE, roughly corresponding to the [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") following the decline of the [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire") [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"). The post-classical period is characterized by the expansion of certain civilizations geographically, by ongoing wars fought over land, resources, and religion, and by the development of trade networks between often-distant civilizations. This period is also called—with various implications and emphases—the **medieval era**, the **post-antiquity era**, the **post-ancient era**, or the **pre-modern era**.

In [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia") and the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") during this time, the [spread of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam "Spread of Islam") helped produce a series of [caliphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate") which fostered the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age"), leading to advances in [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Science in the medieval Islamic world") and greater trade between those in the Asian, [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"), and European continents. [East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia") experienced the entrenchment of the power of a unitary and [Imperial China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_China#Imperial_China "History of China"), the dynastic governance and culture of which influenced Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Religions such as [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") and [neo-Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Confucianism "Neo-Confucianism") spread in the region, while Christianity became entrenched in Europe and increasingly elsewhere. [Gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder "Gunpowder") was developed in China during the post-classical era. The [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire") conquered and controlled much of Europe and Asia, permitting more trade and cultural and intellectual exchange between the two regions.

In total, the [population of the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population_estimates "World population estimates") broadly doubled in this period of history: from approximately 210 million in 500 CE to some 461 million in 1500 CE. The population generally grew slowly throughout the period, with mortality generally high and rapid major declines due to events including the [Plague of Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian "Plague of Justinian"), the [Mongol invasions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_and_conquests "Mongol invasions and conquests"), and the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death").

## Historiography

### Terminology and periodization

Post-classical history is a [periodization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodization "Periodization") used by historians employing a [world history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field) "World history (field)") approach to history, specifically the school developed during the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Outside of world history, the term is also sometimes used to avoid erroneous preconceptions around the terms *[Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages")*, *Medieval Period*, and the *[Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Ages_(historiography) "Dark Ages (historiography)")* (see [medievalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medievalism "Medievalism")), though the application of the term *post-classical* on a global scale is also problematic, and it may likewise be [Eurocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism "Eurocentrism"). Academic publications sometimes use the terms *post-classical* and *late antiquity* synonymously to describe the history of Western Eurasia between 250 and 800 CE.

The post-classical period corresponds roughly to the period from 500 CE to 1450 CE. Beginning and ending dates might vary depending on the region, with the period beginning at the end of the previous classical period: [Han China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") (ending in 220 CE), the [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire") (in 476 CE), the [Gupta Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_Empire "Gupta Empire") (in 543 CE), and the [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire") (in 651 CE).

The post-classical period is one of the five or six major periods world historians use:

1. early civilization,
2. [classical societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history "Ancient history"),
3. post-classical
4. [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period "Early modern period"),
5. [long nineteenth century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_nineteenth_century "Long nineteenth century"), and
6. [contemporary or modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_history "Modern history"). (Sometimes the nineteenth century and modern are combined.)

Although *post-classical* is synonymous with the Middle Ages of Western Europe, the term *post-classical* is not necessarily a member of the traditional [tripartite periodization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages#Historiography_and_periodization "Late Middle Ages") of Western European history into *classical*, *middle*, and *modern*.

### Approaches

The historical field of [world history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field) "World history (field)"), which looks at common themes occurring across multiple cultures and regions, has enjoyed extensive development since the 1980s. However, World History research has tended to focus on [early modern globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-globalization "Proto-globalization") (beginning around 1500) and subsequent developments, and it views post-classical history as mainly pertaining to [Afro-Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia "Afro-Eurasia"). Historians recognize the difficulties of creating a periodization and identifying common themes that include not only this region but also, for example, the Americas, since they had little contact with Afro-Eurasia before the [Columbian exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange "Columbian exchange"). Thus researchers around the year 2020 emphasized that "a global history of the period between 500 and 1500 is still wanting" and that "historians have only just begun to embark on a global history of the Middle Ages".

For many regions of the world, there are well-established histories. Although [medieval studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_studies "Medieval studies") in Europe tended in the 19th century to focus on creating histories for individual nation-states, much 20th-century research focused, successfully, on creating an integrated history of medieval Europe. The Islamic World likewise has a rich regional historiography, ranging from the 14th-century [Ibn Khaldun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun "Ibn Khaldun") to the 20th-century [Marshall Hodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Hodgson "Marshall Hodgson") and beyond. Correspondingly, research into the network of commercial hubs which enabled goods and ideas to move between China in the East and the Atlantic islands in the West—which can be called the [early history of globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_globalization "Archaic globalization")—is fairly advanced; one key historian in this field is [Janet Abu-Lughod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Abu-Lughod "Janet Abu-Lughod"). Understanding of communication within sub-Saharan Africa or the Americas is, by contrast, far more limited.

Around the 2010s, therefore, researchers began to explore the possibilities of writing history covering the Old World, where human activities were fairly interconnected, and establish its relationship with other cultural spheres, such as the Americas and Oceania. In the assessment of [James Belich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Belich_(historian) "James Belich (historian)"), [John Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Darwin_(historian) "John Darwin (historian)"), Margret Frenz, and [Chris Wickham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Wickham "Chris Wickham"),

> Global history may be boundless, but global historians are not. Global history cannot usefully mean the history of everything, everywhere, all the time. [...] Three approaches [...] seem to us to have real promise. One is global history as the pursuit of significant historical problems across time, space, and specialism. This can sometimes be characterized as ['comparative' history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_history "Comparative history"). [...] Another is connectedness, including transnational relationships. [...] The third approach is the study of globalization [...]. Globalization is a term that needs to be rescued from the present, and salvaged for the past. To define it as always encompassing the whole planet is to mistake the current outcome for a very ancient process.

A number of commentators have pointed to the history of the Earth's [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate") as a useful approach to World History in the Middle Ages, noting that certain climate events had effects on all human populations.

## Global trends

The post-classical era saw several common developments or themes. There was the expansion and growth of civilization into new geographic areas; the rise and/or spread of the three major world, or missionary, religions; and a period of rapidly expanding trade and trade networks. While scholastic emphasis has remained on Eurasia, there is a growing effort to examine the effects of these global trends on other places. In describing geographic zones, historians have identified three large self-contained world regions: Afro-Eurasia, the Americas, and Oceania.

### Growth of civilization

First was the expansion and growth of civilization into new geographic areas across [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia"), [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"), [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"), [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica"), and western [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America "South America"). However, as noted by world historian [Peter N. Stearns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_N._Stearns "Peter N. Stearns"), there were no common global political trends during the post-classical period; rather, it was a period of loosely organized states and other developments, but no common political patterns emerged. In Asia, China continued its historic [dynastic cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynastic_cycle "Dynastic cycle") and became more complex, improving its bureaucracy. The creation of the Islamic empires established a new power in the Middle East, North Africa, the Caucasus, and Central Asia. The [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire "Mali Empire") and [Songhai Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_Empire "Songhai Empire") were formed in West Africa. The fall of Roman civilization not only left a power vacuum for the Mediterranean and Europe, but forced certain areas to build what some historians might call new civilizations entirely. An entirely different political system was applied in Western Europe (i.e., [feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism "Feudalism")), as well as a different society (i.e., [manorialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorialism "Manorialism")). However, the once Eastern Roman Empire (Byzantium) retained many features of old Rome, as well as Greek and Persian similarities. Kievan Rus' and subsequently Russia began development in Eastern Europe as well. In the isolated Americas, the [Mississippian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_culture "Mississippian culture") spread in North America, and Mesoamerica saw the building of the [Aztec Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_Empire "Aztec Empire"), while the [Andean region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_civilizations "Andean civilizations") of South America saw the establishment of the [Wari Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wari_Empire "Wari Empire") first and the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire "Inca Empire") later. In Oceania, ancestors of modern Polynesians were established in village communities by the 6th century; a gradual intensification of complexity took place. In the 13th century, complex states were established, most notably the [Tuʻi Tonga Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu%CA%BBi_Tonga_Empire "Tuʻi Tonga Empire"), which collected tribute from many island chains in the greater region.

### Spread of universal religions

Religion that envisaged the possibility that all humans could be included in a universal order had emerged already in the first millennium BCE, particularly with [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism"). In the following millennium, Buddhism was joined by two other major, universalizing, missionary religions, both developing from [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism"): [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") and [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam"). By the end of the period, these three religions were, between them, widespread and often politically dominant across the Old World.

* Buddhism spread from India into China and flourished there briefly before using it as a hub to spread to Japan, Korea, and Vietnam; a similar effect occurred with Confucian revivalism in the later centuries.
* Christianity had become the [state church of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_as_the_Roman_state_religion "Christianity as the Roman state religion") in 380, and continued spreading into northern and eastern Europe during the post-classical period at the expense of belief systems that Christians labelled [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism "Paganism"). An attempt was even made to incur upon the Middle East during the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades").[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] The split of the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") in Western Europe and the [Eastern Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") in Eastern Europe encouraged religious and cultural diversity in Eurasia.
* Islam began between 610 and 632, with a series of revelations to [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad"). It helped unify the warring [Bedouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedouin "Bedouin") clans of the [Arabian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula "Arabian Peninsula") and, through a rapid series of [Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests"), became established to the west across [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_conquest_of_Egypt "Arab conquest of Egypt"), the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Spain "Muslim conquest of Spain"), and parts of [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Africa "Islam in Africa"), and to the east across [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia "Muslim conquest of Persia"), [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_settlement_of_the_Tarim_Basin "Turkic settlement of the Tarim Basin"), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquests_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent"), and [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam_in_Indonesia "Spread of Islam in Indonesia").

Outside of Eurasia, religion or otherwise a veneration of the supernatural was also used to reinforce power structures, articulate worldviews, and create foundational myths for society. Mesoamerican cosmological narratives are an example of this.

### Trade and communication

Finally, communication and trade across [Afro-Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia "Afro-Eurasia") increased rapidly. The [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road") continued to spread cultures and ideas through trade. Communication spread throughout Europe, Asia, and Africa. Trade networks were established between western Europe, Byzantium, early Russia, the Islamic Empires, and the [Far Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_East "Far East") civilizations. In Africa, the earlier introduction of the [camel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel "Camel") allowed for a new and eventually large [trans-Saharan trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade "Trans-Saharan trade"), which connected Sub-Saharan West Africa to Eurasia. The Islamic Empires adopted many Greek, Roman, and Indian advances and spread them through the Islamic sphere of influence, [allowing these developments to reach Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world_contributions_to_Medieval_Europe "Islamic world contributions to Medieval Europe"), North and West Africa, and Central Asia. Islamic sea trade helped connect these areas, including those in the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean") and in the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean "Mediterranean"), replacing Byzantium in the latter region. The Christian Crusades into the Middle East (as well as Muslim Spain and [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily "Sicily")) [brought Islamic science, technology, and goods to Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world_contributions_to_Medieval_Europe "Islamic world contributions to Medieval Europe"). Western trade into East Asia was pioneered by [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo "Marco Polo"). Importantly, China began to influence regions like Japan, Korea, and Vietnam through trade and conquest. Finally, the growth of the [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire") in Central Asia established safe trade, which allowed goods, cultures, ideas, and disease to spread between Asia, Europe, and Africa.

The Americas had their own trade network, but here trade was restricted by range and scope. The Mayan network spread across Mesoamerica but lacked direct connections to the complex societies of South and North America, and these zones remained separate from one another.

In [Oceania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceania "Oceania"), some of the island chains of [Polynesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesia "Polynesia") engaged in trade with one another. For instance, with outrigger canoes, long-distance communication of over 2,300 miles between [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_(island) "Hawaii (island)") and [Tahiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahiti "Tahiti") was maintained for centuries before its disruption and separation. Meanwhile, in [Melanesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanesia "Melanesia") there is evidence of exchanges between mainland [Papua New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea "Papua New Guinea") and the [Trobriand Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trobriand_Islands "Trobriand Islands") off its coast, most likely for [obsidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian "Obsidian"). Populations moved westward until 1200, after which the network dissolved into much smaller economies.

### Climate

During post-classical times, there is evidence that many regions of the world were affected similarly by global climate conditions; however, direct effects in temperature and precipitation varied by region. According to the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Panel_on_Climate_Change "Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change"), changes did not all occur at once. Generally, however, studies found that temperatures were relatively warmer in the 11th century, but colder by the early 17th century. The degree of climate change which occurred in all regions across the world is uncertain, as is whether such changes were all part of a global trend. Climate trends appear to be more recognizable in the [Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere "Northern Hemisphere") than in the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere "Southern Hemisphere"); however, there are instances where climate in areas without written records has been estimated. Historians now believe the Southern Hemisphere became colder between 950 and 1250.

There are shorter climate periods that could be said to roughly account for large-scale climate trends in the post-classical period. These include the [Late Antique Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Antique_Little_Ice_Age "Late Antique Little Ice Age"), the [Medieval Warm Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Warm_Period "Medieval Warm Period"), and the [Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age "Little Ice Age"). The [extreme weather events of 536–537](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather_events_of_535%E2%80%93536 "Extreme weather events of 535–536") were likely initiated by the eruption of the [Lake Ilopango caldera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Ilopango "Lake Ilopango") in [El Salvador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Salvador "El Salvador"). Sulfate emitted into the air initiated global cooling, migrations, and crop failures worldwide, possibly intensifying an already cooler time period. Records show that the world's average temperature remained colder for at least a century afterward.

The Medieval Warm Period from 950 to 1250 occurred mostly in the Northern Hemisphere, causing warmer summers in many areas; the high temperatures would only be surpassed by the global warming of the 20th/21st centuries. It has been hypothesized that the warmer temperatures allowed the Norse to colonize Greenland, due to ice-free waters. Outside of Europe, there is evidence of warming conditions, including higher temperatures in China and major North American droughts which adversely affected numerous cultures.

After 1250, glaciers began to expand in Greenland, affecting its [thermohaline circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermohaline_circulation "Thermohaline circulation"), and cooling the entire North Atlantic. In the 14th century, the growing season in Europe became unreliable; meanwhile, in China the cultivation of oranges was driven southward by colder temperatures. Especially in Europe, the Little Ice Age had great cultural ramifications. It persisted until the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"), long after the post-classical period. Its causes are unclear: possible explanations include [sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_maximum "Solar maximum"), [orbital cycles of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_orbit "Earth's orbit"), [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano "Volcano"), [ocean circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermohaline_circulation "Thermohaline circulation"), and man-made [population decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_decline "Population decline").

### Feudalism

In the context of global history, the label of [feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism "Feudalism") has been used to describe any agricultural society where central authority broke down to be replaced by a warrior [aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy "Aristocracy"). Feudal societies are characterized by reliance on personal relationships with military elites, rather than a bureaucracy with a state-supported professional standing army. The label of feudalism has thus been used to describe many areas of Eurasia including [medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), the Islamic [iqta'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iqta%27 "Iqta'") system, Indian feudalism, and [Heian Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period "Heian period"). Some world historians generalize that societies can be called feudal if authority was fragmented, with a set of obligations between vassal and lord. After the 8th century, feudalism became more common across Europe. Even Byzantium, which had inherited the government of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"), chose to devolve its military obligations into [themes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_(Byzantine_district) "Theme (Byzantine district)") to increase the number of soldiers and ships available for military service during times of crisis. There were similarities between European feudalism and the Islamic [iqta'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iqta%27 "Iqta'"), as both featured landed classes of mounted warriors whose titles were granted by a monarch or sultan. Because of these similarities, it was common for societal structures to be preserved in the face of religious upheaval; for instance, after the Islamic [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate") conquered large portions of India, it imposed higher taxes but otherwise left local feudal structures in place.

Though most of Eurasia adopted feudalism and similar systems during this era, [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_China "Imperial China") employed a centralized bureaucracy throughout much of the post-classical period, particularly after 1000. A major factor that distinguished China from other regions was that local leaders were reluctant to identify themselves by their current location; instead, they typically displayed an ambition to unite the country in times of disunity.

Beyond a broad generalization, the usefulness of the term "feudalism" is debated by contemporary historians, as the daily functions of feudalism sometimes differed greatly between world regions. Comparisons between feudal Europe and post-classical Japan have been particularly controversial. Throughout the 20th century, historians often compared medieval Europe to post-classical Japan. More recently, it has been argued that, until roughly 1400, Japan balanced its decentralized military power with more centralized forms of imperial (governmental) and monastic (religious) authority. Only in the [Sengoku period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sengoku_period "Sengoku period") did there come to be fully decentralized power dominated by private military leaders. Still other historians reject the term feudalism outright, challenging its ability to usefully describe societies either within or outside of medieval Europe.

### Mongol Empire

The Mongol Empire, which existed during the 13th and 14th centuries, was the largest [continuous land empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_empires#Largest_empires_by_land_area "List of largest empires") in history. Originating in the [steppes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steppe "Steppe") of Central Asia, the Mongol Empire eventually stretched from [Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe "Central Europe") to the [Sea of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Japan "Sea of Japan"), extending northward into [Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberia "Siberia"), eastward and southward into the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"), [Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia "Mainland Southeast Asia"), and the [Iranian Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Plateau "Iranian Plateau"), and westward as far as the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") and Arabia.

The Mongol Empire emerged from the unification of nomadic tribes in the [Mongolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolia "Mongolia") homeland under the leadership of [Genghis Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genghis_Khan "Genghis Khan"), who was proclaimed ruler of all Mongols in 1206. The empire grew rapidly under his rule and then under his descendants, who sent [invasions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions "Mongol invasions") in every direction. The vast transcontinental empire connected [east](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_world "Eastern world") and [west](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world") with an enforced *[Pax Mongolica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Mongolica "Pax Mongolica")*, allowing trade, technologies, commodities, and ideologies to be disseminated and exchanged across [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia "Eurasia").

The empire began to split due to wars over succession, as the grandchildren of Genghis Khan disputed whether the royal line should follow from his son and initial heir [Ögedei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96gedei_Khan "Ögedei Khan"), or one of his other sons such as [Tolui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolui "Tolui"), [Chagatai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagatai_Khan "Chagatai Khan"), or [Jochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jochi "Jochi"). After [Möngke Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6ngke_Khan "Möngke Khan") died, rival *[kurultai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurultai "Kurultai")* councils simultaneously elected different successors, the brothers [Ariq Böke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariq_B%C3%B6ke "Ariq Böke") and [Kublai Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kublai_Khan "Kublai Khan"), who then not only fought each other in the [Toluid Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toluid_Civil_War "Toluid Civil War"), but also dealt with challenges from descendants of other sons of Genghis. Kublai successfully took power, but civil war ensued as Kublai sought unsuccessfully to regain control of the [Chagatayid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagatai_khans "Chagatai khans") and [Ögedeid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_%C3%96gedei "House of Ögedei") families.

The [Battle of Ain Jalut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ain_Jalut "Battle of Ain Jalut") in 1260 marked the high point of the [Mongol conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquests "Mongol conquests") and was the first time a Mongol advance had ever been beaten back in direct combat on the battlefield. Though the Mongols launched many more invasions into the Levant, briefly occupying it and raiding as far as Gaza after a decisive victory at the [Battle of Wadi al-Khazandar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wadi_al-Khazandar "Battle of Wadi al-Khazandar") in 1299, they withdrew due to various geopolitical factors.

By the time of Kublai's death in 1294, the Mongol Empire had fractured into four separate khanates or empires, each pursuing its own separate interests and objectives: the [Golden Horde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Horde "Golden Horde") khanate in the northwest; the [Chagatai Khanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagatai_Khanate "Chagatai Khanate") in the west; the [Ilkhanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkhanate "Ilkhanate") in the southwest; and the [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty "Yuan dynasty") based in modern-day [Beijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing "Beijing"). In 1304, the three western khanates briefly accepted the nominal suzerainty of the Yuan dynasty, but it was later overthrown by the [Han Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_Chinese "Han Chinese") [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty "Ming dynasty") in 1368. The Genghisid rulers returned to the Mongolian homeland and continued rule in the [Northern Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Yuan_dynasty "Northern Yuan dynasty"). All of the original Mongol Khanates collapsed by 1500, but smaller successor states remained independent until the 1700s. Descendants of [Chagatai Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagatai_Khan "Chagatai Khan") created the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") that ruled much of India in early modern times.

The conquests and the interactions the Mongol Empire had with western Eurasia are one of the more comprehensively researched areas for historians looking to define a globalized Middle Ages.

### Silk Road

The Silk Road was a Eurasian trade route that played a large role in global communication and interaction. It stimulated cultural exchange; encouraged the learning of new languages; resulted in the trade of many goods, such as silk, gold, and spices; and also spread religion and disease. It is even claimed by some historians—such as [Andre Gunder Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andre_Gunder_Frank "Andre Gunder Frank"), [William Hardy McNeill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hardy_McNeill "William Hardy McNeill"), [Jerry H. Bentley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_H._Bentley "Jerry H. Bentley"), and [Marshall Hodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Hodgson "Marshall Hodgson")—that the Afro-Eurasian world was loosely united culturally, and that the Silk Road was fundamental to this unity. This major trade route began with the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") of China, connecting it to the Roman Empire and any regions in between or nearby. At this time, Central Asia exported horses, [wool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool "Wool"), and [jade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jade "Jade") into China for the latter's silk; the Romans would trade for the Chinese commodity as well, offering wine in return. The Silk Road would often decline and rise again in trade from the Iron Age to the post-classical era. Following one such decline, it was reopened in Central Asia by Han dynasty general [Ban Chao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_Chao "Ban Chao") during the 1st century.

There were vulnerabilities as well to changing political situations. The rise of Islam changed the Silk Road, because Muslim rulers generally closed the Silk Road to [Christian Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") to an extent that Europe would be cut off from Asia for centuries. Specifically, the political developments that affected the Silk Road included the emergence of the Turks, the political movements of the Byzantine and [Sasanian Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire"), and the rise of the Arabs, among others.

The Silk Road flourished again in the 13th century during the reign of the Mongol Empire, which through conquest had brought stability in Central Asia comparable to the [Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana "Pax Romana"). It was claimed by a Muslim historian that Central Asia was peaceful and safe to traverse.

> (Central Asia) enjoyed such a peace that a man might have journeyed from the land of sunrise to the land of sunset with a golden platter upon his head without suffering the least violence from anyone.

As such, trade and communication between Europe, East Asia, South Asia, and West Asia required little effort. Handicraft production, art, and scholarship prospered, and wealthy merchants enjoyed cosmopolitan cities. Notable travelers—including [Ibn Battuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta "Ibn Battuta"), [Rabban Bar Sauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabban_Bar_Sauma "Rabban Bar Sauma"), and [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo "Marco Polo")—traveled across North Africa and Eurasia freely; those who left accounts of their experiences inspired future adventurers. The Silk Road was also a major factor in spreading religion across Afro-Eurasia. Muslim teachings from Arabia and Persia reached East Asia. Buddhism spread from India to China to Central Asia. One significant development in the spread of Buddhism was the carving of the [Gandhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara "Gandhara") School in the cities of [ancient Taxila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxila "Taxila") and [Peshawar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshawar "Peshawar"), allegedly in the mid-1st century. In addition to commercial travel was the esteem of [pilgrimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrimage "Pilgrimage") that existed across all of Afro-Eurasia; in the words of world historian [R. I. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._I._Moore "R. I. Moore"), "if any single institution 'made' the Eurasian Middle Ages it was pilgrimage."

Nevertheless, after the 15th century, the Silk Road disappeared from regular use. This was primarily a result of the growing sea travel pioneered by Europeans, which allowed the trade of goods by sailing around the southern tip of Africa and into the Indian Ocean.

The route was vulnerable to spreading plague. The [Plague of Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian "Plague of Justinian") originated in East Africa and had a major outbreak in Europe in 542, causing the deaths of a quarter of the Mediterranean's population. Trade between Europe, Africa, and Asia along the route was at least partially responsible for spreading the plague. Eight centuries later, the Silk Road trade played a role in spreading the infamous [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death"). The disease, spread by rats, was carried by merchant ships sailing across the Mediterranean that brought the plague back to Sicily, causing an [epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemic "Epidemic") in 1347.

### Plague and disease

In the Eurasian world, disease was an inescapable part of daily life. Europe in particular suffered minor outbreaks of disease every decade during the period. Using both land and sea routes, devastating pandemics could spread far beyond their initial focal point. Tracking the origin of massive [bubonic plagues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague "Bubonic plague") and their potential spread between Eastern and Western Eurasia has been academically contentious. Besides bubonic plague, other diseases—including [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox "Smallpox")—also spread across cultural regions.

#### The first plague

The [first plague pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_plague_pandemic "First plague pandemic") caused by [Yersinia pestis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis "Yersinia pestis") began with the 541–549 Plague of Justinian. The origin of the plague appears to have been the [Tian Shan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tian_Shan "Tian Shan") mountains in [Kyrgyzstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyzstan "Kyrgyzstan"). But the origin of the 541–549 epidemic remains uncertain: some historians postulate East Africa as a possible geographical origin.

There is no record of a disease with the characteristics of Yersinia pestis breaking out in China before its appearance in [Pelusium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelusium "Pelusium"), [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Egypt "Byzantine Egypt"). The plague spread to Europe and West Asia, with a possible spread into East Asia. Established urban civilizations were massively depopulated; the economies and social fabric of established empires were severely destabilized. Rural societies, while still facing horrific death tolls, saw fewer socioeconomic effects. In addition, no evidence has been found of bubonic plague in India before 1600. Nevertheless, it is likely that the trauma of disease (and other natural disasters) was a major cause of profound religious and political changes in Eurasia. Different authorities reacted to disease outbreaks with strategies that they believed would best protect their power. The Catholic Church in France spoke of healing miracles; Confucian bureaucrats asserted that sudden deaths of Chinese emperors represented the loss of a dynasty's [Mandate of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_of_Heaven "Mandate of Heaven"), shifting blame away from themselves. The severe loss of manpower in the Byzantine and Sasanian Empires contributed to early Muslim conquests in the region. In the long term, overland trade in Eurasia diminished, as coastal Indian Ocean trade became more frequent. There were recurrent aftershocks of the Plague of Justinian until around 750, after which many nations saw an economic recovery.

#### Second plague pandemic until 1500

Six centuries later, a relative (but not a direct descendant) of Yersinia Pestis rose to afflict Eurasia: the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death"). The first instance of the [second plague pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_plague_pandemic "Second plague pandemic") was between 1347 and 1351. It killed variously between 25% and 50% of populations. Traditionally, many historians believed the Black Death started in China and was then spread westward by invading Mongols who inadvertently carried infected fleas and rats with them. Although there is no concrete historical evidence for this theory, the plague is considered endemic on the steppe. Currently, there is extensive historiography of the Black Death's effects in Europe and the Islamic world, but beyond Western Eurasia, direct evidence for Black Death's presence is lacking. The Bulletin of the History of Medicine explored the potential linking of known 14th-century epidemics in Asia with the plague. One example is the [Deccan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_Plateau "Deccan Plateau"), where much of the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate")'s army suddenly died of a sickness in 1334. As this was 15 years before Europe's Black Death, and there is little detail about the symptoms, it is unlikely that this was an instance of bubonic plague. Meanwhile, Yuan China suffered from major epidemics in the mid-14th century, including a recorded 90% death rate in Hebei Province. As with the Deccan event, surviving accounts do not describe symptoms; so historians are left to speculate. Perhaps these outbreaks were not the Black Death but instead some other disease already common to East Asia at the time, such as [typhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhus "Typhus"), [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox "Smallpox"), or [dysentery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysentery "Dysentery"). Compared to Western reactions to the Black Death, Chinese records that do mention the epidemics are relatively muted, indicating that epidemics were a routine occurrence. Historians consider the hypothesis of a Chinese origin of a westward-moving plague unlikely given the fragmentation of the Mongol Empire and the 5,000-mile journey between [China proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_proper "China proper") and [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimea "Crimea") through sparsely populated Central Asia.

The aftershocks of the plague continued to affect populations well into the early modern period. In Western Europe, the devastating loss of people created lasting changes. Wage labor began to rise in Western Europe, and there was more emphasis on labor-saving machines and mechanisms. Slavery, which had almost vanished from medieval Europe, returned and was one of the reasons for early Portuguese exploration after 1400. The adoption of [Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_numerals "Arabic numerals") may have been partially caused by the plague. Importantly, many economies became specialized, producing only certain goods, seeking expansion elsewhere for exotic resources and slave labor. While typically Western European expansion as a result of the Black Death is most discussed, Islamic countries including the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") also partook in land-based expansionism and used their own slave trade.

### Science

The term *post-classical science* is often used in academic circles and in college courses to combine the study of [medieval European science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_science_in_the_Middle_Ages "European science in the Middle Ages") and [medieval Islamic science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Science in the medieval Islamic world") due to their interactions with one another. However, scientific knowledge also spread westward by trade and war from Eastern Eurasia, particularly from China through Arabs. The Islamic world also took medical knowledge from [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_influence_on_Islamic_science "Indian influence on Islamic science").

In the Western world and in Islamic realms, much emphasis was placed on preserving the rationalist Greek tradition of figures such as [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"). In the context of science within Islam, there are questions as to whether Islamic scientists simply preserved accomplishments from [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") or built upon earlier Greek advances. Regardless, classical European science was brought back to the Christian kingdoms due to the experience of the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades").

As a result of Persian trade in China and the battle of the [Talas River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Talas "Battle of Talas"), Chinese innovations entered the Islamic intellectual world. These include advances in astronomy and in [papermaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_paper "History of paper"). Paper-making spread through the Islamic world as far west as [Islamic Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus "Al-Andalus"), before paper-making was acquired for Europe by the [Reconquista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista "Reconquista"). There is debate about the [transmission of gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_gunpowder#Spread_throughout_Eurasia_and_Africa "History of gunpowder") regarding whether the Mongols introduced [Chinese gunpowder weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Song_dynasty#Gunpowder_warfare "Science and technology of the Song dynasty") to Europe or whether gunpowder weapons were independently invented in Europe. In the Mongol Empire, information from diverse cultures was brought together for large projects: for instance, in 1303 the Mongol [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty "Yuan dynasty") combined Chinese and Islamic cartography to make a map that likely included all of Eurasia including western Europe. This "Eurasia map" is now lost, but it influenced Chinese and Korean geographical knowledge centuries later. It is apparent that within Eurasia transfer of information between world cultures did occur, usually through translations of written documents.

### Literature and the arts

Within Eurasia, there were four major civilization groups that had literate cultures and created literature and the arts, including Europe, West Asia, South Asia, and East Asia. Southeast Asia could be a possible fifth category but was influenced heavily by both South and East Asian literary cultures. All four cultures in post-classical times used [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry "Poetry"), [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama "Drama"), and [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose"). Throughout the period and until the 19th century, poetry was the dominant form of literary expression. In West Asia, South Asia, Europe, and China, great poetic works often used figurative language. Examples include the [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") *[Shakuntala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakuntala_(play) "Shakuntala (play)")*, the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") *[Thousand and One Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights "One Thousand and One Nights")*, [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") *[Beowulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beowulf "Beowulf"),* and works by the Chinese [Du Fu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Fu "Du Fu") and the Persian [Rumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumi "Rumi"). In Japan, prose uniquely thrived more than in other geographic areas. The *[Tale of Genji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_Genji "The Tale of Genji")* is considered the world's first realistic novel written in the 9th century.

Musically, most regions of the world only used [monophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophony "Monophony") melodies as opposed to [harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony "Harmony"). Medieval Europe was the lone exception to this rule, developing harmonic music in the 14th/15th century as musical culture transitioned from sacred music (meant for the church) to secular music. South Asian and West Asian music were similar to each other for their use of [microtones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microtonal_music "Microtonal music"). East Asian music shared some similarities with European music by using twelve tones and employing scales, but differed in the number of scales used- 5 for the former and seven for the latter

## History by region

### Africa

During the post-classical era, [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") was both culturally and politically affected by the introduction of Islam and the Arab empires. This was especially true in the north, the [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan_(region) "Sudan (region)"), and the east coast. However, this conversion was neither complete nor uniform among different areas, and the lower classes hardly changed their beliefs at all. Prior to the migration and conquest of Muslims into Africa, much of the continent was dominated by diverse societies of varying sizes and complexities. These were ruled by kings or councils of elders who would control their constituents in a variety of ways. Most of these peoples practiced spiritual, animistic religions. Africa was culturally separated between Saharan Africa (which consisted of [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa") and the [Sahara Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara "Sahara")) and [sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa") (everything south of the Sahara). Sub-Saharan Africa was further divided into the Sudan, which covered everything north of [Central Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Africa "Central Africa"), including [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"). The area south of the Sudan was primarily occupied by the [Bantu peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_peoples "Bantu peoples") who spoke the [Bantu language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_language "Bantu language"). From 1100 onward, [Christian Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") and the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world") became dependent on Africa for gold.

After approximately 650, urbanization expanded for the first time beyond the ancient kingdoms of [Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum "Kingdom of Aksum") and [Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia"). African civilizations can be divided into three categories based on religion:

* Christian civilizations on the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa "Horn of Africa")
* Islamic civilizations which formed in the [Niger River Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger_River "Niger River") and on the [Swahili Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_coast "Swahili coast")
* [Traditional societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_society "Traditional society") which adhered to [native African religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religions "Traditional African religions")

Sub-Saharan Africa was part of two large, separate trading networks, the [trans-Saharan trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade "Trans-Saharan trade") that bridged commerce between West and North Africa. Due to the huge profits from trade, native African Islamic empires arose, including those of [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire "Ghana Empire"), Mali, and Songhai. In the 14th century, [Mansa Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_Musa "Mansa Musa") of Mali may have been the wealthiest person of his time. Within Mali, the city of [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu "Timbuktu") was an international center of science and well known throughout the Islamic world, particularly for the [University of Sankoré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankor%C3%A9_Madrasah "Sankoré Madrasah"). East Africa was part of the [Indian Ocean trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade "Indian Ocean trade") network, which included both Arab ruled Islamic cities on the East African Coast such as [Mombasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mombasa "Mombasa") and traditional cities such as [Great Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Zimbabwe "Great Zimbabwe"), which exported gold, copper, and ivory to markets in the Middle East, South Asia, and Southeast Asia.

Following the [Muslim conquest of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Egypt "Muslim conquest of Egypt") in the 7th century, a few organized, large-scale nationwide native rebellions erupted before [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt_in_the_Middle_Ages "Egypt in the Middle Ages")'s transformation to an [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") state. The most significant uprising was the [Bashmurian revolts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashmurian_revolts "Bashmurian revolts"), taking place between 720 and 832. Later, Egypt experienced a renaissance as a pivotal centre of the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world "Islamic world"), following the establishment of the [Tulunid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulunids "Tulunids"), marking Egypt's independence from Abbasid rule in the 9th century. Following the founding of [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo"), which became the [Egyptian capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_historical_capitals_of_Egypt "List of historical capitals of Egypt") and the seat of the [Fatimid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate "Fatimid Caliphate") in 973, the city flourished, witnessing the construction of [Al-Azhar Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azhar_Mosque "Al-Azhar Mosque") and its [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azhar_University "Al-Azhar University") (980), one of the oldest universities in the world.
By the 11th century, Egypt became a global trading power linking the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea") to the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean"), maintaining its regional influence. It was also a beacon of [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge "Knowledge") in the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world "Islamic world"), a period marked by the emergence of numerous Egyptian scientists and scholars. Egypt's strong economic growth supported a strong [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Army "Egyptian Army") establishment, leading to the victory at the [Battle of Ain Jalut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ain_Jalut "Battle of Ain Jalut") (1260) under [Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_of_Egypt "Sultan of Egypt") [Qutuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutuz "Qutuz") and [Baybars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baybars "Baybars"), which was a turning point, halting the westward expansion of the [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire"). Major battles involving the sultanate's army include: [Siege of Acre (1291)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Acre_(1291) "Siege of Acre (1291)"), [Battle of Marj al-Saffar (1303)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marj_al-Saffar_(1303) "Battle of Marj al-Saffar (1303)"), [Mamluk campaigns against Cyprus (1424–1426)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_campaigns_against_Cyprus_(1424%E2%80%931426) "Mamluk campaigns against Cyprus (1424–1426)"), and the [Edessa Campaigns (1429–1433)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edessa_Campaigns_(1429%E2%80%931433) "Edessa Campaigns (1429–1433)").

### Europe

In Europe, Western civilization reconstituted after the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Fall of the Western Roman Empire") into the period now known as the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages "Early Middle Ages") (500–1000). The Early Middle Ages saw a continuation of trends begun in [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity"): depopulation, deurbanization, and increased [barbarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarian "Barbarian") invasion.

From the 7th until the 11th centuries, [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests"), [Magyars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarians "Hungarians"), and [Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age "Viking Age") were all threats to the Christian Kingdoms that killed thousands of people over centuries. Raiders, however, also created new trading networks. In 800, the [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples "Germanic peoples") king [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne "Charlemagne") was crowned Emperor by [Pope Leo III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_III "Pope Leo III"), the [first Western Roman emperor since Romulus Augustus was captured and deposed more than 300 years earlier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire#Later_claims_to_the_Imperial_title_in_the_West "Western Roman Empire"). The conflict-filled relationship between the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") and temporal (mostly Germanic) monarchs would become the single most important political fact of European history for the next 500 years. The stability of Charlemagne's reign stimulated a brief revival of cultural activity known as the [Carolingian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance "Carolingian Renaissance"). The [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire "Holy Roman Empire") would last for almost a millennium until its dissolution during the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars "Napoleonic Wars") in the early 19th century.

In Eastern Europe, the [Eastern Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") survived in what is now called the Byzantine Empire, which created the [Code of Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Juris_Civilis "Corpus Juris Civilis") that inspired the legal structures of modern European states. Overseen by [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") emperors, in the 9th–10th centuries the Byzantine Eastern Orthodox Church Christianized the [First Bulgarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Bulgarian_Empire "First Bulgarian Empire") and [Kievan Rus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kievan_Rus%27 "Kievan Rus'"), the cultural and political ancestors to modern-day [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria "Bulgaria") and [North Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Macedonia "North Macedonia"), on the one hand, and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia") and [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine"), on the other. [Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantium "Byzantium") flourished as the leading power and trade center in its region in the [Macedonian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Renaissance "Macedonian Renaissance") until it was overshadowed by [Italian city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_city-states "Italian city-states") and the Islamic Ottoman Empire near the end of the Middle Ages.

Later in the period, the creation of the [feudal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudal_system "Feudal system") allowed greater degrees of military and agricultural organization. There was sustained [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization "Urbanization") in [northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe "Northern Europe") and [western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe "Western Europe"). Later developments were marked by [manorialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorialism "Manorialism") and [feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism "Feudalism"), and evolved into the prosperous [High Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Middle_Ages "High Middle Ages"). After 1000, the Christian kingdoms that had emerged from Rome's collapse changed dramatically in their cultural and societal character.

During the High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300), [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity")-oriented art and architecture flourished, and the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades") were mounted to recapture the [Holy Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land "Holy Land") from [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim "Muslim") control. The influence of the emerging [nation-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation-state "Nation-state") was tempered by the ideal of an international [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") and the presence of the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") in all western kingdoms. The codes of [chivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chivalry "Chivalry") and [courtly love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtly_love "Courtly love") set rules for proper behavior, while the [Scholastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism") philosophers attempted to reconcile faith and reason. The age of Feudalism would be dramatically transformed by the cataclysm of the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death") and its aftermath. This time would be a major underlying cause for the Renaissance. By the turn of the 16th century, European or [Western civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world") would be engaging in the [Age of Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery "Age of Discovery").

The term "Middle Ages" first appears in Latin in the 15th century and reflects the view that this period was a deviation from the path of classical learning, a path supposedly reconnected by [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance") scholarship.

### West and Central Asia

The Arabian Peninsula and the surrounding [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") and [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East "Near East") regions saw dramatic change during the post-classical era caused primarily by the spread of [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") and the establishment of the Arab [caliphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate").

In the 5th century, the Middle East was separated by empires and their spheres of influence; the two most prominent were the Persian [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire"), centered in what is now [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), and the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") in [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") (modern-day [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey")). The Byzantines and Sasanians fought with each other continually, a reflection of the rivalry between the Roman Empire and the Persian Empire seen during the previous five hundred years. The fighting weakened both states, leaving the stage open to a new power. Meanwhile, the nomadic [Bedouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedouin "Bedouin") tribes who dominated the Arabian desert saw a period of tribal warfare for scarce resources and a familiarity with Abrahamic religions or monotheism.

While the Byzantine and Sasanian Empires were both weakened by the [Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628 "Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628"), a new power in the form of [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") grew in the Middle East under [Muhammad in Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_in_Medina "Muhammad in Medina"). In a series of rapid [Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests"), the [Rashidun army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_army "Rashidun army"), led by the caliphs and skilled military commanders such as [Khalid ibn al-Walid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalid_ibn_al-Walid "Khalid ibn al-Walid"), swept through most of the Middle East, taking more than half of Byzantine territory in the [Arab–Byzantine wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Byzantine_wars "Arab–Byzantine wars") and completely engulfing Persia in the [Muslim conquest of Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia "Muslim conquest of Persia"). It would be the Arab caliphates of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") that would first unify the entire Middle East as a distinct region and create the dominant ethnic identity that persists today. These caliphates included the [Rashidun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate "Rashidun Caliphate"), [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate "Umayyad Caliphate"), and [Abbasid Caliphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate"), along with the later Turkic-based [Seljuk Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire "Seljuk Empire").

After Muhammad introduced Islam, it jump-started Middle Eastern culture into an [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age"), inspiring achievements in [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"), the revival of old advances in science and technology, and the formation of a distinct way of life. Muslims saved and spread Greek advances in [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_medicine "History of medicine"), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_elementary_algebra "History of elementary algebra"), [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_geometry "History of geometry"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_astronomy "History of astronomy"), [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_anatomy "History of anatomy"), and [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_ethics "History of ethics") that would later find their way back to Western Europe.

The dominance of the Arabs came to a sudden end in the mid-11th century with the arrival of the Seljuk Turks, migrating south from the Turkic homelands in Central Asia. They conquered Persia, Iraq (capturing Baghdad in 1055), Syria, Palestine, and the [Hejaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz "Hejaz"). This was followed by a series of Christian Western European invasions. The fragmentation of the Middle East allowed joint European forces mainly from [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England "Kingdom of England"), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_France "Kingdom of France"), and the emerging [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire "Holy Roman Empire") to enter the region. In 1099, the knights of the [First Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade "First Crusade") captured [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") and founded the [Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem "Kingdom of Jerusalem"), which survived until 1187, when [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin "Saladin") retook the city. Smaller crusader fiefdoms survived until 1291. In the early 13th century, a new wave of invaders, the armies of the [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire"), swept through the region, sacking Baghdad in the [siege of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_(1258) "Siege of Baghdad (1258)") and advancing as far south as the border of [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") in what became known as the [Mongol conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquests "Mongol conquests"). The Mongols eventually retreated in 1335, but the chaos that ensued throughout the empire deposed the Seljuk Turks. In 1401, the region was further plagued by the [Turko-Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turco-Mongol_tradition "Turco-Mongol tradition") [Timur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur "Timur") and his ferocious raids. By then, another group of Turks had arisen as well, the Ottomans.

### South Asia

There has been difficulty applying the word "medieval" or "post-classical" to the history of South Asia. This section follows historian Stein Burton's definition that corresponds to the period from the 8th century to the 16th century, more or less following the same time frame as the post-classical period and the European Middle Ages.

Until the 13th century, there were no fewer than 20 to 40 different states on the Indian subcontinent which hosted a variety of cultures, languages, writing systems and religions. At the beginning of the time period, [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") was predominant throughout the area, with the short-lived [Pala Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pala_Empire "Pala Empire") on the [Indo-Gangetic Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain "Indo-Gangetic Plain") sponsoring the faith's institutions. One such institution was the Buddhist [Nalanda mahavihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nalanda_mahavihara "Nalanda mahavihara") in modern-day [Bihar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bihar "Bihar"), a center of scholarship that brought the divided South Asia onto the global intellectual stage. Another accomplishment was the invention of the *[Chaturanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaturanga "Chaturanga")* game, which was later exported to Europe and became [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess "Chess").

In [South India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_India "South India"), the [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus "Hindus") kingdom of [Chola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_dynasty "Chola dynasty") gained prominence with an overseas empire that controlled parts of modern-day Sri Lanka, Malaysia, and [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_invasion_of_Srivijaya "Chola invasion of Srivijaya") as overseas territories and accelerated the spread of [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") into the historic culture of these places. In this time period, neighboring areas such as [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Afghanistan "History of Afghanistan"), [Tibet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tibet "History of Tibet"), and [Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Myanmar "History of Myanmar") were under [South Asian influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_India "Greater India").

From 1206 onward, a series of Turkic invasions from modern-day Afghanistan and Iran conquered massive portions of [North India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_India "North India"), founding the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate "Delhi Sultanate"), which remained supreme until the 16th century. [Buddhism declined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_of_Buddhism_in_the_Indian_subcontinent "Decline of Buddhism in the Indian subcontinent") in South Asia, vanishing in many areas, but Hinduism survived and reinforced itself in areas conquered by [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Islamic_relations "Hindu–Islamic relations"). In the far south, the [Vijayanagara Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vijayanagara_Empire "Vijayanagara Empire") was not conquered by any Muslim state in the period. The turn of the 16th century would see the rise of a new Islamic empire—the [Mughals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") and the establishment of European trade posts by the [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_India "Portuguese India").

### Southeast Asia

From the 8th century onward, [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia") stood to benefit from the trade taking place between South Asia and East Asia; numerous kingdoms arose in the region due to the flow of wealth passing through the [Strait of Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Malacca "Strait of Malacca"). While Southeast Asia had numerous outside influences, including Indian and Chinese Civilization, local cultures strove to cement their own unique identities. North Vietnam (known as Dai Viet) was culturally closer to China for centuries due to conquest.

Since rule from the third century BCE, North Vietnam continued to be subjugated by Chinese states, although it continually resisted periodically.c[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] There were three periods of [Chinese domination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_under_Chinese_rule "Vietnam under Chinese rule") that spanned nearly 1100 years. The Vietnamese gained long-lasting independence in the 10th century when China was [divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Dynasties_and_Ten_Kingdoms_period "Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period") with [Tĩnh Hải quân](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C4%A9nh_H%E1%BA%A3i_qu%C3%A2n "Tĩnh Hải quân") and the successor [Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t "Đại Việt"). Nonetheless, even as an independent state, a sort of begrudging [Sinicization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinicization "Sinicization") occurred. South Vietnam was governed by the ancient Hindu [Champa Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa "Champa") but was [annexed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa%E2%80%93%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t_War_(1471) "Champa–Đại Việt War (1471)") by the Vietnamese in the 15th century.

The spread of Hinduism, Buddhism, and maritime trade between China and South Asia created the foundation for Southeast Asia's first major empires, including the [Khmer Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_Empire "Khmer Empire") from Cambodia and [Srivijaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srivijaya "Srivijaya") from Indonesia. During the Khmer Empire's height in the 12th century, the city of [Angkor Thom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor_Thom "Angkor Thom") was among the largest of the pre-modern world due to its water management. King [Jayavarman II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayavarman_II "Jayavarman II") constructed over a hundred hospitals throughout his realm. Nearby rose the [Pagan Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagan_Empire "Pagan Empire") in modern-day Burma, using elephants as military might. The construction of the Buddhist [Shwezigon Pagoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shwezigon_Pagoda "Shwezigon Pagoda") and its tolerance for believers of older polytheistic gods helped [Theravada Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada "Theravada") become supreme in the region. In Indonesia, [Srivijaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srivijaya "Srivijaya") from the 7th through the 14th century was a [thalassocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalassocracy "Thalassocracy") that focused on maritime city-states and trade. Controlling the vital choke points of the [Sunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunda_Strait "Sunda Strait") and Malacca Straits, it became rich from trade ranging from Japan through Arabia.

Gold, ivory, and ceramics were all major commodities traveling through port cities. The empire was also responsible for the construction of wonders such as [Borobudur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borobudur "Borobudur"). During this time, Indonesian sailors crossed the [Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean "Indian Ocean"); evidence suggests that they may have colonized [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Madagascar "History of Madagascar"). Indian culture spread to the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(900%E2%80%931521) "History of the Philippines (900–1521)"), likely through Indonesian trade, resulting in the first documented use of writing in the archipelago and Indianized kingdoms.

Over time, changing economic and political conditions elsewhere and wars weakened the traditional empires of Southeast Asia. While the Mongol invasions did not directly annex Southeast Asia, the wartime devastation paved the way for the rise of new nations. In the 14th century, the Khmer Empire was uprooted by persistent years of war—losing the functionality and engineering knowledge of its advanced water management system. Srivijaya was overtaken by the [Majapahit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majapahit "Majapahit"). Islamic missionaries and merchants arrived, eventually leading to Islamization in Indonesia.

### East Asia

The time frame of 500–1500 in the history of East Asia—and China in particular—has been proposed as a possible classification for the region's history within the context of global post-classical history. Discussions within Columbia University's Association of Asian Studies have postulated that similarities between China and other regions of Eurasia during post-classical times have often been overlooked. Typically, the English-language historiography of Japan postulates that its 'medieval period' began as late as 1185.

During this period, the Eastern empires continued to expand through trade, migration, and conquests of neighboring areas. [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan") and [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea") went through the process of voluntary Sinicization, or the impression of Chinese cultural and political ideas.

[Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea") and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asuka_period "Asuka period") sinicized because their ruling class was largely impressed by China's bureaucracy. The major influences China had on these countries were the spread of Confucianism, the spread of Buddhism, and the establishment of centralized governance. Throughout East Asia, Buddhism was most visible in monasteries and local educational institutions, and Confucianism remained the ideology of social cohesion and state power.

In the times of the [Sui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_dynasty "Sui dynasty"), [Tang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty"), and [Song dynasties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty "Song dynasty") (581–1279), China remained the world's largest economy and most technologically advanced society. [Inventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Great_Inventions "Four Great Inventions") such as gunpowder, woodblock printing, and the magnetic compass were improved upon. China stood in contrast to other areas at the time as the imperial governments exhibited concentrated central authority instead of feudalism.

China exhibited much interest in [foreign affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_imperial_China "Foreign relations of imperial China") during the Tang and Song dynasties. From the 7th through the 10th centuries, Tang China was focused on securing the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road") as the selling of its goods westward was central to the nation's economy. For a time, China successfully secured its frontiers by integrating its nomadic neighbors—the [Göktürks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6kt%C3%BCrks "Göktürks")—into its civilization. The Tang dynasty expanded into Central Asia and received tribute from countries as distant as Eastern Iran. Western expansion ended with [wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Talas "Battle of Talas") with the Abbasid Caliphate and the deadly [An Lushan Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Lushan_Rebellion "An Lushan Rebellion"), which resulted in a deadly but uncertain death toll of millions. After the collapse of the Tang dynasty and subsequent [civil wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Kingdoms_period "Ten Kingdoms period") came the second phase of Chinese interest in foreign relations. Unlike the Tang, the Song specialized in overseas trade and peacefully created a maritime network, and China's population became concentrated in the south. Chinese merchant ships reached Indonesia, India, and Arabia. Southeast Asia's economy flourished from trade with Song China.

With the country's emphasis on trade and economic growth, Song China's [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Song_dynasty "Economy of the Song dynasty") began to use machines to manufacture goods and coal as a source of energy. The advances of the Song in the 11th/12th centuries have been considered an early [industrial revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_industrialization "Chinese industrialization"). Economic advancements came at the cost of military affairs, and the Song became open to invasions from the north. China became divided as the Song's northern lands were conquered by the [Jurchen people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurchen_people "Jurchen people"). By 1200, there were five Chinese kingdoms stretching from modern-day Turkestan to the Sea of Japan, including the [Western Liao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Khitai "Qara Khitai"), [Western Xia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Xia "Western Xia"), [Jin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234) "Jin dynasty (1115–1234)"), [Southern Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty "Song dynasty"), and [Dali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dali_Kingdom "Dali Kingdom"). Because these states competed with each other, they were all eventually annexed by the rising [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire") before 1279. After seventy years of [conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_China "Mongol conquest of China"), the Mongols proclaimed the [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty "Yuan dynasty") and also subjugated [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_of_Korea "Mongol invasions of Korea"); they failed to conquer [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_of_Japan "Mongol invasions of Japan"). Mongol conquerors also made China accessible to [European travelers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_Medieval_China "Europeans in Medieval China") such as [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo "Marco Polo"). The Mongol era was short-lived due to plagues and famine. After the [revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Turban_Rebellion "Red Turban Rebellion") in 1368, the succeeding [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty "Ming dynasty") ushered in a period of prosperity and brief [foreign expeditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_treasure_voyages "Ming treasure voyages") before isolating itself from global affairs for centuries.

[Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon "Joseon") and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muromachi_period "Muromachi period"), however, continued to have relations with China and other Asian countries. In the 15th century, [Sejong the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sejong_the_Great "Sejong the Great") of Korea cemented his country's identity by creating the [Hangul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul "Hangul") writing system to replace the use of [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters "Chinese characters"). Meanwhile, Japan fell under the military rule of the [Kamakura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamakura_period "Kamakura period") and later [Ashikaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashikaga_shogunate "Ashikaga shogunate") shogunates dominated by the [samurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai "Samurai").

### Oceania

* Map of the three major Pacific cultural regions: [Polynesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesia "Polynesia"), [Melanesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanesia "Melanesia"), and [Micronesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia "Micronesia").

Separate from developments in [Afro-Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia "Afro-Eurasia") and the Americas, the region of greater [Oceania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceania "Oceania") continued to develop independently of the outside world. In [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Australia "History of Australia"), the society of [Aboriginal Australians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aboriginal_Australians "Aboriginal Australians") changed little through the post-classical Period since their arrival in the area from Africa around 50,000 BCE. The only evidence of outside contact was encounters with fishermen of [Indonesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makassan_contact_with_Australia "Makassan contact with Australia") origin.

[Polynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians "Polynesians") and [Micronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesians "Micronesians") peoples are rooted in Taiwan and Southeast Asia and began their migration into the [Pacific Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean "Pacific Ocean") from 3000 to 1500 BCE. After the 4th century, the Micronesians and Polynesians began to explore the South Pacific and later constructed cities in previously uninhabited areas, including [Nan Madol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nan_Madol "Nan Madol"), [Muʻa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%CA%BBa_(Tongatapu) "Muʻa (Tongatapu)"), and others. Around 1200 CE, the [Tuʻi Tonga Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu%CA%BBi_Tonga_Empire "Tuʻi Tonga Empire") spread its influence far and wide throughout the South Pacific Islands, being described by academics as a maritime chiefdom which used trade networks to keep power centralized around the king's capital. Polynesians on [outrigger canoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outrigger_canoe "Outrigger canoe") [discovered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation "Polynesian navigation") and colonized some of the last uninhabited islands of Earth. [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_and_settlement_of_Hawaii "Discovery and settlement of Hawaii"), [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand "History of New Zealand"), and [Easter Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Easter_Island "History of Easter Island") were among the final places to be reached, with settlers discovering pristine lands. [Oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_narrative "Polynesian narrative") claimed that navigator [Ui-te-Rangiora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora "Ui-te-Rangiora") discovered icebergs in the [Southern Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Ocean "Southern Ocean"). In exploring and settling, Polynesian settlers did not strike at random but used their knowledge of wind and water currents to reach their destinations.

On the settled islands, some Polynesian groups became distinct from one another, a significant example being the [Maori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people "Māori people") of New Zealand. Other island systems kept in contact with each other, including [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_(island) "Hawaii (island)") and [Tahiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahiti "Tahiti"); goods in long-distance trade included basalt and Pearl shell. Ecologically, Polynesians had the challenge of sustaining themselves within limited environments. Some settlements caused mass extinctions of some native plant and animal species over time by hunting species such as the [moa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moa "Moa") and introducing the [Polynesian rat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_rat "Polynesian rat"). Easter Island settlers engaged in complete ecological destruction of their habitat, and their population crashed afterward, possibly due to the construction of the [Easter Island Statues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moai "Moai"). Other colonizing groups adapted to accommodate the ecology of specific islands such as the [Moriori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moriori "Moriori") of the [Chatham Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatham_Islands "Chatham Islands").

Europeans on their voyages visited many Pacific islands in the 16th and 17th centuries, but most areas of Oceania were not colonized until after the [voyages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_voyage_of_James_Cook "First voyage of James Cook") of British explorer [James Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook "James Cook") in the 1780s.

### Americas

The post-classical era of the Americas can be considered set at a different time span from that of Afro-Eurasia. As the developments of Mesoamerican and Andean civilization differ greatly from those of the Old World, as well as the speed at which they developed, the post-classical era in the traditional sense does not take place until near the end of the medieval age in Western Europe.

As such, for the purposes of this article, the [Woodland period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland_period "Woodland period") and [Classic stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_stage "Classic stage") of the Americas will be discussed here, which takes place from about 400 to 1400. For the technical post-classical stage in American development which took place on the eve of European contact, see [Post-Classic stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Classic_stage "Post-Classic stage").

#### North America

As a continent, there was little unified trade or communication. Advances in agriculture spread northward from [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica") indirectly through trade. Major cultural areas, however, still developed independently of each other.

##### Norse contact and the polar regions

While there was little regular contact between the Americas and the Old World, the Norse explored and even colonized [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland "Greenland") and [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada "Canada") as early as 1000. None of these settlements survived past medieval times. Outside of Scandinavia, knowledge of the discovery of the Americas was interpreted as a [remote island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinland#Medieval_geographers "Vinland") or the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole "North Pole").

The Norse arriving from Iceland [settled Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland "Norse settlements in Greenland") from approximately 980 to 1450. The Norse arrived in southern Greenland prior to the 13th-century approach of the [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit "Inuit") [Thule people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people "Thule people") in the area. The extent of the interaction between the Norse and Thule is unclear. Greenland was valuable to the Norse due to trade in ivory that came from the tusks of walruses. The [Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age "Little Ice Age") adversely affected the colonies, and they vanished. Greenland would be lost to Europeans until [Danish Colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_colonization_of_the_Americas "Danish colonization of the Americas") in the 18th century.

The Norse also explored and colonized farther south in [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland_(island) "Newfoundland (island)"), Canada, at [L'Anse aux Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%27Anse_aux_Meadows "L'Anse aux Meadows"), referred to by the Norse as *Vinland*. The colony at most existed for twenty years and resulted in no known transmission of diseases or technology to the [First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada "First Nations in Canada"). To the Norse, *Vinland* was known for plentiful grape vines to make superior wine. One reason for the colony's failure was constant violence with the native [Beothuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beothuk "Beothuk") people, whom the Norse referred to as [skrælings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skr%C3%A6ling "Skræling").

After initial expeditions, there is a possibility that the Norse continued to visit modern-day Canada. Surviving records from medieval Iceland indicate some sporadic voyages to a land called *[Markland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markland "Markland")*, possibly the coast of [Labrador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labrador "Labrador"), Canada, as late as 1347—presumably to collect wood for deforested Greenland.

##### Northern areas

In [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America"), many [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") and agricultural societies thrived in the diverse region. [Native American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_in_the_United_States "Native American in the United States") tribes varied greatly in characteristics; some, including the [Mound Builders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mound_Builders "Mound Builders") and the [Oasisamerican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oasisamerica "Oasisamerica") cultures, were complex chiefdoms. Other nations that inhabited the states of the modern northern United States and Canada had less complexity and did not follow technological changes as quickly. Around the year 500, during the [Woodland period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland_period "Woodland period"), Native Americans began to transition to bows and arrows from spears for hunting and warfare. Around the year 1,000, corn was widely adopted as a staple crop in the [Eastern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_United_States "Eastern United States"). Corn would continue to be the staple crop of natives in the Eastern United States and Canada until the Columbian exchange.

In the Eastern United States, rivers were the medium of trade and communication. [Cahokia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia "Cahokia"), located in the modern US state of [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois "Illinois"), was among the most significant cities within the [Mississippian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_culture "Mississippian culture"). Focused around [Monks Mound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monks_Mound "Monks Mound"), archaeology indicates the population increased exponentially after 1000 because it manufactured important tools for agriculture and hosted cultural attractions. Around 1350, Cahokia was abandoned, with environmental factors having been proposed for the city's decline.

At the same time, Ancestral Puebloans constructed clusters of buildings in the [Chaco Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaco_Culture_National_Historical_Park "Chaco Culture National Historical Park") site located in the [State of New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico "New Mexico"). Individual houses may have been occupied by more than 600 residents at any one time. Chaco Canyon was the only pre-Columbian site in the United States to build paved roads. Pottery indicates a society that was becoming more complex; turkeys were also domesticated for the first time in the continental United States. Around 1150, the structures of Chaco Canyon were abandoned, likely as a result of severe drought. Other Pueblo complexes in the Southwestern United States include the notable [Cliff Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliff_Palace "Cliff Palace"), located in [Mesa Verde National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesa_Verde_National_Park "Mesa Verde National Park"). After reaching climaxes, native complex societies in the United States declined and did not entirely recover before the arrival of European explorers.

#### Caribbean

Concentrating a significant number of islands, the [Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean "Caribbean") had been the scene of continual maritime migrations via canoes since the [Lithic stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithic_stage "Lithic stage"), with its first inhabitants reaching the area by around 5000 BCE.

Following a millennium of population flows, the various peoples of the Caribbean entered the post-classical period having achieved notable developments. Their permanent settlements grew increasingly complex, a direct result of refined agricultural techniques and substantial demographic growth. Consequently, villages expanded into grand ceremonial centers governed by different [caciques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacique "Cacique"). Concurrently, a vibrant regional trade network flourished, with goods like shells, cotton, gold, colored stones, and rare feathers extensively exchanged across the islands, spanning from the [Lesser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Antilles "Lesser Antilles") to the [Greater Antilles.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antilles "Antilles")

By around 650 CE and 800 CE, new migratory waves from the Caribbean coast of present-day [Venezuela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venezuela "Venezuela") took place, and several peoples began a process of major cultural, sociopolitical, and ritual reformulations that led to the formation of the first [chiefdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiefdoms "Chiefdoms") and the emergence of [social hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_hierarchy "Social hierarchy"). This moment can also be described by the expulsion of the ancient [Saladoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladoid "Saladoid") peoples from the main islands of the Caribbean and their subsequent replacement by the newly arrived [Taíno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%C3%ADno "Taíno") people, who constantly fought with other [Arawak-speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arawakan_languages "Arawakan languages") groups for farmable lands and war captives. Despite little evidence, some scholars still claim that the Taíno may have had a tenuous influence from the [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization"), as certain customs, such as the practice of *[batey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batey_(game) "Batey (game)")*, may have been inherited from the original [Mesoamerican ballgame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_ballgame "Mesoamerican ballgame").

When [Christopher Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus "Christopher Columbus") landed in [the Bahamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bahamas "The Bahamas") in 1492, he and his crew initially maintained a peaceful contact with the local Taíno people, but soon afterward they were enslaved by the Spanish colonizers, bringing the area into the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period "Early modern period").

#### Mesoamerica

At the beginning of the global post-classical period, the city of [Teotihuacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teotihuacan "Teotihuacan") was at its zenith, housing over 125,000 people; at 500 AD it was the sixth largest city in the world at the time. The city's residents built the [Pyramid of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_of_the_Sun "Pyramid of the Sun"), the third largest pyramid in the world, oriented to follow astronomical events. In the 6th and 7th centuries, the city suddenly declined, possibly as a result of severe environmental damage caused by [extreme weather events of 535–536](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather_events_of_535%E2%80%93536 "Extreme weather events of 535–536"). There is evidence that large parts of the city were burned, possibly in a domestic rebellion. The city's legacy would inspire all future civilizations in the region.

At the same time was the Classic Age of the [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") clustered in dozens of city-states on the [Yucatán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucat%C3%A1n_Peninsula "Yucatán Peninsula") and modern-day [Guatemala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guatemala "Guatemala"). The most significant of these cities was [Chichen Itza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichen_Itza "Chichen Itza"), which often fiercely competed with anywhere from 60 to 80 city-states to be the dominant economic influence in the region. Likewise, other Mayan cities such as [Tikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikal "Tikal") and [Calakmul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calakmul "Calakmul") also initiated a series of full-scale conflicts in the area over power and prestige, culminating in the [Tikal-Calakmul Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikal%E2%80%93Calakmul_wars "Tikal–Calakmul wars") in the 6th century.

The Mayans had an upper caste of priests, who were well versed in astronomy, mathematics, and writing. The Mayans developed the concept of zero and a 365-day calendar, which possibly predates its creation in Old World societies. After 900, many Mayan cities suddenly declined due to ecological disaster, which was likely caused by a combination of drought and an incessant cycle of warfare. It has also been noted that classical Mayan Cities lacked food storage facilities.

The [Toltec Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toltec_Empire "Toltec Empire") arose from the [Toltec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toltec "Toltec") culture, and they were remembered as wise and benevolent leaders. One priest-king called [Ce Acatl Topiltzin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ce_Acatl_Topiltzin "Ce Acatl Topiltzin") advocated against human sacrifice. After his death in 947, civil wars of religious character broke out between those who supported and opposed Topiltzin's teachings. Modern historians, however, are skeptical of the extent of Toltec influence and believe that much of the information known about the Toltecs was created by the later [Aztecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztecs "Aztecs") as an inspiration myth.

In the 1300s, a small band of violent, religious radicals called the Aztecs began minor raids throughout the area. Eventually they began to claim connections with the Toltec civilization, and insisted they were the rightful successors. They began to grow in numbers and conquer large areas of land. Fundamental to their conquest was the use of [political terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism "Terrorism") in the sense that the Aztec leaders and priests would command the [human sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sacrifice "Human sacrifice") of their [subjugated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquest_(military)#Subjugation "Conquest (military)") people as a means of humility and coercion. Most of the Mesoamerican region would eventually fall under the Aztec Empire. On the [Yucatán Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucat%C3%A1n_Peninsula "Yucatán Peninsula"), most of the [Maya peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_peoples "Maya peoples") continued to be independent of the Aztecs, but their traditional civilization declined. Aztec developments expanded cultivation, applying the use of [chinampas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinampa "Chinampa"), irrigation, and [terrace agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrace_agriculture "Terrace agriculture"); important crops included [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize"), [sweet potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato "Sweet potato"), and [avocados](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avocado "Avocado").

In 1428, the city of [Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan "Tenochtitlan") allied with other powerful [Nahuatl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahuatl "Nahuatl")-speaking cities, [Texcoco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texcoco_(altepetl) "Texcoco (altepetl)") and [Tlacopan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlacopan "Tlacopan"), to create the Aztec Empire, otherwise known as the Triple Alliance. Though referred to as an empire, the Aztec Empire functioned as a system of tribute collection with Tenochtitlan at its center. By the turn of the 16th century, "[flower wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_war "Flower war")" between the Aztecs and rival states such as [Tlaxcala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlaxcala_(Nahua_state) "Tlaxcala (Nahua state)") had continued for over fifty years.

#### South America

South American civilization was concentrated in the Andean region, which had already hosted complex cultures since 2,500 BCE. East of the Andean region, societies were generally semi-nomadic. Discoveries on the [Amazon River Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_basin "Amazon basin") indicate the region likely had a pre-contact population of five million people and hosted complex societies. Around the continent, numerous agricultural peoples from [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Colombia "History of Colombia") to [Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Argentina "History of Argentina") steadily advanced through numerous stages of development from 500 CE until European contact.

##### Andes

During ancient times, the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes "Andes") had developed civilizations independent of outside influences, including that of Mesoamerica. Through the Post-classical era, a cycle of civilizations continued until [Spanish contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Inca_Empire "Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire"). Collectively, Andean societies lacked currency, a written language, and solid draft animals enjoyed by Old World civilizations. Instead, Andeans developed other methods to foster their growth, including the use of the [quipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu "Quipu") system to communicate messages, [llamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llama "Llama") to carry smaller loads, and an economy based on [reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocity_(social_and_political_philosophy) "Reciprocity (social and political philosophy)"). Societies were often based on strict social hierarchies and economic redistribution from the ruling class.

In the first half of the Post-classical period, the Andes were dominated by two almost equally powerful states. In the north of Peru was the [Wari Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wari_Empire "Wari Empire"), and in the south of Peru and Bolivia there was the [Tiwanaku Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiwanaku_Empire "Tiwanaku Empire")—both of which were inspired by the earlier [Moche people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moche_culture "Moche culture"). While the extent of their relationship to each other is unknown, it is believed that they competed with one another but avoided direct conflict. Without war, there was prosperity, and around the year 700, the city of Tiwanaku hosted a population of 1.4 million. After the 8th century, both states declined due to changing environmental conditions, laying the groundwork for the Incas and other minor kingdoms to emerge as distinct cultures centuries later.

In the 15th century, the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire "Inca Empire") rose to annex all other nations in the area. Led by their sun-god king, [Sapa Inca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapa_Inca "Sapa Inca"), they slowly conquered what is now [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru"), and they built their society throughout the Andes cultural region. The Incas spoke the [Quechua languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechua_languages "Quechua languages"). Taking advantage of ancient advances left by previous Andean societies, the Incas were able to create the most advanced system of trade routes in South America, known as the [Inca road system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system "Inca road system"), which allowed greater interconnection between the conquered provinces. Incas have been known to have used [abacuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abacus "Abacus") to calculate mathematics. The Inca Empire is known for some of its magnificent structures, such as [Machu Picchu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machu_Picchu "Machu Picchu") in the [Cusco region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cusco_region "Cusco region"). The empire expanded quickly northward to Ecuador, southward to central Chile. To the north of the Inca Empire remained the independent [Tairona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tairona "Tairona") and [Muisca Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_Confederation "Muisca Confederation"), which practiced agriculture and gold metallurgy.

## End of the period

As the post-classical era drew to a close in the 15th century, many of the empires established throughout the period were in decline. The Byzantine Empire would soon be overshadowed in the Mediterranean by both Islamic and Christian rivals including [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice "Republic of Venice"), [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Genoa "Republic of Genoa"), and the Ottoman Empire. The Byzantines faced repeated attacks from eastern and western powers during the [Fourth Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Crusade "Fourth Crusade"), and declined further until the loss of [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople") to the Ottomans in 1453.

The largest change came in terms of trade and technology. The global significance of the fall of the Byzantines was the disruption of overland routes between Asia and Europe. Traditional dominance of [nomadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad "Nomad") in Eurasia declined, and the [Pax Mongolica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Mongolica "Pax Mongolica") that had allowed for interactions between different civilizations was no longer available. West Asia and South Asia were conquered by [gunpowder empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder_empires "Gunpowder empires") which successfully used advances in military technology but closed the Silk Road.

Europeans—specifically the [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Portugal "Kingdom of Portugal") and various Italian explorers—intended to replace land travel with sea travel. Originally, European exploration merely looked for new routes to reach known destinations. Portuguese explorer [Vasco da Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasco_da_Gama "Vasco da Gama") traveled to India by sea in 1498 by circumnavigating Africa around the [Cape of Good Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_of_Good_Hope "Cape of Good Hope"). India and the coast of Africa were already known to Europeans, but none had attempted a large trading mission prior to that time. Due to navigation advances, Portugal would create a [global colonial empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire "Portuguese Empire") beginning with the [conquest of Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Malacca_(1511) "Capture of Malacca (1511)") in modern-day [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia") from 1511.

Other explorers such as the Spanish-sponsored Italian Christopher Columbus intended to engage in trade by traveling on unfamiliar routes west from Europe. The subsequent [European discovery of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_discovery_of_the_Americas "European discovery of the Americas") in 1492 resulted in the [Columbian exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange "Columbian exchange") and the world's first pan-oceanic [globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization "Globalization"). Spanish explorer [Ferdinand Magellan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Magellan "Ferdinand Magellan") performed the [first known circumnavigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellan_expedition "Magellan expedition") of Earth in 1521. The transfer of goods and diseases across [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean") was unprecedented in creating a more connected world. From developments in navigation and trade, [modern history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_era "Modern era") began.
