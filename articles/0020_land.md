# Land

**Land**, also known as **dry land**, **ground**, or **earth**, is the solid terrestrial surface of [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") not submerged by the [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean") or another [body of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water "Body of water"). It makes up approximately 29% of Earth's surface and includes all [continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent") and [islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island "Island"). Earth's land surface is almost entirely covered by [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith"), a layer of [rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_(geology) "Rock (geology)"), [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil "Soil"), and [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral "Mineral") that forms the outer part of the [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_crust "Earth's crust"). Land plays an important role in Earth's [climate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_system "Climate system"), being involved in the [carbon cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_cycle "Carbon cycle"), [nitrogen cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_cycle "Nitrogen cycle"), and [water cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle "Water cycle"). One-third of land is covered in [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trees "Trees") another third is used for [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture"), and roughly one-tenth is covered in [glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacier "Glacier") ice. The remainder consists of [desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert "Desert"), [savannah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannah "Savannah"), and [prairie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie "Prairie").

Land [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain "Terrain") varies greatly, consisting of mountains, deserts, plains, plateaus, glaciers, and other [landforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landform "Landform"). In physical geology, the land is divided into two major categories: [Mountain ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_range "Mountain range") and relatively flat interiors called [cratons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craton "Craton"). Both form over millions of years through [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics"). [Streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream "Stream") – a major part of Earth's water cycle – shape the [landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape "Landscape"), carve rocks, transport sediments, and replenish groundwater. At high elevations or [latitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude "Latitude"), snow is compacted and recrystallized over hundreds or thousands of years to form glaciers, which can be so heavy that they warp the Earth's crust. About 30 percent of land has a dry climate, due to losing more water through evaporation than it gains from [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation"). Since warm air rises, this generates winds, though [Earth's rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_rotation "Earth's rotation") and uneven sun distribution also play a part.

Land is commonly defined as the solid, dry surface of Earth. It can also refer to the collective [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources "Natural resources") that the land holds, including [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers "Rivers"), [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakes "Lakes"), and the [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere "Biosphere"). Human manipulation of the land, including [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture") and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"), can also be considered part of land. Land is formed from the [continental crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_crust "Continental crust"), the layer of rock on which soil, [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater "Groundwater"), and human and other animal activity sit.

Though modern terrestrial [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_plant "Terrestrial plant") and [animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_animal "Terrestrial animal") evolved from aquatic creatures, Earth's [first cellular life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earliest_known_life_forms "Earliest known life forms") likely originated on land. Survival on land relies on [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water "Fresh water") from rivers, streams, lakes, and glaciers, which constitute only three percent of the water on Earth. The vast majority of human activity throughout history has occurred in [habitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitable "Habitable") land areas supporting agriculture and various natural resources. In recent decades, scientists and policymakers have emphasized the need to [manage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_land_management "Sustainable land management") land and its biosphere more sustainably, through measures such as restoring [degraded soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_degradation "Land degradation"), preserving [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity "Biodiversity"), protecting [endangered species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_species "Endangered species"), and addressing [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change").

## Definition

Land is often defined as the solid, dry surface of Earth. The word *land* may also collectively refer to the collective [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources "Natural resources") of Earth, including its [land cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_cover "Land cover"), [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River "River"), shallow [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake "Lake"), its [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere "Biosphere"), the lowest layer of the atmosphere ([troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere "Troposphere")), [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater "Groundwater") reserves, and the physical results of human activity on land, such as [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture") and agriculture. The boundary between land and sea is called the [shoreline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreline "Shoreline").

## Etymology

The word *land* is derived from [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English"), from the [Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic "Proto-Germanic") word \**landą*, "untilled land", and then the [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European "Proto-Indo-European") \**lendʰ-*, especially in northern regions that were home to languages like [Proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic "Proto-Celtic") and [Proto-Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Slavic "Proto-Slavic"). Examples include [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish "Old Irish") *land*, "land, plot, church building" and [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish "Old Irish") *ithlann*, "threshing floor", and [Old East Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_East_Slavic "Old East Slavic") *ljadina* "wasteland, weeds".

A [country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country "Country") or [nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation "Nation") may be referred to as the [motherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherland "Motherland"), [fatherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatherland "Fatherland"), or [homeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland "Homeland") of its people. Many countries and other places have names incorporating the suffix [-land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-land "-land") (e.g., [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England"), [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland "Greenland"), and [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand "New Zealand")). The equivalent suffix *[-stan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-stan "-stan")* from [Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Iranian_languages "Indo-Iranian languages"), ultimately derived from the [Proto-Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Iranian "Proto-Indo-Iranian") \**sthāna-*, is also present in many country and location names, such as [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan"), [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan "Afghanistan"), and others throughout [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"). The suffix is also used more generally, as in [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language") *rigestân* (ریگستان) "place of sand, desert", *golestân* (گلستان) "place of flowers, garden", *gurestân* (گورستان) "graveyard, cemetery", and *[Hindustân](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindust%C3%A2n "Hindustân")* (هندوستان) "land of the [Indo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent") people".

## Physical science

The study of land and its history in general is called [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography "Geography"). [Mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy "Mineralogy") is the study of minerals, and [petrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrology "Petrology") is the study of rocks. [Soil science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_science "Soil science") is the study of soils, encompassing the sub-disciplines of [pedology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedology "Pedology"), which focuses on soil formation, and [edaphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edaphology "Edaphology"), which focuses on the relationship between soil and life.

### Formation

The earliest material found in the Solar System is dated to 4.5672±0.0006 [bya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billion_years_ago "Billion years ago") (billion years ago); therefore, Earth itself must have been formed by [accretion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accretion_(astrophysics) "Accretion (astrophysics)") around this time. The [formation and evolution of the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System "Formation and evolution of the Solar System") bodies occurred in tandem with the Sun. In theory, a [solar nebula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_nebula "Solar nebula") partitions a volume out of a [molecular cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_cloud "Molecular cloud") by gravitational collapse, which begins to spin and flatten into a [circumstellar disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumstellar_disc "Circumstellar disc"), out of which the planets then grow (in tandem with the star). A nebula contains gas, ice grains and [dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_dust "Cosmic dust") (including [primordial nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_nuclide "Primordial nuclide")). In the [nebular hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebular_hypothesis "Nebular hypothesis"), [planetesimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetesimal "Planetesimal") begin to form as [particulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granular_material#Granular_gases "Granular material") matter accumulates by [cohesive clumping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohesion_(geology) "Cohesion (geology)") and then by gravity. The primordial Earth's assembly took 10–20 [myr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myr "Myr"). By 4.54±0.04 bya, the primordial Earth had formed.

Earth's atmosphere and oceans were formed by [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano "Volcano") activity and [outgassing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgassing "Outgassing") that included [water vapour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor "Water vapor"). The [origin of the world's oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_water_on_Earth "Origin of water on Earth") was condensation augmented by water and ice delivered by [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid"), [protoplanets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoplanet "Protoplanet"), and [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet "Comet"). In [this model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faint_young_Sun_paradox "Faint young Sun paradox"), atmospheric "[greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas")" kept the oceans from freezing while the newly formed Sun was only at 70% [luminosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_luminosity "Solar luminosity"). By 3.5 bya, the [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_magnetic_field "Earth's magnetic field") was established, which helped prevent the atmosphere from being stripped away by the [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind "Solar wind"). The atmosphere and oceans of the Earth continuously shape the land by eroding and transporting solids on the surface.

Earth's crust formed when the molten outer layer of Planet Earth cooled to [form a solid mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition") as the accumulated water vapour began to act in the atmosphere. Once land became capable of supporting life, biodiversity evolved over hundreds of millions of years, expanding continually except when punctuated by mass extinctions.

The two models that explain landmasses propose either a steady growth to the present-day forms or, more likely, a rapid growth early in Earth's history followed by a long-term steady continental area. [Continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent") are formed by [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics"), a process ultimately driven by the continuous loss of heat from the Earth's interior. On [time scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale "Geologic time scale") lasting hundreds of millions of years, the [supercontinents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercontinent "Supercontinent") have formed and broken apart three times. Roughly 750 [mya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mya_(unit) "Mya (unit)") (million years ago), one of the earliest known supercontinents, [Rodinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodinia "Rodinia"), began to break apart. The continents later recombined to form [Pannotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannotia "Pannotia"), 600–540 mya, then finally [Pangaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangaea "Pangaea"), which also broke apart 180 mya.

### Landmasses

A continuous area of land surrounded by an ocean is called a landmass. Although it is most often written as one word to distinguish it from the usage "land mass"—the measure of land area—it may also be written as two words. There are four major continuous landmasses on Earth: [Africa–Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia "Afro-Eurasia"), [America (landmass)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"), [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica "Antarctica"), and [Australia (landmass)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Australia "Mainland Australia"), which are subdivided into [continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent"). Up to seven geographical regions are commonly regarded as continents. Ordered from greatest to least land area, these continents are [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia"), [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"), [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America"), [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America "South America"), [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica "Antarctica"), [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"), and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_(continent) "Australia (continent)").

### Terrain

Terrain refers to an area of land and its features. Terrain affects travel, mapmaking, ecosystems, and surface [water flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_flow "Water flow") and distribution. Over a large area, it can influence climate and weather patterns. The terrain of a region largely determines its suitability for human settlement: flatter [alluvial plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_plain "Alluvial plain") tend to have better farming soils than steeper, rockier uplands.

[Elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation "Elevation") is defined as the vertical distance between an object and sea level, while [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude "Altitude") is defined as the vertical distance from an object to Earth's surface. The elevation of Earth's land surface varies from the low point of −418 metres (−1,371 feet) at the [Dead Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea "Dead Sea") to a maximum altitude of 8,848 metres (29,029 feet) at the top of Mount Everest. The mean height of land above sea level is about 797 metres (2,615 feet), with 98.9% of dry land situated above sea level.

[Relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_relief "Topographic relief") refers to the difference in elevation within a landscape; for example, flat terrain would have "low relief", while terrain with a large elevation difference between the highest and lowest points would be deemed "high relief". Most land has relatively low relief. The change in elevation between two points of the terrain is called a slope or gradient. A [topographic map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_map "Topographic map") is a form of [terrain cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain_cartography "Terrain cartography") which depicts terrain in terms of its elevation, slope, and the orientation of its landforms. It has prominent [contour lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_line "Contour line"), which connect points of similar elevation, while perpendicular slope lines point in the direction of the steepest slope. [Hypsometric tints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypsometric_tints "Hypsometric tints") are colors placed between contour lines to indicate elevation relative to [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level "Sea level").

A difference between uplands, or [highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland "Highland"), and lowlands is drawn in several [earth science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_science "Earth science") fields. In river ecology, "[upland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_and_lowland "Upland and lowland")" rivers are fast-moving and colder than "lowland" rivers, encouraging different species of fish and other aquatic wildlife to live in these habitats. For example, nutrients are more present in slow-moving lowland rivers, encouraging different species of [macrophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrophytes "Macrophytes") to grow there. The term "upland" is also used in wetland ecology, where "upland" plants indicate an area that is not a wetland. In addition, the term [moorland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorland "Moorland") refers to upland [shrubland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrubland "Shrubland") biomes with acidic soils, while [heathlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathland "Heathland") are lowland shrublands with acidic soils.

### Geomorphology

Geomorphology refers to the study of the natural processes that shape land's surface, creating landforms. [Erosion and tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion_and_tectonics "Erosion and tectonics"), [volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_eruptions "Volcanic eruptions"), [flooding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flooding "Flooding"), [weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weathering "Weathering"), [glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation "Glaciation"), the growth of [coral reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_reef "Coral reef"), and meteorite impacts are among the processes that constantly reshape Earth's surface over [geological time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_time "Geological time").

[Erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion "Erosion") transports one part of land to another via natural processes, such as [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolian_processes "Aeolian processes"), water, [ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice "Ice"), and [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_wasting "Mass wasting"). In contrast, weathering wears away rock and other solid land without transporting the land somewhere else. Natural erosional processes usually take a long time to cause noticeable changes in the landscape—for example, the [Grand Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon "Grand Canyon") was created over the past 70 million years by the [Colorado River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_River "Colorado River"), which scientists estimate continues to erode the canyon at a rate of 0.3 meters (0.98 feet) every 200 years. However, humans have caused erosion to be 10–40 times faster than normal, causing half the [topsoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topsoil "Topsoil") of the surface of Earth's land to be lost within the past 150 years.

[Plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics") refers to the theory that Earth's lithosphere is divided into "tectonic plates" that move over the mantle. This results in [continental drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift "Continental drift"), with continents moving relative to each other. The scientist [Alfred Wegener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener "Alfred Wegener") first hypothesized the theory of continental drift in 1912. More researchers developed his idea throughout the 20th century into the now widely accepted theory of plate tectonics.

Several key characteristics define the modern understanding of plate tectonics. The place where two tectonic plates meet is called a [plate boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_boundary "Plate boundary"), with different geological phenomena occurring across different kinds of boundaries. For example, at [divergent plate boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divergent_plate_boundaries "Divergent plate boundaries"), [seafloor spreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafloor_spreading "Seafloor spreading") is usually seen, in contrast with the [subduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction "Subduction") zones of [convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_plate_boundary "Convergent plate boundary") or [transform plate boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_fault "Transform fault").

[Earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquakes "Earthquakes") and [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_activity "Volcanic activity") are common in all types of boundaries. Volcanic activity refers to any rupture in Earth's surface where [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma "Magma") escapes, therefore becoming [lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava "Lava"). The [Ring of Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_Fire "Ring of Fire"), containing two-thirds of the world's volcanoes, and over 70% of Earth's [seismological activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismology "Seismology"), comprises plate boundaries surrounding the [Pacific Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean "Pacific Ocean").

### Climate

Earth's land interacts with and influences its [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate") heavily, since the land's surface heats up and cools down faster than air or water. [Latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude "Latitude"), [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation "Elevation"), [topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography "Topography"), [reflectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflectivity "Reflectivity"), and [land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use "Land use") all have varying effects on climate. The latitude of the land will influence how much [solar radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_radiation "Solar radiation") reaches its surface. High latitudes receive less solar radiation than low latitudes. The land's topography is important in creating and transforming airflow and [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation"). Large landforms, such as mountain ranges, can divert wind energy and make [air parcels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_parcel "Fluid parcel") less dense and therefore able to hold less heat. As air rises, this cooling effect causes [condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation "Condensation") and precipitation.

Different types of land cover will influence the land's [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo "Albedo"), a measure of the solar radiation that is reflected, rather than absorbed and transferred to Earth. [Vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation "Vegetation") has a relatively low albedo, meaning that vegetated surfaces are good absorbers of the sun's energy. [Forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest "Forest") have an albedo of 10–15 percent while [grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassland "Grassland") have an albedo of 15–20 percent. In comparison, sandy [deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert "Desert") have an albedo of 25–40 percent.

Land use by humans also plays a role in the regional and global climate. Densely populated cities are warmer and create [urban heat islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island "Urban heat island") that have effects on the precipitation, [cloud cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_cover "Cloud cover"), and temperature of the region.

## Features

A landform is a natural or manmade land feature. Landforms together make up a given terrain, and their arrangement in the [landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape "Landscape") is known as [topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography "Topography"). Landforms include [hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill "Hill"), [mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain "Mountain"), [canyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canyon "Canyon"), and [valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley "Valley"), as well as [shoreline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreline "Shoreline") features such as [bays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay "Bay"), [capes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_(geography) "Cape (geography)"), and [peninsulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsula "Peninsula").

### Coasts and islands

The [shoreline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreline "Shoreline") is the interface between the land and the [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean"). It migrates each day as [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide "Tide") rise and fall and moves over long periods of time as [sea levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level "Sea level") change. The shore extends from the low tide line to the highest elevation that can be reached by storm waves, and the [coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coast "Coast") stretches out inland until the point where ocean-related features are no longer found.

When land is in contact with bodies of water, it can be eroded. The weathering of a coastline may be impacted by the [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide "Tide"), caused by changes in gravitational forces on larger bodies of water. Coasts are important zones in natural ecosystems, often home to a wide range of [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity "Biodiversity"). On land, they harbour important ecosystems such as freshwater or estuarine [wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland "Wetland"), which are important for bird populations and other terrestrial animals. In wave-protected areas, they harbor [saltmarshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltmarshes "Saltmarshes"), [mangroves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangrove "Mangrove") or [seagrasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagrass_meadow "Seagrass meadow"), all of which can provide [nursery habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursery_habitat "Nursery habitat") for finfish, [shellfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shellfish "Shellfish"), and other aquatic species. Rocky shores are usually found along exposed coasts and provide habitat for a wide range of [sessile animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sessile_animals "Sessile animals") (e.g., [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussel "Mussel"), [starfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starfish "Starfish"), [barnacles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle "Barnacle")) and various kinds of [seaweeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaweed "Seaweed"). Along [tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical "Tropical") coasts with clear, nutrient-poor water, [coral reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_reef "Coral reef") can often be found between depths of 1–50 meters (3.3–164.0 ft).

According to a [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations") atlas, 44% of all people live within 150 km (93 mi) of the sea. Because of their importance in society and high concentration of population, the coast is important for major parts of the global food and economic system, and they provide many [ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_service "Ecosystem service") to humankind. For example, important human activities happen in [port cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port "Port"). Coastal [fisheries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisheries "Fisheries") for commercial, recreational, and subsistence purposes, and [aquaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaculture "Aquaculture") are major economic activities and provide jobs, livelihoods, and [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein") for the majority of coastal human populations. Other coastal spaces like [beaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach "Beach") and [seaside resorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaside_resort "Seaside resort") generate economic activity through [tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism "Tourism"). [Marine coastal ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_coastal_ecosystem "Marine coastal ecosystem") can also provide protection against [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise "Sea level rise") and [tsunamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsunami "Tsunami"). In many countries, the coastal [mangrove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangrove "Mangrove") is the primary source of wood for fuel (e.g., charcoal) and building materials. Coastal ecosystems have a much higher capacity for [carbon sequestration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sequestration "Carbon sequestration") than many [terrestrial ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_ecosystem "Terrestrial ecosystem"), and as such can play a critical role in the near future to help [mitigate climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitigate_climate_change "Mitigate climate change") effects by uptake of [atmospheric anthropogenic carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_in_Earth%27s_atmosphere "Carbon dioxide in Earth's atmosphere").

A subcontinental area of land surrounded by water is an [island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island "Island"), and a chain of islands is an [archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago "Archipelago"). The smaller the island, the larger the percentage of its land area will be adjacent to the water, and subsequently will be coast or beach. Islands can be formed by a variety of processes. The [Hawaiian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_islands "Hawaiian islands"), for example, even though they are not near a plate boundary, formed from [isolated volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotspot_(geology) "Hotspot (geology)"). [Atolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atolls "Atolls") are ring-shaped islands made of [coral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral "Coral"), created when [subsidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsidence "Subsidence") causes an island to sink beneath the ocean surface and leaves a ring of reefs around it.

### Mountains and plateaus

[Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain "Mountain") are features that usually rise at least 300 metres (980 ft) higher than the surrounding terrain. The [formation of mountain belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_formation "Mountain formation") is called orogenesis, and results from [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics"). For example, where a plate at a convergent plate boundary pushes one plate above the other, mountains could be formed by either collisional events, such that Earth's crust is pushed upwards, or subductional events, where Earth's crust is pushed into the mantle, causing the crust to melt, rise due to its low density, and solidify into hardened rock, thickening the crust.

A [plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateau "Plateau"), also called a high plain or a tableland, is an area of a highland consisting of flat terrain that is raised sharply above the surrounding area on at least one side, creating steep [cliffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliff "Cliff") or [escarpments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escarpment "Escarpment"). Both volcanic activity – such as the [upwelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwelling "Upwelling") of magma and [extrusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrusion "Extrusion") of lava – and erosion of mountains caused by water, glaciers, or aeolian processes can create plateaus. Plateaus are classified according to their surrounding environment as [intermontane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermontane "Intermontane"), [piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foothills "Foothills"), or [continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent"). A few plateaus may have a small flat top, while others are wider. [Buttes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttes "Buttes") are smaller, with less extrusive and more intrusive igneous rock, while plateaus or highlands are the widest, and [mesas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesas "Mesas") are a general-sized plateau with horizontal [bedrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedrock "Bedrock") [strata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strata "Strata").

### Plains and valleys

Wide, flat areas of land are called [plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain "Plain"), which cover more than one-third of Earth's land area. When they occur as lowered areas between mountains, they can create [valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley "Valley"), [canyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canyon "Canyon") (or gorges), and [ravines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravines "Ravines"). A plateau can be thought of as an elevated plain. Plains are known to have fertile soils and be important for agriculture due to their flatness supporting grasses suitable for livestock and facilitating the harvest of crops. [Floodplains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodplain "Floodplain") provided agricultural land for some of the [earliest civilizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization "Cradle of civilization"). Erosion is often a main driver for the creation of plains and valleys, with [rift valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift_valley "Rift valley") being a noticeable exception. [Fjords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord "Fjord") are glacial valleys that can be thousands of meters deep, opening out to the sea.

### Caves and craters

Any natural void in the ground that can be entered by a human can be considered a [cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave "Cave"). They have been important to humans as a place of [shelter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelter_(building) "Shelter (building)") since the dawn of humanity.

[Craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater "Crater") are depressions in the ground, but unlike caves, they do not provide shelter or extend [underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subterranea_(geography) "Subterranea (geography)"). There are many kinds of craters, such as [impact craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_craters "Impact craters"), volcanic [calderas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calderas "Calderas"), and [isostatic depressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isostatic_depression "Isostatic depression"). [Karst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karst "Karst") processes can create both [solution caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_cave "Solution cave"), the most frequent cave type, and craters, as seen in karst [sinkholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinkhole "Sinkhole").

## Layers

The [pedosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedosphere "Pedosphere") is the outermost layer of Earth's continental surface and is composed of soil and subject to [soil formation processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedogenesis "Pedogenesis"). Below it, the [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere "Lithosphere") encompasses both Earth's crust and the uppermost layer of the [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_mantle "Earth's mantle"). The lithosphere rests, or "floats", on top of the mantle below it via [isostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isostasy "Isostasy"). Above the solid ground, the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere "Troposphere") and humans' use of land can be considered layers of the land.

### Land cover

Land cover refers to the material physically present on the land surface, for example, woody crops, herbaceous crops, barren land, and shrub-covered areas. Artificial surfaces (including cities) account for about a third of a percent of all land. [Land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use "Land use") refers to human allocation of land for various purposes, including farming, ranching, and recreation (e.g., national parks); worldwide, there are an estimated 16.7 million km2 (6.4 million sq mi) of cropland, and 33.5 million km2 (12.9 million sq mi) of pastureland.

Land cover change detection using [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing "Remote sensing") and geospatial data provides baseline information for assessing the climate change impacts on habitats and biodiversity, as well as natural resources, in the target areas. Land cover change detection and mapping is a key component of interdisciplinary [land change science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_change_science "Land change science"), which uses it to determine the consequences of land change on climate. [Land change modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_change_modeling "Land change modeling") is used to predict and analyze changes in land cover and use.

### Soil

Soil is a [mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture "Mixture") of [organic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_matter "Organic matter"), [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerals "Minerals"), [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas "Gas"), [liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid "Liquid"), and [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism") that together support [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life "Life"). Soil consists of a solid phase of minerals and organic matter (the soil matrix), as well as a [porous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porous "Porous") phase that holds [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_gas "Soil gas") (the soil atmosphere) and [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water "Water") (the soil solution). Accordingly, soil is a three-[state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_matter "State of matter") system of solids, liquids, and gases. Soil is a product of several factors: the influence of [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate"), [relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain "Terrain") (elevation, orientation, and slope of terrain), organisms, and the soil's [parent materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parent_material "Parent material") (original minerals) interacting over time. It continually undergoes development by way of numerous physical, chemical and biological processes, which include weathering and [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion "Erosion").

Given its complexity and strong internal [connectedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connectedness "Connectedness"), [soil ecologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_ecology "Soil ecology") regard soil as an [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem "Ecosystem"). Soil acts as an engineering medium, a habitat for [soil organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_organisms "Soil organisms"), a recycling system for [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrients "Nutrients") and [organic wastes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_waste "Organic waste"), a regulator of [water quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_quality "Water quality"), a modifier of [atmospheric composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_chemistry "Atmospheric chemistry"), and a medium for [plant growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_growth "Plant growth"), making it a critically important provider of [ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_services "Ecosystem services"). Since soil has a tremendous range of available [niches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_niche "Ecological niche") and [habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat "Habitat"), it contains a prominent part of the Earth's [genetic diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_diversity "Genetic diversity"). A gram of soil can contain billions of organisms, belonging to thousands of species, mostly microbial and largely still unexplored.

Soil is a major component of the Earth's ecosystem. The world's ecosystems are impacted in far-reaching ways by the processes carried out in the soil, with effects ranging from [ozone depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion "Ozone depletion") and [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming "Global warming") to [rainforest destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest_destruction "Rainforest destruction") and [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution "Water pollution"). With respect to Earth's [carbon cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_cycle "Carbon cycle"), soil acts as an important [carbon reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink "Carbon sink"), and it is potentially one of the most reactive to human disturbance and climate change. As the planet warms, it has been predicted that soils will add carbon dioxide to the atmosphere due to increased [biological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_biology "Soil biology") activity at higher temperatures, a [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") (amplification). This prediction has, however, been questioned in consideration of more recent knowledge on [soil carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_carbon "Soil carbon") turnover.

### Continental crust

Continental crust is the layer of [igneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous "Igneous"), [sedimentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary "Sedimentary"), and [metamorphic rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphic_rock "Metamorphic rock") that forms the geological continents and the areas of shallow seabed close to their shores, known as [continental shelves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelves "Continental shelves"). This layer is sometimes called *[sial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sial "Sial")* because its bulk composition is richer in [aluminium silicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_silicate "Aluminium silicate") and has a lower density compared to the [oceanic crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_crust "Oceanic crust"), called *[sima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sima_(geology) "Sima (geology)")*, which is richer in [magnesium silicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_silicate "Magnesium silicate"). Changes in [seismic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_wave "Seismic wave") velocities have shown that at a certain depth (the [Conrad discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_discontinuity "Conrad discontinuity")), there is a reasonably sharp contrast between the more [felsic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felsic "Felsic") upper continental crust and the lower continental crust, which is more [mafic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafic "Mafic") in character.

The composition of land is not uniform across the Earth, varying between locations and between [strata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strata "Strata") within the same location. The most prominent components of upper continental crust include [silicon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide "Silicon dioxide"), [aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide "Aluminium oxide"), and [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium "Magnesium"). The [continental crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_crust "Continental crust") consists of lower-density material such as the igneous rocks [granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite "Granite") and [andesite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andesite "Andesite"). Less common is [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt "Basalt"), a denser volcanic rock that is the primary constituent of the [ocean floors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_floor "Ocean floor"). Sedimentary rock is formed from the accumulation of sediment that becomes buried and [compacted together](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagenesis "Diagenesis"). Nearly 75% of the continental surfaces are covered by sedimentary rocks, although they form about 5% of the crust.

The most abundant [silicate minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_mineral "Silicate mineral") on Earth's surface include [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz "Quartz"), [feldspars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldspar "Feldspar"), [amphibole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibole "Amphibole"), [mica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mica "Mica"), [pyroxene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroxene "Pyroxene") and [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine "Olivine"). Common [carbonate minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate_mineral "Carbonate mineral") include [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite "Calcite") (found in [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone "Limestone")) and [dolomite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolomite_(mineral) "Dolomite (mineral)"). The rock that makes up land is thicker than [oceanic crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_crust "Oceanic crust"), and it is far more varied in terms of composition. About 31% of this continental crust is submerged in shallow water, forming continental shelves.

## Life science

Land provides many [ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_service "Ecosystem service"), such as mitigating climate change, regulating water supply through drainage basins and river systems, and supporting food production. Land resources are finite, which has led to regulations intended to safeguard these ecosystem services and to a set of practices called [sustainable land management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_land_management "Sustainable land management").

### Land biomes

A biome is an area "characterized by its vegetation, soil, climate, and [wildlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife "Wildlife")." There are five major types of biomes on land: grasslands, forests, deserts, tundras, and freshwater. Other types of biomes include shrublands, wetlands, and [polar ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_ice_caps "Polar ice caps"). An [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem "Ecosystem") refers to the interaction between organisms within a particular environment, and a [habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat "Habitat") refers to the environment where a given species or population of organisms lives. Biomes may span more than one continent, and contain a variety of ecosystems and habitats.

* [Deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert "Desert") have an [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid "Arid") climate, generally defined to mean that they receive less than 25 centimetres (9.8 in) of [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation") per year. They make up around one fifth of the Earth's land area, are found on every continent, and can be very hot or very cold (see [polar desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_desert "Polar desert")). They are home to animals and plants which evolved to be tolerant of droughts. In deserts, most erosion is caused by running water, usually during violent [thunderstorms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunderstorms "Thunderstorms"), which cause [flash floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_flood "Flash flood"). Deserts are expanding due to [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification "Desertification"), which is caused by excessive deforestation and overgrazing.
* [Tundra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tundra "Tundra") is a biome where tree growth is hindered by frigid temperatures and short growing seasons. There are types of tundra associated with different regions: Arctic tundra, [alpine tundra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_tundra "Alpine tundra"), and Antarctic tundra.
* A [forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest "Forest") is an area of land dominated by [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree "Tree"). Many definitions of "forest" are used throughout the world, incorporating factors such as tree density, tree height, land use, legal standing, and ecological function. The United Nations' [Food and Agriculture Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Agriculture_Organization "Food and Agriculture Organization") (FAO) defines a forest as: "land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 meters and a [canopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canopy_(biology) "Canopy (biology)") cover of more than 10 per cent, or trees able to reach these thresholds *in situ*. It does not include land that is predominantly under agricultural or urban use." Types of forests include [rainforests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforests "Rainforests"), [deciduous forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous_forests "Deciduous forests"), and [boreal forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreal_forests "Boreal forests").
* [Grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassland "Grassland") are areas where the [vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation "Vegetation") is dominated by grasses ([Poaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaceae "Poaceae")). However, sedge ([Cyperaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyperaceae "Cyperaceae")) and rush ([Juncaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juncaceae "Juncaceae")) can also be found, along with variable proportions of [legumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legumes "Legumes") like [clover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clover "Clover") and other [herbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous_plant "Herbaceous plant"). Grasslands occur naturally on all continents except [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica "Antarctica") and are found in most [ecoregions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecoregion "Ecoregion") of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth"). Furthermore, grasslands are one of the largest biomes on Earth and dominate the landscape worldwide. Types include natural, semi-natural, and agricultural grasslands. [Savannas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannas "Savannas") are grasslands with occasional, scattered trees.

### Fauna and flora

Land plants evolved from [green algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_algae "Green algae"), and they are called [embryophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryophyte "Embryophyte"). They include [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree "Tree"), [shrubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrub "Shrub"), [ferns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fern "Fern"), [grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grass "Grass"), [moss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moss "Moss"), and [flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower "Flower"). Most plants are [vascular plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_plant "Vascular plant"), meaning that their tissues distribute water and minerals throughout the plant. Through [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis "Photosynthesis"), most plants nourish themselves with sunlight and water, breathing in carbon dioxide and breathing out oxygen. Between 20 and 50% of oxygen is produced by land vegetation.

Unlike plants, [terrestrial animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_animals "Terrestrial animals") are not a [monophyletic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophyly "Monophyly")—that is, a group including all terrestrial animals does not encompass all lineages from a [common ancestor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_ancestor "Common ancestor"). This is because there are organisms, such as the [whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale "Whale"), that [evolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cetaceans "Evolution of cetaceans") from terrestrial mammals back to an aquatic lifestyle. Many [megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megafauna "Megafauna") of the past, such as non-avian [dinosaurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinosaurs "Dinosaurs"), have become extinct due to extinction events, e.g., the [Quaternary extinction event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_extinction_event "Quaternary extinction event").

## Humans and land

Land is "deeply intertwined with human development." It is a crucial resource for human survival; humans depend on land for subsistence, and they can develop strong symbolic attachments to it. Access to land can determine "survival and wealth," particularly in developing countries, giving rise to complex power relationships in production and consumption. Most of the world's philosophies and religions recognize a human duty of [stewardship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewardship_(theology) "Stewardship (theology)") towards land and nature.

### Culture

Many humans see land as a source of "spirituality, inspiration, and beauty." Many also derive a sense of belonging from land, especially if it also belonged to their ancestors. Various [religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") teach about a connection between humans and the land (such as veneration of [Bhumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhumi_(goddess) "Bhumi (goddess)"), a personification of the Earth in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"), and the obligation to protect land as [hima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hima_(environmental_protection) "Hima (environmental protection)") in [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam")), and in almost every [Indigenous group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_group "Indigenous group") there are [etiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiological "Etiological") stories about the land they live on. For Indigenous peoples, connection to the land is an important part of their identity and culture, and some religious groups consider a particular area of land to be [sacred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred "Sacred"), such as the [Holy Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land "Holy Land") in the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions").

[Creation myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_myth "Creation myth") in many religions involve stories of the creation of the world by a supernatural [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity") or deities, including accounts wherein the land is separated from the oceans and the air. The Earth itself has often been personified as a [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity"), in particular a [goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddess "Goddess"). In many cultures, the [mother goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_goddess "Mother goddess") is also portrayed as a [fertility deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_deity "Fertility deity"). To the [Aztecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec "Aztec"), Earth was called *[Tonantzin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonantzin "Tonantzin")*—"our mother"; to the [Incas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incas "Incas"), Earth was called *[Pachamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pachamama "Pachamama")*—"mother earth". In [Norse mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_mythology "Norse mythology"), the Earth giantess [Jörð](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B6r%C3%B0 "Jörð") was the mother of [Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor "Thor") and the daughter of [Annar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annar "Annar"). [Ancient Egyptian mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion "Ancient Egyptian religion") is different from that of other cultures because Earth ([Geb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geb "Geb")) is male and the sky ([Nut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nut_(goddess) "Nut (goddess)")) is female.

[Ancient Near Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East "Ancient Near East") cultures conceived of the world as a flat disk of land surrounded by ocean. The [Pyramid Texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_Texts "Pyramid Texts") and [Coffin Texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffin_Texts "Coffin Texts") reveal that the ancient Egyptians believed [Nun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nu_(mythology) "Nu (mythology)") (the ocean) was a circular body surrounding *nbwt* (a term meaning "dry lands" or "islands"). The [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible"), drawing on other Near Eastern ideas, [depicts the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_cosmology#Earth "Biblical cosmology") as a flat disc floating on water, with another expanse of water above it. A similar model is found in the [Homeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric "Homeric") account of the 8th century BC in which "Okeanos, the personified body of water surrounding the circular surface of the Earth, is the begetter of all life and possibly of all gods."

The spherical form of the Earth was suggested by early [Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophers "Greek philosophers"), a belief espoused by [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras "Pythagoras"). Contrary to popular belief, most educated people in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") did not believe the Earth was flat: this misconception is often called the "[Myth of the Flat Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_the_Flat_Earth "Myth of the Flat Earth")". As evidenced by thinkers such as [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas"), the European belief in a [spherical Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_Earth "Spherical Earth") was widespread by this point in time. Prior to [circumnavigation of the planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellan_expedition "Magellan expedition") and the introduction of [space flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_flight "Space flight"), belief in a spherical Earth was based on observations of the secondary effects of the Earth's shape and parallels drawn with the shape of other planets.

### Travel

Humans have commonly [traveled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel "Travel") for business, pleasure, discovery, and adventure, all made easier in recent human history as a result of technologies like [cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car "Car"), [trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Train "Train"), [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplane "Airplane"), and [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship "Ship"). [Land navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_navigation "Land navigation") is an aspect of travel and refers to progressing through unfamiliar terrain using navigational tools like maps with references to terrain, a [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass "Compass"), or [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation "Satellite navigation"). Navigation on land is often facilitated by reference to [landmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landmark "Landmark") – enduring and recognizable natural or artificial features that stand out from their nearby environment and are often visible from long distances. Natural landmarks can be characteristic features, such as mountains or plateaus, with examples including [Table Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_Mountain "Table Mountain") in South Africa, [Mount Ararat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Ararat "Mount Ararat") in Turkey, the [Grand Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon "Grand Canyon") in the United States, [Uluru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluru "Uluru") in Australia, and [Mount Fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Fuji "Mount Fuji") in Japan.

Two major eras of exploration occurred in human history: one of divergence, and one of convergence. The former saw humans moving out of Africa, settling in new lands, and developing distinct cultures in relative isolation. Early explorers settled in Europe and Asia; 14,000 years ago, some crossed the [Ice Age land bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settlement_of_the_Americas "Settlement of the Americas") from Siberia to Alaska and moved southbound to settle in the Americas. For the most part, these cultures were ignorant of each other's existence. The second period, occurring over roughly the last 10,000 years, saw increased cross-cultural exchange through trade and exploration, marking a new era of cultural intermingling.

### Trade

Human trade has occurred since the prehistoric era. [Peter Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Watson_(business_writer) "Peter Watson (business writer)") dates the [history of long-distance commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_international_trade "History of international trade") from c. 150,000 years ago. Major [trade routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_route "Trade route") throughout history have existed on land, such as the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road") which linked [East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia") with [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"), and the [Amber Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber_Road "Amber Road") which was used to transfer [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber "Amber") from [Northern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe "Northern Europe") to the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea"). The [Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Ages_(historiography) "Dark Ages (historiography)") led trade to collapse in the West, but it continued to flourish among the kingdoms of Africa, the Middle East, India, China, and Southeast Asia. During the Middle Ages, Central Asia was the economic centre of the world, and luxury goods were commonly traded in Europe. Physical money (either barter or precious metals) was dangerous to carry over a long distance. To address this, a burgeoning banking industry enabled the shift to movable wealth or capital, making it far easier and safer to trade across long distances. After the [Age of Sail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Sail "Age of Sail"), international trade mostly occurred along sea routes, notably to prevent intermediary countries from being able to control trade routes and the flow of goods.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In economics, [*land*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_(economics) "Land (economics)") refers to a [factor of production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_of_production "Factor of production"). It can be leased in exchange for [rent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renting "Renting") and the use of its various [raw material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_material "Raw material") resources (trees, oil, metals).

### Land use

Land is the foundation of agriculture, supporting over 95% of food production while providing essential ecosystem services that sustain life on Earth. As a finite resource, [agricultural land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_land "Agricultural land") faces pressures from competing demands including urban expansion, biofuel production, and changing consumption patterns driven by rising incomes and shifting diets. Land is the basis of food security, biodiversity conservation, climate regulation and – in 2025 – the livelihoods of 892 million agricultural workers worldwide.

For more than 10,000 years, humans have engaged in activities on land such as [hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunting "Hunting"), [foraging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foraging "Foraging"), [controlled burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn "Controlled burn"), [land clearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_clearing "Land clearing"), and [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture"). Beginning with the [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution") and the spread of agriculture around the world, human land use has significantly altered [terrestrial ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_ecosystem "Terrestrial ecosystem"), with an essentially global transformation of Earth's landscape by 3000 years ago. From around 1750, human land use has increased at an accelerating rate due to the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"), which created a greater demand for natural resources and caused rapid population growth.

Agriculture includes both [crop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop "Crop") farming and [animal husbandry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_husbandry "Animal husbandry"). A third of Earth's land surface is used for agriculture, with an estimated 16.7 million km2 (6.4 million sq mi) of cropland and 33.5 million km2 (12.9 million sq mi) of pastureland. This has had significant impacts on Earth's ecosystems. When land is cleared to make way for agriculture, native flora and fauna are replaced with newly introduced crops and livestock. Excessively high agricultural land use is driven by poor management practices (which lead to lower food yields, necessitating more land use), food demand, [food waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_waste "Food waste"), and [diets high in meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_meat_production#Land_use "Environmental impact of meat production").

[Urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization "Urbanization") has led to greater population growth in [urban areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_areas "Urban areas") in the last century. Although urban areas make up less than 3 percent of Earth's land area, the global population shifted from a majority living in [rural areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_area "Rural area") to a majority living in urban areas in 2007. People living in urban areas depend on food produced in rural areas outside of their cities, which creates greater demand for agriculture and drives [land use change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use_change "Land use change") well beyond city boundaries. Urbanization also displaces agricultural land because it mainly takes place on the most fertile land. Urban expansion in [peri-urban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peri-urban "Peri-urban") areas fragments agricultural and natural lands, forcing agriculture to move to less fertile land elsewhere. Because this land is less fertile, more land is needed for the same output, which increases the total agricultural land use.

Another form of land use is [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining "Mining"), whereby [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerals "Minerals") are extracted from the ground using a variety of methods. Evidence of mining activity dates back to around 3000 BCE in [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"). Important minerals include [iron ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_ore "Iron ore"), mined for use as a [raw material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_material "Raw material"); [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal "Coal"), mined for [energy production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel "Fossil fuel"); and [gemstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemstone "Gemstone"), mined for use in [jewellery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewellery "Jewellery") and [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency "Currency").

### Land ownership and reform

Ownership of agricultural land and its distribution among the population is a key driver of a wide range of [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_inequality "Social inequality"), political and [economic inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality "Economic inequality"). [Land reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform "Land reform") (also known as agrarian reform) refers to the process of changing who owns agricultural land and who has the right to use these lands. Redistributive land reform refers to the narrower concept of transferring agricultural land ownership from large private landowners to small farmers and people without any landholdings. It involves the [redistribution of wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistribution_of_income_and_wealth "Redistribution of income and wealth") and [political power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_(political_science) "Power (political science)") that comes from owning large swathes of land. There are different types of land reform, including state-led and market-led agrarian reforms.

### Law

The phrase "[the law of the land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_land "Law of the land")" first appeared in 1215 in [Magna Carta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta "Magna Carta"), inspiring its later usage in the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution "United States Constitution"). The idea of [common land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_land "Common land") also originated with medieval [English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_law "English law"), and refers to collective ownership of land, treating it as a [common good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_good "Common good"). In environmental science, economics, and game theory, the [tragedy of the commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy_of_the_commons "Tragedy of the commons") refers to individuals' use of common spaces for their own gain, deteriorating the land overall by taking more than their fair share and not cooperating with others. The idea of common land suggests public ownership; but there is still some land that can be privatized as [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property "Property") for an individual, such as a [landlord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landlord "Landlord") or [king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King "King"). In the developed world, land is expected to be privately owned by an individual with legal [title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_(property) "Title (property)"), but in the developing world the right to use land is often divided, with the rights to land resources being given to different people at different times for the same area of land. Beginning in the late 20th century, the international community has begun to recognise [Indigenous land rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_land_rights "Indigenous land rights") in law, for example, the [Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Waitangi "Treaty of Waitangi") for [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people "Māori people") people, the [Act on Greenland Self-Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Greenlandic_self-government_referendum "2008 Greenlandic self-government referendum") for [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit "Inuit"), and the Indigenous Peoples Rights Act in the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines "Philippines").

### Geopolitics

Borders are geographical boundaries imposed either by geographic features ([oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean"), [mountain ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_range "Mountain range"), [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River "River")) or by [political entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_entities "Political entities") ([governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government "Government"), states, or subnational entities). Political borders can be established through [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War "War"), [colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonization "Colonization"), or mutual agreements between the political entities that reside in those areas; the creation of these agreements is called [boundary delimitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_boundary_delimitation "National boundary delimitation").

Many [wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War "War") and other conflicts have occurred in efforts by participants to expand the land under their control, or to assert control of a specific area considered to hold strategic, historical, or cultural significance. The [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire") of the 13th and 14th centuries became the [largest contiguous land empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_empires "List of largest empires") in [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history "Human history") through war and conquest.

In the [19th-century United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th_century_in_the_United_States "19th century in the United States"), a concept of [manifest destiny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifest_destiny "Manifest destiny") was developed by various groups, asserting that American settlers were destined to expand across [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America"). This concept was used to justify military action against the [indigenous peoples of North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_North_America "Indigenous peoples of North America") and [of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Mexico "Indigenous peoples of Mexico").

The aggression of [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") was motivated in part by the concept of *[Lebensraum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebensraum "Lebensraum")* ("living space"), which had first become a geopolitical goal of [Imperial Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Germany "Imperial Germany") in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") (1914–1918), originally as the core element of the *[Septemberprogramm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septemberprogramm "Septemberprogramm")* of territorial expansion. The most extreme form of this ideology was supported by the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party "Nazi Party") (NSDAP). Lebensraum was one of the leading motivations Nazi Germany had in initiating [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), and it would continue this policy until the end of World War II.

## Environmental issues

Land degradation is "the reduction or loss of the biological or economic productivity and complexity" of land as a result of human activity. Land degradation is driven by many different activities, including agriculture, urbanization, energy production, and mining. Humans have altered more than three-quarters of ice-free land through habitation and other use, fundamentally changing ecosystems. Human activity is a major factor in the [Holocene extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_extinction "Holocene extinction"), and human-caused climate change is causing [rising sea levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rising_sea_levels "Rising sea levels") and ecosystem loss. Environmental scientists study land ecosystems, natural resources, the [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere "Biosphere") ([fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna "Fauna") and [flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora "Flora")), [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere "Troposphere"), and the impact of human activity on these. Their recommendations have led to international action to prevent [biodiversity loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss "Biodiversity loss") and [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification "Desertification"), and encourage sustainable [forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_management "Forest management") and [waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_management "Waste management") management. The [conservation movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_movement "Conservation movement") lobbies for the protection of [endangered species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_species "Endangered species") and the protection of natural areas, such as [parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parks "Parks"). International frameworks have focused on analyzing how humans can meet their needs while using land more efficiently and preserving its natural resources, notably under the United Nations' [Sustainable Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals "Sustainable Development Goals") framework.

### Soil degradation

Human land use can cause soil to degrade, both in quality and in quantity. Soil degradation can be caused by [agrochemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrochemical "Agrochemical") (such as [fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer "Fertilizer"), [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide "Pesticide"), and [herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide "Herbicide")), [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure "Infrastructure") development, and [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining "Mining"), among other activities. Several different processes lead to soil degradation. Physical processes, such as [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_erosion "Soil erosion"), [sealing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_sealing "Soil sealing"), and [crusting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_crust#Physical_soil_crusts "Soil crust"), lead to the structural breakdown of the soil. This means water cannot penetrate the soil surface, causing [surface runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff "Surface runoff"). Chemical processes, such as [salinization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_salinity "Soil salinity"), [acidification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_acidification "Soil acidification"), and [toxication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxication "Toxication"), lead to chemical imbalances in the soil. Salinization in particular is detrimental, as it makes land less productive for agriculture and affects at least 20% of all irrigated lands. Deliberate disruption of soil in the form of [tillage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tillage "Tillage") can also alter biological processes in the soil, which leads to excessive [mineralization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralization_(soil_science) "Mineralization (soil science)") and the loss of nutrients.

[Desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification "Desertification") is a type of land degradation in [drylands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drylands "Drylands") in which fertile areas become increasingly arid as a result of natural processes or human activities, resulting in loss of biological productivity. This spread of arid areas can be influenced by a variety of human factors, such as [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation "Deforestation"), improper [land management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_management "Land management"), [overgrazing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overgrazing "Overgrazing"), anthropogenic [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change"), and [overexploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overexploitation "Overexploitation") of [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil "Soil"). Throughout geological history, desertification has occurred naturally, though in recent times it is greatly accelerated by human activity.

### Pollution

Ground pollution is [soil contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_contamination "Soil contamination") via [pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollutants "Pollutants"), such as [hazardous waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_waste "Hazardous waste") or [litter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litter "Litter"). Ground pollution can be prevented by properly monitoring and disposing of waste, along with reducing unnecessary chemical and plastic use. Unfortunately, proper disposal of waste often is not economically beneficial or technologically viable, leading to short-term solutions of waste disposal that pollute the earth. Examples include dumping harmful industrial byproducts, overusing agricultural fertilizers and other chemicals, and poorly maintaining [landfills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landfills "Landfills"). Some landfills can be thousands of acres in size, such as the [Apex Regional landfill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apex_landfill "Apex landfill") in Las Vegas.

[Water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution "Water pollution") on land is the contamination of non-oceanic hydrological surface and underground water features such as [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakes "Lakes"), [ponds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponds "Ponds"), [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers "Rivers"), [streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streams "Streams"), [wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetlands "Wetlands"), [aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifers "Aquifers"), [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoirs "Reservoirs"), and [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater "Groundwater") as a result of human activities. It may be caused by toxic substances (e.g., oil, metals, plastics, [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide "Pesticide"), [persistent organic pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant "Persistent organic pollutant"), industrial waste products), stressful conditions (e.g., changes of pH, [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_(environmental) "Hypoxia (environmental)") or anoxia, increased temperatures, excessive turbidity, unpleasant taste or odor, and changes of [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity "Salinity")), or [pathogenic organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen "Pathogen").

### Biodiversity loss

The [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity "Biodiversity") of Earth—the variety and variability of life—is threatened by climate change, human activities, and invasive species. Due to an increase in the rate of [extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction "Extinction"), biodiversity loss is increasing. Agriculture can cause biodiversity loss as land is converted for agricultural use at a very high rate, particularly in the tropics, which directly causes habitat loss. The use of pesticides and herbicides can also negatively impact the health of local species. Ecosystems can also be divided and degraded by [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure "Infrastructure") development outside of urban areas.

Biodiversity loss can sometimes be reversed through [ecological restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_restoration "Ecological restoration") or [ecological resilience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_resilience "Ecological resilience"), such as through the restoration of abandoned agricultural areas; however, it may also be permanent (e.g., through [land loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_loss "Land loss")). The planet's ecosystem is quite sensitive: occasionally, minor changes from a healthy [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_point "Equilibrium point") can have dramatic influence on a [food web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_web "Food web") or [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain "Food chain"), up to and including the [coextinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coextinction "Coextinction") of that entire food chain. Biodiversity loss leads to reduced [ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_services "Ecosystem services"), and it can eventually threaten [food security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_security "Food security"). Earth is currently undergoing its [sixth mass extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_mass_extinction "Sixth mass extinction") (the *Holocene extinction*) as a result of human activities which push beyond the [planetary boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_boundaries "Planetary boundaries"). So far, this extinction has proven irreversible.

### Resource depletion

Although humans have used land for its [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources "Natural resources") since ancient times, demand for resources such as [timber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timber "Timber"), [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerals "Minerals"), and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy "Energy") has grown exponentially since the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution") due to population growth. When a natural resource is depleted to the point of [diminishing returns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminishing_returns "Diminishing returns"), it is considered the overexploitation of that resource. Some natural resources – such as timber – are considered renewable because, with sustainable practices, they replenish to their previous levels. [Fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels "Fossil fuels") such as [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal "Coal") are not considered renewable, as they take millions of years to form, with the current supply of coal expected to peak in the middle of the 21st century. [Economic materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_materialism "Economic materialism"), or [consumerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumerism "Consumerism"), has influenced destructive patterns of modern resource usage, in contrast with pre-industrial usage.
