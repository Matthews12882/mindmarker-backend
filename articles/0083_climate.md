# Climate

**Climate** is the long-term [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather "Weather") pattern in a region, typically averaged over 30 years. More rigorously, it is the mean and [variability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_dispersion "Statistical dispersion") of [meteorological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology "Meteorology") variables over a time spanning from months to millions of years. Some of the [meteorological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology "Meteorology") variables that are commonly measured are [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature "Temperature"), [humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity "Humidity"), [atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure "Atmospheric pressure"), [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind "Wind"), and [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation"). In a broader sense, climate is the state of the components of the [climate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_system "Climate system"), including the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere "Atmosphere"), [hydrosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosphere "Hydrosphere"), [cryosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryosphere "Cryosphere"), [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere "Lithosphere") and [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere "Biosphere") and the interactions between them. The climate of a location is affected by its [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude "Latitude"), [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude "Longitude"), [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain "Terrain"), [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude "Altitude"), [land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use "Land use") and nearby [water bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water "Body of water") and their currents.

Climates can be [classified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_classification "Climate classification") according to the average and typical variables, most commonly [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature "Temperature") and [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation"). The most widely used classification scheme is the [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification "Köppen climate classification"). The [Thornthwaite system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornthwaite_climate_classification "Thornthwaite climate classification"), in use since 1948, incorporates [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration "Evapotranspiration") along with temperature and [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation "Precipitation") information and is used in studying [biological diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_diversity "Biological diversity") and how [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") affects it. The major classifications in Thornthwaite's climate classification are microthermal, mesothermal, and megathermal. Finally, the Bergeron and [Spatial Synoptic Classification systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_Synoptic_Classification_system "Spatial Synoptic Classification system") focus on the origin of air masses that define the climate of a region.

[Paleoclimatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimatology "Paleoclimatology") is the study of ancient climates. [Paleoclimatologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimatologists "Paleoclimatologists") seek to explain climate variations for all parts of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") during any given [geologic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology "Geology") period, beginning with the time of the Earth's formation. Since very few direct observations of climate were available before the 19th century, [paleoclimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimate "Paleoclimate") are inferred from [proxy variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_(climate) "Proxy (climate)"). They include non-biotic evidence—such as [sediments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment "Sediment") found in [lake beds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_beds "Lake beds") and [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core "Ice core")—and [biotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotic_component "Biotic component") evidence—such as [tree rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrochronology "Dendrochronology") and coral. [Climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_model "Climate model") are mathematical models of past, present, and future climates. Climate change may occur over long and short timescales due to various factors. Recent warming is discussed in terms of [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change"), which results in redistributions of [biota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life "Life"). For example, as climate scientist [Lesley Ann Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesley_Ann_Hughes "Lesley Ann Hughes") has written: "a 3 °C [5 °F] change in mean annual temperature corresponds to a shift in isotherms of approximately 300–400 km [190–250 mi] in latitude (in the temperate zone) or 500 m [1,600 ft] in elevation. Therefore, species are expected to move upwards in elevation or towards the poles in [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude "Latitude") in response to shifting climate zones."

## Definition

Climate (from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language")  *κλίμα* 'inclination') is commonly defined as the weather averaged over a long period. The standard averaging period is 30 years, but other periods may be used depending on the purpose. Climate also includes statistics other than the average, such as the magnitudes of day-to-day or year-to-year variations. The [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Panel_on_Climate_Change "Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change") (IPCC) [2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Third_Assessment_Report "IPCC Third Assessment Report") glossary definition is as follows:

> "Climate in a narrow sense is usually defined as the "average weather", or more rigorously, as the statistical description in terms of the mean and variability of relevant quantities over a period ranging from months to thousands or millions of years. The classical period is 30 years, as defined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). These quantities are most often surface variables such as temperature, precipitation, and wind. Climate in a wider sense is the state, including a statistical description, of the climate system."

The [World Meteorological Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Meteorological_Organization "World Meteorological Organization") (WMO) describes "[climate normals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_normal "Climate normal")" as "reference points used by [climatologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climatology "Climatology") to compare current climatological trends to that of the past or what is considered typical. A climate normal is defined as the arithmetic average of a climate element (e.g. temperature) over a 30-year period. A 30-year period is used as it is long enough to filter out any interannual variation or anomalies such as [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Ni%C3%B1o%E2%80%93Southern_Oscillation "El Niño–Southern Oscillation"), but also short enough to be able to show longer climatic trends."

The WMO originated from the [International Meteorological Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Meteorological_Organization "International Meteorological Organization") which set up a technical commission for climatology in 1929. At its 1934 [Wiesbaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiesbaden "Wiesbaden") meeting, the technical commission designated the thirty-year period from 1901 to 1930 as the reference time frame for climatological standard normals. In 1982, the WMO agreed to update climate normals, and these were subsequently completed on the basis of climate data from 1 January 1961 to 31 December 1990. The 1961–1990 climate normals serve as the baseline reference period. The next set of climate normals to be published by WMO is from 1991 to 2020. Aside from collecting from the most common atmospheric variables (air temperature, pressure, precipitation and wind), other variables such as humidity, visibility, cloud amount, solar radiation, soil temperature, pan evaporation rate, days with thunder and days with hail are also collected to measure change in climate conditions.

The difference between climate and weather is usefully summarized by the popular phrase "Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get." Over [historical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History "History") time spans, there are a number of nearly constant variables that determine climate, including [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude "Latitude"), altitude, proportion of land to water, and proximity to oceans and mountains. All of these variables change only over periods of millions of years due to processes such as [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics"). Other climate determinants are more dynamic: the [thermohaline circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermohaline_circulation "Thermohaline circulation") of the ocean leads to a 5 °C (9 °F) warming of the northern Atlantic Ocean compared to other ocean basins. Other [ocean currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current "Ocean current") redistribute heat between land and water on a more regional scale. The density and type of vegetation coverage affects solar heat absorption, water retention, and rainfall on a regional level. Alterations in the quantity of atmospheric [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas") (particularly [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") and [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane")) determines the amount of solar energy retained by the planet, leading to [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") or [global cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_cooling "Global cooling"). The variables which determine climate are numerous and the interactions complex, but there is general agreement that the broad outlines are understood, at least insofar as the determinants of historical climate change are concerned.

## Climate classification

Climate classifications are systems that categorize the world's climates. A climate classification may correlate closely with a [biome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome "Biome") classification, as climate is a major influence on life in a region. One of the most used is the [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification "Köppen climate classification") scheme first developed in 1899.

There are several ways to classify climates into similar regimes. Originally, [climes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clime "Clime") were defined in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") to describe the weather depending upon a location's latitude. Modern climate classification methods can be broadly divided into *genetic* methods, which focus on the causes of climate, and *empiric* methods, which focus on the effects of climate. Examples of genetic classification include methods based on the [relative frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_frequency "Relative frequency") of different [air mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_mass "Air mass") types or locations within [synoptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_scale_meteorology "Synoptic scale meteorology") weather disturbances. Examples of [empiric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical "Empirical") classifications include [climate zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_zone "Climate zone") defined by [plant hardiness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_hardiness "Plant hardiness"), evapotranspiration, or more generally the [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification "Köppen climate classification") which was originally designed to identify the climates associated with certain [biomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome "Biome"). A common shortcoming of these [classification schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_scheme "Classification scheme") is that they produce distinct boundaries between the zones they define, rather than the gradual transition of climate properties more common in nature.

## Record

### Paleoclimatology

Paleoclimatology is the study of past climate over a great period of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth")'s history. It uses evidence with different time scales (from decades to millennia) from ice sheets, tree rings, sediments, pollen, coral, and rocks to determine the past state of the climate. It demonstrates periods of stability and periods of change and can indicate whether changes follow patterns such as regular cycles.

### Modern

Details of the modern climate record are known through the taking of measurements from such weather instruments as [thermometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer "Thermometer"), [barometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometer "Barometer"), and [anemometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anemometer "Anemometer") during the past few centuries. The instruments used to study weather over the modern time scale, their observation frequency, their known error, their immediate environment, and their exposure have changed over the years, which must be considered when studying the climate of centuries past. Long-term modern climate records skew towards population centres and affluent countries. Since the 1960s, the launch of satellites allow records to be gathered on a global scale, including areas with little to no human presence, such as the Arctic region and oceans.

## Climate variability

Climate variability is the term to describe variations in the mean state and other characteristics of climate (such as chances or possibility of [extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather "Extreme weather"), etc.) "on all spatial and temporal scales beyond that of individual weather events." Some of the variability does not appear to be caused systematically and occurs at random times. Such variability is called *random variability* or *[noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_(signal_processing) "Noise (signal processing)")*. On the other hand, periodic variability occurs relatively regularly and in distinct modes of variability or climate patterns.

There are close correlations between Earth's climate oscillations and astronomical factors ([barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter "Barycenter") changes, [solar variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_variation "Solar variation"), [cosmic ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray") flux, [cloud albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_albedo "Cloud albedo") [feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_feedback "Cloud feedback"), [Milankovic cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milankovic_cycle "Milankovic cycle")), and modes of [heat distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") between the ocean-atmosphere climate system. In some cases, current, historical and [paleoclimatological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimate "Paleoclimate") natural oscillations may be masked by significant [volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_eruption "Volcanic eruption"), [impact events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event "Impact event"), irregularities in [climate proxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_proxy "Climate proxy") data, [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") processes or [anthropogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment "Human impact on the environment") [emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas#Greenhouse_gas_emissions_from_human_activities "Greenhouse gas") of substances such as [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas").

Over the years, the definitions of *climate variability* and the related term *[climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change")* have shifted. While the term *climate change* now implies change that is both long-term and of human causation, in the 1960s the word climate change was used for what we now describe as climate variability, that is, climatic inconsistencies and anomalies.

## Climate change

Climate change is the variation in global or regional climates over time. It reflects changes in the variability or average state of the atmosphere over time scales ranging from decades to millions of years. These changes can be caused by processes internal to the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth"), external forces (e.g. variations in sunlight intensity) or human activities, as found recently. Scientists have identified [Earth's Energy Imbalance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_Energy_Imbalance "Earth's Energy Imbalance") (EEI) to be a fundamental metric of the status of global change.

In recent usage, especially in the context of [environmental policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_policy "Environmental policy"), the term "climate change" often refers only to changes in modern climate, including the rise in average surface [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature "Temperature") known as [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change"). In some cases, the term is also used with a presumption of human causation, as in the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations") [Framework Convention on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNFCCC "UNFCCC") (UNFCCC). The UNFCCC uses "climate variability" for non-human caused variations.

Earth has undergone periodic climate shifts in the past, including four major [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age "Ice age"). These consist of glacial periods where conditions are colder than normal, separated by [interglacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interglacial "Interglacial") periods. The accumulation of snow and ice during a glacial period increases the surface [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo "Albedo"), reflecting more of the Sun's energy into space and maintaining a lower atmospheric temperature. Increases in [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas"), such as by [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_impacts_on_the_oceans "Volcanic impacts on the oceans"), can increase the global temperature and produce an interglacial period. Suggested causes of ice age periods include the positions of the [continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent "Continent"), variations in the Earth's orbit, changes in the solar output, and volcanism. However, these naturally caused changes in climate occur on a much slower time scale than the present rate of change which is caused by the emission of greenhouse gases by human activities.

According to the EU's Copernicus Climate Change Service, average global air temperature has passed 1.5C of warming the period from February 2023 to January 2024.

## Climate models

[Climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_model "Climate model") use quantitative methods to simulate the interactions and transfer of radiative energy between the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_atmosphere "Earth's atmosphere"), [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean "Ocean"), land surface and ice through a series of physics equations. They are used for a variety of purposes, from the study of the dynamics of the weather and climate system to projections of future climate. All climate models balance, or very nearly balance, incoming energy as short wave (including visible) electromagnetic radiation to the Earth with outgoing energy as long wave (infrared) electromagnetic radiation from the Earth. Any imbalance results in a change in the average temperature of the Earth.

Climate models are available on different resolutions ranging from >100 km to 1 km. High resolutions in [global climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_climate_model "Global climate model") require significant computational resources, and so only a few global datasets exist. Global climate models can be dynamically or statistically downscaled to regional climate models to analyze impacts of climate change on a local scale. Examples are ICON or mechanistically downscaled data such as CHELSA (Climatologies at high resolution for the earth's land surface areas).

The most talked-about applications of these models in recent years have been their use to infer the consequences of increasing greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, primarily [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") (see [greenhouse gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas")). These models predict an upward trend in the [global mean surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_temperature_record "Surface temperature record"), with the most rapid increase in temperature being projected for the higher latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere.

Models can range from relatively simple to quite complex. Simple radiant heat transfer models treat the Earth as a single point and average outgoing energy. This can be expanded vertically (as in radiative-convective models), or horizontally. Finally, more complex (coupled) atmosphere–ocean–[sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice "Sea ice") [global climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_climate_model "Global climate model") discretise and solve the full equations for mass and energy transfer and radiant exchange.
