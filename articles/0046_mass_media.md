# Mass media

**Mass media** refers to the forms of [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_(communication) "Media (communication)") that reach large audiences via [mass communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_communication "Mass communication"). It includes [broadcast media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting "Broadcasting"), [digital media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media "Digital media"), [print media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publishing "Publishing"), [social media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media "Social media"), [streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media "Streaming media"), [advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising "Advertising"), and [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_marketing "Event marketing").

Mass media encompasses [news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News "News"), [advocacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advocacy "Advocacy"), [entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment "Entertainment"), and [public service announcements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service_announcement "Public service announcement"), and intersects with the study of [marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing "Marketing"), [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda "Propaganda"), [public relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations "Public relations"), [political communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_communication "Political communication"), [journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism "Journalism"), [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art "Art"), [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama "Drama"), [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing "Computing"), and [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology "Technology"). The [influence of mass media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence_of_mass_media "Influence of mass media") on individuals and groups has also been analysed from the standpoint of [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology"), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics"), [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History "History"), [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law "Law"), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology"), and [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology "Sociology").

Mass media is often controlled by [media conglomerates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_conglomerate "Media conglomerate"), which may include mass media organisations, companies, and networks, and may be susceptible to [media capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_capture "Media capture").

## Definition

In the late 20th century, mass media could be classified into eight mass media industries: books, the Internet, magazines, movies, newspapers, radio, recordings, and television. The explosion of digital [communication technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_and_communications_technology "Information and communications technology") in the late 20th and early 21st centuries challenged this classification. By the early 2000s, a classification called the "seven mass media" came into use, comprising:

1. [Print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing "Printing") (books, pamphlets, newspapers, magazines, posters, etc.) – late 15th century
2. [Recordings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_recording_and_reproduction "Sound recording and reproduction") ([gramophone records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gramophone_record "Gramophone record"), [magnetic tapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_tape "Magnetic tape"), [cassettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassette_tape "Cassette tape"), [cartridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereo-Pak "Stereo-Pak"), [CDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD "CD"), and [DVDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD "DVD")) – late 19th century
3. [Cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film "Film") – c. 1900
4. [Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_broadcasting "Radio broadcasting") – c. 1910
5. [Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television "Television") – c. 1950
6. The [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet "Internet") – c. 1990
7. [Mobile phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_phone "Mobile phone") – c. 2000

The sixth and seventh media, Internet and mobile phones, are often referred to collectively as [digital media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media "Digital media"), and the fourth and fifth, radio and TV, as [broadcast media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_media "Broadcast media"). Some argue that [video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_games "Video games") have developed into a distinct mass form of media.

### Characteristics

Five characteristics of mass communication have been identified by sociologist [John Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Thompson_(sociologist) "John Thompson (sociologist)") of [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University"):

1. "Comprises both technical and institutional methods of production and distribution".[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]
2. Involves the "[commodification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodification "Commodification") of symbolic forms"[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]
3. "Separate contexts between the production and reception of information"[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]
4. Its "reach to those 'far removed' in time and space, in comparison to the producers"[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]
5. "Information distribution" – a "one-to-many" form of communication, whereby products are [mass-produced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_production "Mass production") and disseminated to large audiences

### Mainstream media

In common usage, the term "mass" denotes not that a given number of individuals receives the products, but rather that the products are available in principle to a plurality of recipients. The term "mass media" is sometimes used as a synonym for "[mainstream media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainstream_media "Mainstream media")". However, mass media may include [alternative media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_media "Alternative media") outlets that employ [mass communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_communication "Mass communication") technology, even if their audience is smaller than mainstream media. In contrast, mainstream media are distinguished from alternative media by their content and point of view.

## History

The first dated print book known as the [Diamond Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra "Diamond Sutra") was printed in China in 868 AD, although it is clear that books were printed earlier.[*[according to whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")*] [Movable type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type "Movable type") made of clay was invented in 1041 in China. However, due to the slow spread of literacy in China and the relatively high cost of paper there, the earliest printed mass medium was probably the European [popular prints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_prints "Popular prints") from about 1400.[*[according to whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")*] Although these were produced in huge numbers, very few early examples survived, and even most known to be printed before about 1600 have not survived.

The term "mass media" was coined with the creation of print media, which is notable for being the first example of mass media as we use the term today.[*[according to whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")*] This form of media started in Europe in the Middle Ages.[*[according to whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")*]

[Johannes Gutenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Gutenberg "Johannes Gutenberg")'s invention of the [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press "Printing press") allowed for the mass production of books to [spread globally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_spread_of_the_printing_press "Global spread of the printing press"). He printed the [Latin Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutenberg_Bible "Gutenberg Bible") on a printing press with movable type in 1453. The invention of the printing press gave rise to some of the first forms of mass communication by enabling the publication of books and newspapers on a scale much larger than was previously possible. The invention also transformed the way the world received printed materials, although books remained too expensive to be called a mass medium for at least a century after that.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Newspapers developed from c. 1612, with the first English example in 1620, although they took until the 19th century to reach a mass audience. The first high-circulation newspapers arose in London in the early 1800s, such as *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")*, and were made possible by the invention of the high-speed rotary steam printing press and railroads which allowed large-scale distribution over wide geographical areas. The increase in circulation, however, led to a decline in feedback and interactivity from the readership, making newspapers a one-way medium.

The phrase "the media" began to be used in the 1920s. The notion of "mass media" was generally restricted to print media until after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), when radio, television and video were introduced. The audio-visual facilities became very popular, because they provided both information and entertainment, because the colour and sound engaged the viewers/listeners and because it was easier for the general public to passively watch TV or listen to the radio than to actively read.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

During the 20th century, the growth of mass media was driven by [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology "Technology"), including that which allowed large-scale duplication of material. Physical duplication technologies such as [printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing "Printing"), [record pressing](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Record_pressing&action=edit&redlink=1 "Record pressing (page does not exist)") and [film duplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Film_duplication&action=edit&redlink=1 "Film duplication (page does not exist)") allowed the distribution of books, newspapers and movies at low prices to huge audiences. [Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio "Radio") and [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television "Television") allowed the electronic duplication of information for the first time. Mass media had the economics of linear replication: a single work could make money proportional to the number of copies sold, and as volumes went up, unit costs went down, increasing profit margins further. Vast fortunes were made in mass media.

In a democratic society, the media can serve the [electorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituency "Constituency") about issues regarding government and corporate entities. Some consider the [concentration of media ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_of_media_ownership "Concentration of media ownership") to be a threat to democracy.

In recent times, the Internet has become the latest and most popular mass medium. Information has become readily available through websites, and easily accessible through [search engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine "Search engine"). Modern-day mass media includes the internet, mobile phones, [blogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blog "Blog"), [podcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast "Podcast") and [RSS feeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSS_feed "RSS feed").

### Mergers and acquisitions

Between 1985 and 2018, about 76,720 deals had been announced in the media industry, with an overall value of around [US$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Dollar "US Dollar")5,634 billion. There have been three major waves of mergers and acquisitions (M&A) in the mass media sector (2000, 2007 and 2015), while the most active year in terms of numbers was 2007 with around 3,808 deals. The United States is the most prominent country in media M&A with 41 of the top 50 deals having an acquirer from the United States.[*[importance?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:What_Wikipedia_is_not#Encyclopedic_content "Wikipedia:What Wikipedia is not")*]

The largest deal in history was the acquisition of [Time Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WarnerMedia "WarnerMedia") by [AOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOL "AOL") Inc. for US$164,746.86 million.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] In 2012, an article asserted that 90 percent of US mass media—including radio, video news, sports entertainment, and other—were owned by six major companies ([General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric"), [News Corp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Corp "News Corp"), [Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney "Disney"), [Viacom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viacom_(2005%E2%80%932019) "Viacom (2005–2019)"), [Time Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Warner "Time Warner") and [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS "CBS")).[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

## Influence and sociology

In 1997, J. R. Finnegan Jr. and K. Viswanath identified three main theories of the effects or functions of mass media.

* First, *The Knowledge Gap*: the mass media influences knowledge gaps due to factors including "the extent to which the content is appealing, the degree to which information channels are accessible and desirable, and the amount of social conflict and diversity there is in a community".[*[quote needs citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Inline_citation#When_you_must_use_inline_citations "Wikipedia:Inline citation")*]

* Second, *[Agenda Setting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda-setting_theory "Agenda-setting theory")*: people are influenced in how they think about issues due to the selective nature of what media groups choose for public consumption. J. J. Davis states that "when risks are highlighted in the media, particularly in great detail, the extent of agenda setting is likely to be based on the degree to which a public sense of outrage and threat is provoked". When wanting to set an agenda, framing can be invaluably useful to a mass media organisation. Framing involves "taking a leadership role in the organisation of public discourse about an issue".[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] The media is influenced by the desire for balance in coverage, and the resulting pressures can come from groups with particular political action and advocacy positions. Finnegan and Viswanath say "groups, institutions and advocates compete to identify problems, to move them onto the public agenda, and to define the issues symbolically".

* Third, *Cultivation of Perceptions*: the extent to which media exposure shapes audience perceptions over time is known as cultivation. Television is a common experience, especially in places like the United States, to the point where it can be described as a "homogenising agent" (S. W. Littlejohn).[*[full citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#What_information_to_include "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] However, instead of being merely a result of the TV, the effect is often based on socioeconomic factors. Having a prolonged exposure to TV or movie violence might affect a viewer to the extent where they actively think community violence is a problem, or alternatively find it justifiable. The resulting belief is likely to be different depending on where people live. Mass media [normalizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalization_(sociology) "Normalization (sociology)") violence can contribute to a [culture of violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_violence_theory "Culture of violence theory") and [rape culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rape_culture "Rape culture").

Since the 1950s, when cinema, radio and TV began to be the primary or only source of information for most of the population, these media became the central instruments of mass control. When a country reaches a high level of industrialisation, the country itself "belongs to the person who controls communications", according to [Umberto Eco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umberto_Eco "Umberto Eco").

Mass media play a significant role in shaping public perceptions on a variety of important issues, both through the information that is dispensed through them and through the interpretations they place upon this information. They also play a large role in shaping modern culture, by selecting and portraying a particular set of beliefs, values and traditions as reality. By portraying a certain interpretation of reality, they shape reality to be more in line with that interpretation. Mass media also play a crucial role in the spread of [civil unrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_unrest "Civil unrest") activities such as anti-government demonstrations, riots and [general strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_strike "General strike"). The use of radio and television receivers has made the unrest influence among cities not only by the geographic location of cities, but also by proximity within the mass media distribution networks.[*[needs copy edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Basic_copyediting "Wikipedia:Basic copyediting")*]

Media artist [Joey Skaggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joey_Skaggs "Joey Skaggs") has demonstrated the ease with which mass media can be manipulated using fabricated press releases, staged events, and fictitious experts. His long-running series of media hoaxes reveal how news outlets can be drawn to sensational narratives, often publishing stories with minimal fact-checking. Skaggs' work has been cited as a critique of journalistic practices and a case study in the vulnerabilities of modern media systems.

*Limited-effects theory* theorizes that because people usually choose what media to interact with based on what they already believe, media exerts a negligible influence. *Class-dominant theory* argues that the media reflects and projects the view of a minority elite, which controls it. *Culturalist theory* combines the other two theories and claims people interact with media to create their own meanings out of the images and messages they receive.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Ethical issues and criticism

Lack of local or specific topic focus is a common criticism of mass media. A mass [news media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_media "News media") outlet often chooses to cover national and international news to cater to and be relevant for a wide demographic. As such, it can skip over many interesting or important local stories because they do not interest the majority of their viewers.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The term "mass" suggests that the recipients of media products constitute a vast sea of passive, undifferentiated individuals. This is an image associated with some earlier critiques of "mass culture" and [mass society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_society "Mass society"), which generally assumed that the development of mass communication has had a largely negative impact on modern social life, creating a homogeneous culture which entertains individuals without challenging them. However, interactive digital media have also been seen to challenge the read-only paradigm of earlier broadcast media.

Contemporary research demonstrates an increasing level of [concentration of media ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_of_media_ownership "Concentration of media ownership"), with many media industries already highly concentrated and dominated by a small number of firms.

### Criticism

When the study of mass media began, the media was compiled of only mass media, which is a very different media system than the social media empire of the 21st century.[*[incomprehensible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Cleanup "Wikipedia:Cleanup")*] As such, some critiques claim mass media no longer exists, or at least that it does not exist in its previous form. Theorist Lance Bennett says that excluding a few major events in recent history, it is uncommon for a group big enough to be labeled a "mass" to be watching the same news via the same medium of mass production. Bennett argues that today it is more common for a group of people to be receiving different news stories, from completely different sources; thus, mass media has been re-invented.

Due to the widespread use of [social media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media "Social media") and the rapid development of [information technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology "Information technology"), the media landscape has undergone significant changes. As a result, the Hallin and Mancini media model, based on traditional indicators, no longer fully aligns with today’s media ecosystem.[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] While television continues to target a more mature audience and uphold professional journalism standards, [digital journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_journalism "Digital journalism") and social media tend to adapt those standards to align more closely with audience preferences.

Social media is a large contributor to the change from mass media to a new paradigm – the line between mass communication and [interpersonal communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_communication "Interpersonal communication") is blurred. This original form of mass media put filters on what the general public would be exposed to in regards to "news" – something that is harder to do in a society of social media.

#### Discrimination and bias

Mass media sources, through framing and [agenda-setting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda-setting_theory "Agenda-setting theory"), can [affect the impact of a story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_influence "Media influence"), by highlighting particular facts and information. This can correlate with how individuals perceive certain groups of people, as the media coverage a person receives can be limited and may not reflect the full situation. Stories are often covered to reflect a particular perspective, sometimes to target a specific demographic. Mass media, as well as [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda "Propaganda"), can reinforce or introduce [stereotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype "Stereotype") to the general public.

One example is how mass media has played a large role in the way white Americans perceive [African Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American "African American"). Historical media focus on African Americans in the contexts of crime, drug use, gang violence and other forms of anti-social behavior has resulted in a distorted and harmful public perception of African Americans. In his article "Mass Media and Racism", Stephen Balkaran states: "The media has played a key role in perpetuating the effects of this historical oppression and in contributing to African Americans' continuing status as second-class citizens." Negative portrayal of men in media has been claimed to contribute to harmful public perception of men.

[Media bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_bias "Media bias") on a particular topic can be assessed in comparison to the median voter.[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]

## Forms

### Broadcast media

Broadcast media includes radio and [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio "Radio") and [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television "Television") programs. Television includes [cable television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_television "Cable television"), which may require a [cable converter box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_converter_box "Cable converter box"), and generally includes [subscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subscription "Subscription")-based channels and [pay-per-view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pay-per-view "Pay-per-view") services. [Digital radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_radio "Digital radio") and [digital television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_television "Digital television") may also transmit [multiplexed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplexing "Multiplexing") programming, with several channels [compressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression") into one [ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DAB_ensemble "DAB ensemble"). [Broadcast regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_law "Broadcast law"), [programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_programming "Broadcast programming"), and [terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_broadcasting_terms "Glossary of broadcasting terms") have emerged as independent fields of inquiry. When broadcasting is done via the Internet, the term [webcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webcast "Webcast") is often used. In 2004, a new phenomenon occurred when a number of technologies combined to produce [podcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast "Podcast").

### Film

The term 'film' encompasses motion pictures as individual projects, as well as the field in general. The name comes from the [photographic film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_film "Photographic film") (also called [film stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_stock "Film stock")), historically the primary [medium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_medium "Recording medium") for recording and displaying motion pictures. Many other terms for film exist, such as *motion pictures* (or just *pictures* and "picture"), *the silver screen*, *photoplays*, *the cinema*, *picture shows*, *flicks* and, most commonly, *movies*.

Films are produced by [recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_recording "Video recording") people and objects with [cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera "Camera"), or by creating them using [animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animation "Animation") techniques or [special effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_effect "Special effect"). Films comprise a series of individual frames, but when these images are shown in rapid succession, an illusion of motion is created. Flickering between frames is not seen because of an effect known as [persistence of vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistence_of_vision "Persistence of vision"), whereby the eye retains a visual image for a fraction of a second after the source has been removed. Also of relevance is what causes the perception of motion: a psychological effect identified as [beta movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_movement "Beta movement").

### Video games

A video game is a computer-controlled game in which a video display, such as a monitor or television set, is the primary feedback device. There must also be some sort of [input device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input_device "Input device"), usually in the form of [button/joystick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Button_(control) "Button (control)") combinations, a keyboard and mouse combination, a [controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_controller "Game controller"), or a player's motion. It will have a set of rules that are engaged in to reach a goal or outcome.

### Audio recording and reproduction

[Sound recording and reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_recording_and_reproduction "Sound recording and reproduction") is the [electrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric "Electric") or mechanical re-creation or amplification of [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound "Sound"), often as [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music "Music"). This involves the use of [audio equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_equipment "Audio equipment") such as microphones, recording devices and loudspeakers. From early beginnings, with the invention of the [phonograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph "Phonograph") using purely mechanical techniques, the field has advanced with the invention of electrical recording, the mass production of the [78 record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/78_record "78 record"), the magnetic [wire recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire_recorder "Wire recorder") followed by the [tape recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_recorder "Tape recorder"), and the vinyl [LP record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph_record "Phonograph record"). The invention of the [compact cassette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_cassette "Compact cassette") in the 1960s, followed by Sony's [Walkman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkman "Walkman"), gave a major boost to the mass distribution of music recordings, and the invention of [digital recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_recording "Digital recording") and the [compact disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_disc "Compact disc") in 1983 brought massive improvements in ruggedness and quality.

### Internet media

The [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet "Internet") is a more interactive medium of mass media, and can be briefly described as "a network of networks". Specifically, it is the worldwide, publicly accessible network of interconnected [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network") that transmit [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_(computing) "Data (computing)") by [packet switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_switching "Packet switching") using the standard [Internet Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Protocol "Internet Protocol") (IP). It consists of millions of smaller domestic, academic, business and governmental networks, which together carry various [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information") and services, such as [email](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email "Email"), [online chat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_chat "Online chat"), [file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_file "Computer file") transfer, and the interlinked [web pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_page "Web page") and other documents of the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web").

The Internet is the system of interconnected *computer networks*, linked by [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper "Copper") wires, [fibre-optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre-optic "Fibre-optic") cables, and [Wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_communication "Wireless communication") connections, while the Web is the content of the internet linked by [hyperlinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperlink "Hyperlink") and [URLs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/URL "URL"). The [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web") is accessible through the Internet, along with many other services including e-mail, [file sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_sharing "File sharing") and others described below.

Toward the end of the 20th century, the advent of the World Wide Web marked the first era in which most individuals could have a means of exposure on a scale comparable to that of mass media. Forms of internet media include [blogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blog "Blog"), [microblogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microblogging "Microblogging"), [RSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSS "RSS") feeds, and [podcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast "Podcast").

### Mobile

[Mobile phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_phone "Mobile phone") were introduced in [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan") in 1979 but became a mass medium only in 1998 when the first downloadable ringing tones were introduced in Finland. Soon most forms of media content were introduced on mobile phones, [tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer "Tablet computer") and other portable devices. Similar to the internet, mobile is also an [interactive media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_media "Interactive media").

### Print media

#### Magazine

A [magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magazine "Magazine") is a [periodical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodical_literature "Periodical literature") [publication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication "Publication") containing a variety of articles, generally financed by [advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising "Advertising") or purchase by readers. Magazines are typically published [weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Week "Week"), [biweekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biweekly "Biweekly"), [monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Month "Month"), [bimonthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimonthly "Bimonthly") or [quarterly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarterly "Quarterly"), with a [date on the cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodical_cover_date "Periodical cover date") that is in advance of the date it is actually published. They are often printed in colour on [coated paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coated_paper "Coated paper"), and are bound with a [soft cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookbinding "Bookbinding").

#### Newspaper

A [newspaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper "Newspaper") is a [publication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication "Publication") containing news, information, and advertising, usually printed on low-cost paper called [newsprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsprint "Newsprint"). It may be general or special interest, and is usually published serially, most often daily or weekly. The dominant function of newspapers is to inform the public of significant events. Newspapers originated after the invention and spread of the [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press "Printing press") by [Johann Gutenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Gutenberg "Johannes Gutenberg") around 1450, with the first newspaper being the German-language *[Relation aller Fürnemmen und gedenckwürdigen Historien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_aller_F%C3%BCrnemmen_und_gedenckw%C3%BCrdigen_Historien "Relation aller Fürnemmen und gedenckwürdigen Historien")*, first published in 1605. The increasing prevalence of internet-based news media has, while challenging newspapers as an alternative source of information and opinion, has also provided a new platform for mass media organisations to reach new audiences. As such, in the twenty-first century, newspaper circulation has fallen in almost all regions.

### Outdoor media

Outdoor media is a form of mass media which comprises billboards, signs, placards, flying billboards, blimps, skywriting, and [augmented reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_reality "Augmented reality") advertising. Many commercial advertisers use this form of mass media when advertising in sports stadiums.

## Professions

### Journalism

[Journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism "Journalism") is the discipline of collecting, analyzing, verifying and presenting [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information") regarding [current events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News "News"), [trends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fads_and_trends "Fads and trends"), issues and people.

### Public relations

[Public relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations "Public relations") is management of communication between an organisation and its key publics to build, manage and sustain its positive image.

### Publishing

[Publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publishing "Publishing") is the industry concerned with the production of [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature "Literature") or [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information") – the activity of making information available for public view. In some cases, authors may be their own publishers. Traditionally, the term refers to the distribution of printed works such as [books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book "Book") and [newspapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper "Newspaper"). With the advent of digital information systems and the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet "Internet"), the scope of publishing has expanded to include [websites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Website "Website"), [blogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blog "Blog") and the like.

#### Software publishing

A [software publisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_publisher "Software publisher") is a [publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publishing "Publishing") [company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_(law) "Company (law)") in the [software industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_industry "Software industry") between the [developer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_developer "Software developer") and the [distributor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_(business) "Distribution (business)"). In some companies, two or all three of these roles may be combined (and indeed, may reside in a single person, especially in the case of [shareware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareware "Shareware")).

### Internet celebrity

An [internet celebrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_celebrity "Internet celebrity") is anyone who gained fame on the Internet.
