# Mesopotamia

**Mesopotamia** is a [historical region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_region "Historical region") of [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia "West Asia") situated within the [Tigris–Euphrates river system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris%E2%80%93Euphrates_river_system "Tigris–Euphrates river system"), in the northern part of the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent"). It corresponds roughly to the territory of modern [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq"). Just beyond it lies southwestern [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), where the region transitions into the [Iranian plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_plateau "Iranian plateau"), marking the shift from the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world "Arab world") to Iran.

Mesopotamia is the site of the earliest developments of the [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution") from around 10,000 BC. It has been identified as having "inspired some of the most important developments in human history, including the invention of the [wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel "Wheel"), the planting of the first [cereal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal "Cereal") [crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop "Crop"), the development of [cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive "Cursive") script, [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), and [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture")." It is recognised as the cradle of some of the world's earliest civilizations.

The [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") and [Akkadians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadians "Akkadians"), each originating from different areas, dominated Mesopotamia from the beginning of [recorded history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recorded_history "Recorded history") (c. 3100 BC) to the [fall of Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Babylon "Fall of Babylon") in 539 BC. The rise of empires, beginning with [Sargon of Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad "Sargon of Akkad") around 2350 BC, characterized the subsequent 2,000 years of Mesopotamian history, marked by the succession of kingdoms and empires such as the [Akkadian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire "Akkadian Empire"). The early second millennium BC saw the polarization of Mesopotamian society into [Assyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria") in the north and [Babylonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia "Babylonia") in the south. From 900 to 612 BC, the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire "Neo-Assyrian Empire") asserted control over much of the ancient Near East. Subsequently, the Babylonians, who had long been overshadowed by Assyria, [seized power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire "Neo-Babylonian Empire"), dominating the region for a century as the final independent Mesopotamian realm until the modern era. In 539 BC, Mesopotamia was conquered by the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") under [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great "Cyrus the Great"). The area was next conquered by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") in 332 BC. After his death, it was fought over by the various [Diadochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi "Diadochi") (successors of Alexander), of whom the [Seleucids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire "Seleucid Empire") emerged victorious.

Around 150 BC, Mesopotamia was under the control of the [Parthian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire"). It became a battleground between the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") and Parthians, with western parts of the region coming under ephemeral Roman control. In 226 AD, the eastern regions of Mesopotamia fell to the [Sassanid Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanid_Persians "Sassanid Persians") under [Ardashir I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardashir_I "Ardashir I"). The division of the region between the Roman Empire and the Sassanid Empire lasted until the 7th century [Muslim conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia "Muslim conquest of Persia") of the [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire") and the [Muslim conquest of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Levant "Muslim conquest of the Levant") from the Byzantines. A number of primarily neo-Assyrian and Christian native Mesopotamian states existed between the 1st century BC and 3rd century AD, including [Adiabene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabene "Adiabene"), [Osroene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osroene "Osroene"), and [Hatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hatra "Kingdom of Hatra").

## Present

In the broader sense, the historical region of Mesopotamia also includes parts of present-day [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran") (southwest), [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey") (southeast), [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria") (northeast), and [Kuwait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwait "Kuwait"). The [Marsh Iraqis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh_Arabs "Marsh Arabs") are genetically and culturally the closest modern people to ancient [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") and are the direct descendants of Ancient Sumerians. Modern [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians "Assyrians") remain direct descendants of the native Mesopotamians.

## Etymology

The regional toponym *Mesopotamia* ([/ˌmɛsəpəˈteɪmiə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): Μεσοποταμία '[land] between rivers'; [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language"): بِلَاد ٱلرَّافِدَيْن *Bilād ar-Rāfidayn* or بَيْن ٱلنَّهْرَيْن *Bayn an-Nahrayn*; [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"): میان‌رودان *miyân rudân*; [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language"): ܒܝܬ ܢܗܪ̈ܝܢ *[Beth Nahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beth_Nahrain "Beth Nahrain")* "(land) between the (two) rivers") comes from the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") root words μέσος (*mesos*, 'middle') and ποταμός (*potamos*, 'river') and translates to '(land) between rivers'. It is used throughout the Greek [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint "Septuagint") (c. 250 BC) to translate the Hebrew and Aramaic equivalent *Naharaim*. An even earlier Greek usage of the name *Mesopotamia* is evident from *[The Anabasis of Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anabasis_of_Alexander "The Anabasis of Alexander")*, which was written in the late 2nd century AD but specifically refers to sources from the time of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great"). In the *Anabasis*, Mesopotamia was used to designate the land east of the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates") in north [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria").

The [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_language "Akkadian language") term *biritum/birit narim* corresponded to a similar geographical concept. Later, the term *Mesopotamia* was more generally applied to all the lands between the Euphrates and the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris"), thereby incorporating not only parts of Syria but also almost all of [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq") and southeastern [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey"). The neighbouring [steppes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steppe "Steppe") to the west of the Euphrates and the western part of the [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains "Zagros Mountains") are also often included under the wider term *Mesopotamia*.

A further distinction is usually made between *Northern* or *[Upper Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Mesopotamia "Upper Mesopotamia")* and *Southern* or *[Lower Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Mesopotamia_(region) "Lower Mesopotamia (region)")*. Upper Mesopotamia is the area between the Euphrates and the Tigris from their sources down to [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad"). Lower Mesopotamia is the area from Baghdad to the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf") and includes Kuwait and parts of western Iran.

Following [the Islamic conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_conquest_of_Mesopotamia "Islamic conquest of Mesopotamia"), the historical region of Mesopotamia came to be known as [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology_of_Iraq "Etymology of Iraq"), and the names of its constituent regions consequently changed as well. [Upper Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Mesopotamia "Upper Mesopotamia") came to be known as *al-Jazīra* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"): الجزيرة), an Arabic term meaning “island,” referring to a land surrounded by water. Jazīra constituted the upper part of the land of Iraq. The Muslim historian and geographer [Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y%C4%81q%C5%ABt_al-%E1%B8%A4amaw%C4%AB "Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī") attested:

> Iraq comprises Basra, Wāsit, Kūfa, Baghdad, al-Anbār, Hīt, and al-Jazīra.

— Muʿjam al-Buldān

[Lower Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Mesopotamia "Lower Mesopotamia") came to be known as *al-Sawād* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"): السواد) an Arabic term meaning “black land” or “arable land”. Sawād constituted the lower part of the land of Iraq. [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad") constituted the geographical boundary between Jazīra and Sawād, the two regions that together comprised *al-ʿIrāq* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"): العراق). Baghdad was itself regarded as the geographical center of the [land of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Iraq "Arab Iraq"), as attested by the Muslim historian and geographer [al-Yaʿqūbī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ya%CA%BFq%C5%ABb%C4%AB "Al-Yaʿqūbī"):

> I have begun with al-ʿIrāq because it lies at the center of the world […] and I have mentioned Baghdād because it lies at the center of al-ʿIrāq and is the great city.

— Kitāb al-Buldān

During the [Gunpowder Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder_empires "Gunpowder empires"), it was referred to by several names, the most prominent being [Iraq al-ʿArab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Iraq "Arab Iraq") and [Ḥiṭṭa-i ʿIrāqiyye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Iraq "Ottoman Iraq"). In modern academic usage, the term *Mesopotamia* often also has a chronological connotation. It is usually used to designate the area until the [Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests"), with names like *Syria*, *Jazira*, and *Iraq* being used to describe the region after that date. It has been argued[*[by whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")*] that these later euphemisms[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] are [Eurocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism "Eurocentrism") terms attributed to the region in the midst of various 19th-century Western encroachments.

## Geography

Mesopotamia encompasses the land between the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates") and [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris") rivers, both of which have their headwaters in the neighboring [Armenian highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_highlands "Armenian highlands"). Both rivers are fed by numerous tributaries, and the entire river system drains a vast mountainous region. Overland routes in Mesopotamia usually follow the Euphrates because the banks of the Tigris are frequently steep and difficult. The climate of the region is semi-arid with a vast desert expanse in the north which gives way to a 15,000-square-kilometre (5,800 sq mi) region of marshes, lagoons, mudflats, and reed banks in the south. In the extreme south, the Euphrates and the Tigris unite and empty into the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf "Persian Gulf").

The [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aridity "Aridity") environment ranges from the northern areas of rain-fed agriculture to the south where irrigation of agriculture is essential. This irrigation is aided by a high water table and by melting snows from the high peaks of the northern [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains "Zagros Mountains") and from the Armenian Highlands, the source of the Tigris and Euphrates Rivers that give the region its name. The usefulness of irrigation depends upon the ability to mobilize sufficient labor for the construction and maintenance of canals, and this, from the earliest period, has assisted the development of urban settlements and centralized systems of political authority.

Agriculture throughout the region has been supplemented by nomadic pastoralism, where tent-dwelling nomads herded sheep and goats (and later camels) from the river pastures in the dry summer months, out into seasonal grazing lands on the desert fringe in the wet winter season. The area is generally lacking in building stone, precious metals, and timber, and so historically has relied upon long-distance trade of agricultural products to secure these items from outlying areas. In the marshlands to the south of the area, a complex water-borne fishing culture has existed since prehistoric times and has added to the cultural mix.

Periodic breakdowns in the cultural system have occurred for a number of reasons. The demands for labor have from time to time led to population increases that push the limits of the ecological [carrying capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrying_capacity "Carrying capacity"); should a period of climatic instability ensue, central government can collapse and populations can decline. Alternatively, military vulnerability to invasion from marginal hill tribes or nomadic pastoralists has led to periods of trade collapse and neglect of irrigation systems. Equally, centripetal tendencies amongst city-states have meant that central authority over the whole region, when imposed, has tended to be ephemeral, and localism has fragmented power into tribal or smaller regional units. These trends have continued to the present day in Iraq.

## History

The prehistory of the [Ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East "Ancient Near East") begins in the [Lower Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Paleolithic "Lower Paleolithic") period. Therein, writing emerged with a pictographic script, [Proto-cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform "Proto-cuneiform"), in the Uruk IV period (c. late 4th millennium BC). The documented record of actual historical events—and the ancient history of lower Mesopotamia—commenced in the early-third millennium BC with cuneiform records of early dynastic kings. This entire history ends with either the arrival of the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") in the late 6th century BC or with the Muslim conquest and the establishment of the [Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate") in the late 7th century AD, from which point the region came to be known as [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Iraq "History of Iraq"). In the long span of this period, Mesopotamia housed some of the world's most ancient highly developed, and socially complex states.

The region was home to one of the [four riverine civilizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_valley_civilization "River valley civilization") where [writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing "Writing") was invented, along with the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile") valley in [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"), the [Indus Valley civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilization "Indus Valley civilization") in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"), and the [Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River "Yellow River") in [Ancient China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_China "Ancient China"). Mesopotamia housed historically important cities such as [Uruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk "Uruk"), [Nippur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippur "Nippur"), [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh "Nineveh"), [Assur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assur "Assur") and [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon"), as well as major territorial states such as the city of [Eridu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eridu "Eridu"), the Akkadian kingdoms, the [Third Dynasty of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Dynasty_of_Ur "Third Dynasty of Ur"), and the various [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria") empires. Some of the important historical Mesopotamian leaders were [Ur-Nammu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur-Nammu "Ur-Nammu") (king of Ur), [Sargon of Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad "Sargon of Akkad") (who established the Akkadian Empire), [Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi "Hammurabi") (who established the Old Babylonian state), [Ashur-uballit I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashur-uballit_I "Ashur-uballit I") and [Tiglath-Pileser I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiglath-Pileser_I "Tiglath-Pileser I") (who established the Assyrian Empire).

Scientists analysed [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") from the 8,000-year-old remains of early farmers found at an ancient graveyard in [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany"). They compared the genetic signatures to those of modern populations and found similarities with the DNA of people living in today's [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey") and [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq").

## Language and writing

The earliest language written in Mesopotamia was [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_language "Sumerian language"), an [agglutinative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agglutinative_language "Agglutinative language") [language isolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_isolate "Language isolate"). Along with Sumerian, [Semitic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages "Semitic languages") were also spoken in early Mesopotamia. [Subartuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subartu "Subartu"), a language of the Zagros possibly related to the [Hurro-Urartuan language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurro-Urartian_languages "Hurro-Urartian languages"), is attested in personal names, rivers and mountains and in various crafts. [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_language "Akkadian language") came to be the dominant language during the [Akkadian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire "Akkadian Empire") and the [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria") empires, but Sumerian was retained for administrative, religious, literary and scientific purposes.

Different varieties of Akkadian were used until the end of the [Neo-Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire "Neo-Babylonian Empire") period. [Old Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Aramaic_language "Old Aramaic language"), which had already become common in Mesopotamia, then became the official provincial administration language of first the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire "Neo-Assyrian Empire"), and then the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire"): the official [lect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(linguistics) "Variety (linguistics)") is called [Imperial Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Aramaic "Imperial Aramaic"). Akkadian fell into disuse, but both it and Sumerian were still used in temples for some centuries. The last Akkadian texts date from the late 1st century AD.

Early in Mesopotamia's history, around the mid-4th millennium BC, [cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform "Cuneiform") was invented for the Sumerian language. Cuneiform literally means "wedge-shaped", due to the triangular tip of the stylus used for impressing signs on wet clay. The standardized form of each cuneiform sign appears to have been developed from [pictograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictogram "Pictogram"). The earliest texts, 7 archaic tablets, come from the [É](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89_(temple) "É (temple)"), a temple dedicated to the goddess Inanna at Uruk, from a building labeled as Temple C by its excavators.

The early [logographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logogram "Logogram") system of cuneiform script took many years to master. Thus, only a limited number of individuals were hired as [scribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribe "Scribe") to be trained in its use. It was not until the widespread use of a [syllabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllabary "Syllabary") script was adopted under Sargon's rule that significant portions of the Mesopotamian population became literate. Massive archives of texts were recovered from the archaeological contexts of Old Babylonian scribal schools, through which literacy was disseminated.

Akkadian gradually replaced Sumerian as the spoken language of Mesopotamia somewhere around the turn of the 3rd and the 2nd millennium BC. The exact dating is a matter of debate. Sumerian continued to be used as a sacred, ceremonial, literary, and scientific language in Mesopotamia until the 1st century AD.

### Literature

[Libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library "Library") were extant in towns and temples during the Babylonian Empire. An old Sumerian proverb averred that "he who would excel in the school of the scribes must rise with the dawn." Women as well as men learned to read and write, and for the [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages "Semitic languages") Babylonians, this involved knowledge of the extinct Sumerian language, and a complicated and extensive syllabary.

A considerable amount of [Babylonian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_literature "Akkadian literature") was translated from Sumerian originals, and the language of religion and law long continued to be the old agglutinative language of Sumer. Vocabularies, grammars, and interlinear translations were compiled for the use of students, as well as commentaries on the older texts and explanations of obscure words and phrases. The characters of the syllabary were all arranged and named, and elaborate lists were drawn up.

Many Babylonian literary works are still studied today. One of the most famous of these was the [Epic of Gilgamesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh "Epic of Gilgamesh"), in twelve books, translated from the original Sumerian by a certain [Sîn-lēqi-unninni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni "Sîn-lēqi-unninni"), and arranged upon an astronomical principle. Each division contains the story of a single adventure in the career of [Gilgamesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh "Gilgamesh"). The whole story is a composite product, although it is probable that some of the stories are artificially attached to the central figure.

## Science and technology

### Mathematics

Mesopotamian mathematics and science was based on a [sexagesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal "Sexagesimal") (base 60) [numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_system "Numeral system"). This is the source of the 60-minute hour, the 24-hour day, and the 360-[degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_(angle) "Degree (angle)") circle. The [Sumerian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_calendar "Sumerian calendar") was lunisolar, with three seven-day weeks of a lunar month. This form of mathematics was instrumental in early [map-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cartography "History of cartography"). The Babylonians also had theorems on how to measure the area of shapes and solids. They measured the circumference of a circle as three times the diameter and the area as one-twelfth the square of the circumference, which would be correct if π were fixed at 3.

The volume of a cylinder was taken as the product of the area of the base and the height; however, the volume of the [frustum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frustum "Frustum") of a cone or a [square pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_pyramid "Square pyramid") was incorrectly taken as the product of the height and half the sum of the bases. Also, there was a recent discovery in which a tablet used π as 25/8 (3.125 instead of 3.14159~). The Babylonians are also known for the Babylonian mile, which was a measure of distance equal to about seven modern miles (11 km). This measurement for distances eventually was converted to a time-mile used for measuring the travel of the Sun, therefore, representing time.

One of the most famous tablets is the [Plimpton 322 tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plimpton_322 "Plimpton 322"), created around 1900–1600 BC, which gives a table of [Pythagorean triples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triples "Pythagorean triples") and represents some of the most advanced mathematics prior to Greek mathematics.

### Astronomy

From [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") times, temple priesthoods had attempted to associate current events with certain positions of the planets and stars. This continued to Assyrian times, when [Limmu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limmu "Limmu") lists were created as a year by year association of events with planetary positions, which, when they have survived to the present day, allow accurate associations of relative with absolute dating for establishing the history of Mesopotamia.

The Babylonian astronomers were very adept at mathematics and could predict [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse_cycle "Eclipse cycle") and [solstices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice#Solstice_determination "Solstice"). Scholars thought that everything had some purpose in astronomy. Most of these related to religion and omens. Mesopotamian astronomers worked out a 12-month calendar based on the cycles of the moon. They divided the year into two seasons: summer and winter. The origins of astronomy as well as astrology date from this time.

During the 8th and 7th centuries BC, Babylonian astronomers developed a new approach to astronomy. They began studying philosophy dealing with the ideal nature of the early [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") and began employing an internal logic within their predictive planetary systems. This was an important contribution to astronomy and the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science") and some scholars have thus referred to this new approach as the first scientific revolution. This new approach to astronomy was adopted and further developed in Greek and Hellenistic astronomy.

In the [Seleucid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid "Seleucid") and [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire") eras, the astronomical reports were thoroughly scientific. How much earlier their advanced knowledge and methods were developed is uncertain. The Babylonian development of methods for predicting the motions of the planets is considered to be a major episode in the [history of astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_astronomy "Assyrian astronomy").

The only Greek-Babylonian astronomer known to have supported a [heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentrism "Heliocentrism") model of planetary motion was [Seleucus of Seleucia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_of_Seleucia "Seleucus of Seleucia") (b. 190 BC). Seleucus is known from the writings of [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch "Plutarch"). He supported Aristarchus of Samos' heliocentric theory where the [Earth rotated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_rotation "Earth's rotation") around its own axis which in turn revolved around the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun"). According to Plutarch, Seleucus even proved the heliocentric system, but it is not known what arguments he used, except that he correctly theorized on tides as a result of the Moon's attraction.

Babylonian astronomy served as the basis for much of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_astronomy "Ancient Greek astronomy"), [classical Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy "Indian astronomy"), [Sasanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire"), [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire"), [Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria"), [medieval Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Astronomy in the medieval Islamic world"), [Central Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"), and [Western European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe "Western Europe") astronomy.

### Medicine

The oldest Babylonian texts on [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine") date back to the [Old Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Babylonian_dynasty "First Babylonian dynasty") period in the first half of the [2nd millennium BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_millennium_BC "2nd millennium BC"). The most extensive Babylonian medical text, however, is the *Diagnostic Handbook* written by the *ummânū*, or chief scholar, [Esagil-kin-apli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esagil-kin-apli "Esagil-kin-apli") of [Borsippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsippa "Borsippa"), during the reign of the Babylonian king [Adad-apla-iddina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adad-apla-iddina "Adad-apla-iddina") (1069–1046 BC).

Along with contemporary [Egyptian medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_medicine "Ancient Egyptian medicine"), the Babylonians introduced the concepts of [diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diagnosis "Medical diagnosis"), [prognosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prognosis "Prognosis"), [physical examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_examination "Physical examination"), [enemas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enema "Enema"), and [prescriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_prescription "Medical prescription"). The *Diagnostic Handbook* introduced the methods of [therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapy "Therapy") and [aetiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aetiology "Aetiology") and the use of [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism "Empiricism"), [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), and [rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality "Rationality") in diagnosis, prognosis and therapy. The text contains a list of medical [symptoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symptom "Symptom") and often detailed empirical [observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation "Observation") along with logical rules used in combining observed symptoms on the body of a [patient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patient "Patient") with its diagnosis and prognosis.

The symptoms and diseases of a patient were treated through therapeutic means such as [bandages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandage "Bandage"), [creams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cream_(pharmaceutical) "Cream (pharmaceutical)") and [pills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pill_(pharmacy) "Pill (pharmacy)"). If a patient could not be cured physically, the Babylonian physicians often relied on [exorcism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism "Exorcism") to cleanse the patient from any [curses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse "Curse"). Esagil-kin-apli's *Diagnostic Handbook* was based on a logical set of [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom "Axiom") and assumptions, including the modern view that through the examination and [inspection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspection "Inspection") of the symptoms of a patient, it is possible to determine the patient's [disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease "Disease"), its aetiology, its future development, and the chances of the patient's recovery.

Esagil-kin-apli discovered a variety of [illnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illness "Illness") and diseases and described their symptoms in his *Diagnostic Handbook*. These include the symptoms for many varieties of [epilepsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epilepsy "Epilepsy") and related [ailments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ailment "Ailment") along with their diagnosis and prognosis. Some treatments used were likely based on the known characteristics of the ingredients used. The others were based on the symbolic qualities.

### Technology

Mesopotamian people invented many technologies including metal and copper-working, glass and lamp making, textile weaving, [flood control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_control "Flood control"), water storage, and irrigation. They were also one of the first [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") societies in the world. They developed from copper, bronze, and gold on to iron. Palaces were decorated with hundreds of kilograms of these very expensive metals. Also, copper, bronze, and iron were used for armor as well as for different weapons such as swords, daggers, spears, and [maces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mace_(bludgeon) "Mace (bludgeon)").

According to a recent hypothesis, the [Archimedes' screw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_screw "Archimedes' screw") may have been used by Sennacherib, King of Assyria, for the water systems at the [Hanging Gardens of Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanging_Gardens_of_Babylon "Hanging Gardens of Babylon") and [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh "Nineveh") in the 7th century BC, although mainstream scholarship holds it to be a [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") invention of later times. Later, during the Parthian or Sasanian periods, the [Baghdad Battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Battery "Baghdad Battery"), which may have been the world's first battery, was created in Mesopotamia.

## Religion and philosophy

The [Ancient Mesopotamian religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Mesopotamian_religion "Ancient Mesopotamian religion") was the first recorded. Mesopotamians believed that the world was a flat disc, surrounded by a huge, holed space, and above that, [heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven "Heaven"). They believed that water was everywhere, the top, bottom and sides, and that the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") was born from this enormous sea. Mesopotamian religion was [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"). Although the [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief "Belief") described above were held in common among Mesopotamians, there were regional variations. The Sumerian word for universe is *an-ki*, which refers to the god [An](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anu "Anu") and the goddess [Ki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ki_(goddess) "Ki (goddess)"). Their son was Enlil, the air god. They believed that Enlil was the most powerful god. He was the chief god of the [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_(religion) "Pantheon (religion)").

### Philosophy

The numerous civilizations of the area influenced the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions"), especially the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible"). Its cultural values and literary influence are especially evident in the [Book of Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis "Book of Genesis").

[Giorgio Buccellati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Buccellati "Giorgio Buccellati") believes that the origins of [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy") can be traced back to early Mesopotamian [wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom "Wisdom"), which embodied certain philosophies of life, particularly [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), in the forms of [dialectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic "Dialectic"), [dialogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue "Dialogue"), [epic poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry "Epic poetry"), [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore "Folklore"), [hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymn "Hymn"), [lyrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyrics "Lyrics"), [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose") works, and [proverbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proverb "Proverb"). Babylonian [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason") and [rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality "Rationality") developed beyond [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism "Empiricism") observation.

Babylonian thought was also based on an [open-systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_system_(systems_theory) "Open system (systems theory)") [ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology "Ontology") which is compatible with [ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic "Ergodic") axioms. Logic was employed to some extent in [Babylonian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy "Babylonian astronomy") and medicine.

Babylonian thought had a considerable influence on early [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy") and [Hellenistic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophy "Hellenistic philosophy"). In particular, the Babylonian text *[Dialogue of Pessimism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue_of_Pessimism "Dialogue of Pessimism")* contains similarities to the agonistic thought of the [Sophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophist "Sophist"), the [Heraclitean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitean "Heraclitean") doctrine of [dialectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic "Dialectic"), and the dialogs of [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato"), as well as a precursor to the [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method "Socratic method"). The [Ionian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionians "Ionians") philosopher [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales "Thales") was influenced by Babylonian cosmological ideas.

## Culture

### Festivals

Ancient Mesopotamians had ceremonies each month. The theme of the rituals and festivals for each month was determined by at least six important factors:

1. The [Lunar phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase "Lunar phase") (a waxing moon meant abundance and growth, while a waning moon was associated with decline, conservation, and festivals of the Underworld)
2. The phase of the annual agricultural cycle
3. [Equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinoxes "Equinoxes") and [solstices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstices "Solstices")
4. The local mythos and its divine Patrons
5. The success of the reigning Monarch
6. The [Akitu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akitu "Akitu"), or [New Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Year "New Year") Festival (first full moon after spring equinox)
7. Commemoration of specific historical events (founding, military victories, temple holidays, etc.)

### Music

Some songs were written for the gods but many were written to describe important events. Although music and songs amused [kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch "Monarch"), they were also enjoyed by ordinary people who liked to sing and dance in their homes or in the [marketplaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketplace "Marketplace").

Songs were sung to children who passed them on to their children. Thus songs were passed on through many [generations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation "Generation") as an oral tradition until writing was more universal. These songs provided a means of passing on through the [centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century "Century") highly important information about historical events.

### Games

[Hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunting "Hunting") was popular among Assyrian kings. [Boxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxing "Boxing") and [wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrestling "Wrestling") feature frequently in art, and some form of [polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo "Polo") was probably popular, with men sitting on the shoulders of other men rather than on horses.

They also played a board game similar to [senet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senet "Senet") and [backgammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backgammon "Backgammon"), now known as the "[Royal Game of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Game_of_Ur "Royal Game of Ur")".

### Family life

Mesopotamia, as shown by successive law codes, those of [Urukagina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urukagina "Urukagina"), [Lipit Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipit_Ishtar "Lipit Ishtar") and [Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi "Hammurabi"), across its history became more and more a [patriarchal society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchal_society "Patriarchal society"), one in which the men were far more powerful than the women. For example, during the earliest Sumerian period, the *"en"*, or high priest of male gods was originally a woman, that of female goddesses. [Thorkild Jacobsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorkild_Jacobsen "Thorkild Jacobsen"), as well as others, have suggested that early Mesopotamian society was ruled by a "council of elders" in which men and women were equally represented, but that over time, as the status of women fell, that of men increased.

As for schooling, only royal offspring and sons of the rich and professionals, such as scribes, physicians, temple administrators, went to school. Most boys were taught their father's trade or were apprenticed out to learn a trade. Girls had to stay home with their mothers to learn [housekeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housekeeping "Housekeeping") and [cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking "Cooking"), and to look after the younger children. Some children would help with crushing grain or cleaning birds. Unusually for that time in history, women in Mesopotamia had [rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rights "Rights"). They could own [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property "Property") and, if they had good reason, get a [divorce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divorce "Divorce").

### Burials

Hundreds of [graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_(burial) "Grave (burial)") have been excavated in parts of Mesopotamia, revealing information about Mesopotamian [burial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burial "Burial") habits. In the city of [Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur "Ur"), most people were buried in family graves under their houses, along with some possessions. A few have been found wrapped in mats and [carpets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpets "Carpets"). Deceased children were put in big "jars" which were placed in the family [chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel "Chapel"). Other remains have been found buried in common city [graveyards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graveyard "Graveyard"). 17 graves have been found with very precious objects in them. It is assumed that these were royal graves. Rich people of various periods have been discovered to have sought burial in Bahrein, identified with Sumerian Dilmun.

## Economy

Sumerian temples functioned as banks and developed the first large-scale [system of loans and credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy "Economy"). The Babylonians developed the earliest system of commercial [banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking "Banking"). It was comparable in some ways to modern [post-Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Keynesian_economics "Post-Keynesian economics"), but with a more "anything goes" approach.

### Agriculture

Irrigated agriculture spread southwards from the Zagros foothills with the Samara and Hadji Muhammed culture, from about 5,000 BC.

In the early period down to [Ur III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur_III "Ur III") temples owned up to one third of the available land, declining over time as royal and other private holdings increased in frequency. The word [*Ensi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ens%C3%AD "Ensí") was used to describe the official who organized the work of all facets of temple agriculture. [Villeins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villein "Villein") are known to have worked most frequently within agriculture, especially in the grounds of temples or palaces.

The geography of southern Mesopotamia is such that agriculture is possible only with irrigation and with good drainage, a fact which had a profound effect on the evolution of early Mesopotamian civilization. The need for irrigation led the Sumerians, and later the Akkadians, to build their cities along the Tigris and Euphrates and the branches of these rivers. Major cities, such as Ur and Uruk, took root on tributaries of the Euphrates, while others, notably Lagash, were built on branches of the Tigris. The rivers provided the further benefits of fish, used both for food and fertilizer, reeds, and clay, for building materials. With irrigation, the [food supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_supply "Food supply") in Mesopotamia was comparable to that of the Canadian prairies.

The Tigris and Euphrates River valleys form the northeastern portion of the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent"), which also included the Jordan River valley and that of the Nile. Although land nearer to the rivers was fertile and good for [crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crops "Crops"), portions of land farther from the water were dry and largely uninhabitable. Thus the development of [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation "Irrigation") became very important for [settlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler "Settler") of Mesopotamia. Other Mesopotamian [innovations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innovation "Innovation") include the control of water by [dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam "Dam") and the use of aqueducts. Early settlers of fertile land in Mesopotamia used wooden [plows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plow "Plow") to soften the [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil "Soil") before planting crops such as [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley "Barley"), [onions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onion "Onion"), [grapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grape "Grape"), [turnips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnip "Turnip"), and [apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple "Apple").

Mesopotamian settlers were among the first people to make [beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer "Beer") and [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine "Wine"). As a result of the skills needed to farm in the Mesopotamian region, farmers did not generally depend on [slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slaves "Slaves") to do the work. Although the rivers sustained life, they also destroyed it by frequent floods that ravaged entire cities. The unpredictable Mesopotamian weather was often hard on farmers. Crops were often ruined, so backup sources of food such as cows and lambs were kept. Over time the southernmost parts of Sumerian Mesopotamia suffered from increased salinity of the soils, leading to a slow urban decline and a centring of power in Akkad, further north.

### Trade

Mesopotamian trade with the [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation "Indus Valley Civilisation") flourished as early as the third millennium BC. Cylinder seals found throughout ANE is evidence of trade between Mesopotamian cities. Starting in the 4th millennium BC, Mesopotamian civilizations also traded with [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") (see [Egypt–Mesopotamia relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt%E2%80%93Mesopotamia_relations "Egypt–Mesopotamia relations")).

For much of history, Mesopotamia served as a [trade nexus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_route "Trade route") – east-west between Central Asia and the Mediterranean world
(part of the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road")), as well as north–south between the Eastern Europe and [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad "Baghdad") ([Volga trade route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volga_trade_route "Volga trade route")). [Vasco da Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasco_da_Gama "Vasco da Gama")'s pioneering (1497–1499) of the [sea route between India and Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_discovery_of_the_sea_route_to_India "Portuguese discovery of the sea route to India") and the opening of the [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal "Suez Canal") in 1869 impacted on this nexus.

## Genetics

Genetic studies on the modern day people of [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq") are limited and generally restricted to analysis of classical keys due to the country's modern political instability, although lately, there have been several published studies displaying a genealogical connection between all Iraqis and the neighboring countries, across religious, ethnic and linguistic barriers. Studies indicate that the different ethno-religious groups of Iraq (Mesopotamia) share significant similarities in genetics and that Mesopotamian Arabs, who make up the majority of Iraqis, are more genetically similar to Iraqi Kurds than other Arab populations in the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") and [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia "Arabia").

No significant differences in Y-DNA variation were observed among Iraqi Mesopotamian Arabs, Assyrians, or Kurds. Modern genetic studies indicate that Iraqi Mesopotamian Arabs are more related to [Iraqi-Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi-Assyrians "Iraqi-Assyrians") than [Iraqi Kurds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds_in_Iraq "Kurds in Iraq").

Dogan et al. (2017) states that contemporary [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_people "Syriac people") and [Yazidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazidis "Yazidis") from northern Iraq might "have stronger continuity with the original genetic stock of the Mesopotamian people, which possibly provided the basis for the ethnogenesis of various subsequent Near Eastern populations." Among northern Iraqi Assyrians, [J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_J_(Y-DNA) "Haplogroup J (Y-DNA)") and [R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R_(Y-DNA) "Haplogroup R (Y-DNA)") subclades were observed at 36% and 41% respectively, where [R1a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R1a "Haplogroup R1a"), [R1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R1b "Haplogroup R1b"), [J1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_J-M267 "Haplogroup J-M267") and [J2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_J-M172 "Haplogroup J-M172") sub-clades accounted for 11%, 30%, 12% and 24%. For Yazidis, R haplogroup subclades dominate, where R1a and R1b account for 9% and 21%, respectively. The high prevalence of R and J macrohaplogroups is attributed to pre-[Last Glacial Maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum "Last Glacial Maximum") events in the Near East.

Many historians and anthropologists provide strong circumstantial evidence to presuppose that Iraq's [Marsh Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh_Arabs "Marsh Arabs") share very strong links to the ancient Sumerians.

While other studies indicate that the Iraqi-Assyrian population was found to be significantly related to other Iraqis, especially Mesopotamian Arabs, likely due to the assimilation of indigenous Assyrians with other people groups who occupied and settled Mesopotamia after the fall of the [Neo-Babylonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire "Neo-Babylonian Empire").

## Government

The geography of Mesopotamia had a profound impact on the political development of the region. Among the rivers and streams, the Sumerian people built the first cities, along with irrigation canals which were separated by vast stretches of open desert or swamp where nomadic tribes roamed. Communication among the isolated cities was difficult and, at times, dangerous. Thus, each Sumerian city became a [city-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state "City-state"), independent of the others and protective of its independence.

At times, one city would try to conquer and unify the region, but such efforts were resisted and failed for centuries. As a result, the political history of Sumer is one of almost constant warfare. Eventually Sumer was unified by [Eannatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eannatum "Eannatum"). The unification was tenuous and failed to last, as the Akkadians conquered Sumer in 2331 BC only a generation later. The Akkadian Empire was the first successful empire to last beyond a generation and see a peaceful succession of kings. The empire was relatively short-lived, as the Babylonians conquered them within only a few generations.

### Kings

The Mesopotamians believed their kings and queens were descended from the city [gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity"), but, unlike the [ancient Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptians "Ancient Egyptians"), they never believed their kings were real gods. Most kings named themselves "king of the universe" or "great king". Another common name was "[shepherd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd "Shepherd")", as kings had to look after their people.

### Power

When Assyria grew into an empire, it was divided into smaller parts, called [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces "Provinces"). Each of these were named after their main cities, like [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh "Nineveh"), [Samaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaria "Samaria"), [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus "Damascus"), and [Arpad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arpad_(Syria) "Arpad (Syria)"). They all had their own governor, who had to make sure everyone paid their taxes. Governors had to call up soldiers to war and supply workers when a temple was built. He was responsible for enforcing the laws. In this way, it was easier to keep control of a large empire.

Although Babylon was quite a small [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_state "Sovereign state") in Sumer, it grew tremendously throughout the time of [Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi "Hammurabi")'s rule. He was known as "the lawmaker" and created the [Code of Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi "Code of Hammurabi"). Soon [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon") became one of the main cities in Mesopotamia. It was later called Babylonia, which meant "the gateway of the gods". It became one of history's greatest centers of learning.

### Warfare

With the end of the [Uruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk "Uruk") phase, walled cities grew. Many isolated [Ubaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubaid_period "Ubaid period") villages were abandoned, indicating a rise in communal violence. An early king [Lugalbanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugalbanda "Lugalbanda") was supposed to have built the white walls around the city. As [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-states "City-states") began to grow, their spheres of influence overlapped, creating arguments between other city-states, especially over land and canals. These arguments were recorded in tablets several hundreds of years before any major war—the first recording of a war occurred around 3200 BC, but was not common until about 2500 BC.

An [Early Dynastic II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Dynastic_II "Early Dynastic II") king (Ensi) of Uruk in Sumer, Gilgamesh (c. 2600 BC), was commended for military exploits against [Humbaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humbaba "Humbaba") guardian of the Cedar Mountain, and was later celebrated in many later poems and songs in which he was claimed to be two-thirds god and only one-third human. The later [Stele of the Vultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stele_of_the_Vultures "Stele of the Vultures") at the end of the [Early Dynastic III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Dynastic_III "Early Dynastic III") period (2600–2350 BC), commemorating the victory of [Eannatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eannatum "Eannatum") of [Lagash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagash "Lagash") over the neighbouring rival city of [Umma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umma "Umma"), is the oldest monument in the world that celebrates a massacre.

From this point forwards, warfare was incorporated into the Mesopotamian political system. At times, a neutral city acted as an arbitrator for two rival cities. This helped to form unions between cities, leading to regional states. When empires were created, they went to war more with foreign countries. King Sargon, for example, conquered all the cities of Sumer, some cities in Mari, and then went to war with cities in modern-day Syria. Many Assyrian and Babylonian palace walls were decorated with pictures of the successful fights and the enemy either desperately escaping or hiding amongst reeds.

The Neo-Babylonian kings used deportation as a means of control, like their predecessors, the Assyrians. For the Neo-Babylonian kings, war was a means to obtain tribute, plunder, sought after materials such as various metals and quality wood, and prisoners of war which could be put to work as slaves in the temples which they built. The Assyrians displaced populations throughout their vast empire. This practice under the Babylonian kings was more limited, only being used to establish new populations in Babylonia itself. Though royal inscriptions from the Neo-Babylonian period don't speak of acts of destruction and deportation in the same boastful way royal inscriptions from the Neo-Assyrian period do, this does not prove that the practice ceased, or that the Babylonians were less brutal than the Assyrians, since there is evidence that the city [Ascalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascalon "Ascalon") was destroyed by [Nebuchadnezzar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II "Nebuchadnezzar II") in 604 BC.

### Laws

City-states of Mesopotamia created the first law codes, drawn from legal precedence and decisions made by kings. The codes of [Urukagina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urukagina "Urukagina") and [Lipit-Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipit-Ishtar "Lipit-Ishtar") (the [Code of Lipit-Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Lipit-Ishtar "Code of Lipit-Ishtar")) have been found. The most renowned of these was that of [Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammurabi "Hammurabi"), as mentioned above, who was posthumously famous for his set of laws, the [Code of Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi "Code of Hammurabi"), created c. 1780 BC, which is one of the earliest sets of laws found and one of the best preserved examples of this type of document from ancient Mesopotamia. He codified over 200 laws for Mesopotamia. Examination of the laws show a progressive weakening of the rights of women, and increasing severity in the treatment of slaves.

## Art

The art of Mesopotamia rivalled [that of Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Ancient_Egypt "Art of Ancient Egypt") as the most grand, sophisticated and elaborate in western [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia "Eurasia"), from the 4th millennium BC until the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia "Persia") Achaemenid Empire conquered the region in the 6th century BC. The main emphasis was on very durable, forms of sculpture in stone and clay. Little painting has survived, but it seems that painting was mainly used for geometrical and plant-based decorative schemes. Most sculptures were also painted.

The [Protoliterate period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoliterate_period "Protoliterate period"), dominated by [Uruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk "Uruk"), saw the production of sophisticated works like the [Warka Vase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warka_Vase "Warka Vase") and [cylinder seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_seal "Cylinder seal"). The [Guennol Lioness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guennol_Lioness "Guennol Lioness") is an outstanding small [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone "Limestone") figure from [Elam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam "Elam") of about 3000–2800 BC, part man and part lion. A little later there are a number of figures of large-eyed priests and worshippers, mostly in alabaster and up to a foot high, who attended temple [cult images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_image "Cult image") of the deity, but very few of these have survived. Sculptures from the [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer "Sumer") and [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_Empire "Akkadian Empire") period generally had large, staring eyes, and long beards on the men. Many masterpieces have been found at the Royal Cemetery at [Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur "Ur") (c. 2650 BC), including the two figures of a *[Ram in a Thicket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram_in_a_Thicket "Ram in a Thicket")*, the *[Copper Bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_Bull "Copper Bull")* and a bull's head on one of the [Lyres of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyres_of_Ur "Lyres of Ur").

From the many subsequent periods before the ascendency of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, Mesopotamian art survives in a number of forms: cylinder seals, relatively small figures in the round, and reliefs of various sizes, including cheap plaques of moulded pottery for the home, some religious and some apparently not. The [Burney Relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burney_Relief "Burney Relief") is an unusual elaborate and relatively large (20 x 15 inches) [terracotta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terracotta "Terracotta") plaque of a naked winged goddess with the feet of a bird of prey, and attendant owls and lions. It comes from the 18th or 19th century BC, and may also be moulded.

Stone [stelae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stela "Stela"), [votive offerings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Votive_offering "Votive offering"), or ones probably commemorating victories and showing feasts, are found from temples, which unlike more official ones lack inscriptions that would explain them. The fragmentary [Stele of the Vultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stele_of_the_Vultures "Stele of the Vultures") is an early example of the inscribed type. The Assyrian [Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Obelisk_of_Shalmaneser_III "Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III") a large and solid late one.

The conquest of the whole of Mesopotamia and much surrounding territory by the Assyrians created a larger and wealthier state than the region had known before, and very grandiose art in palaces and public places, no doubt partly intended to match the splendour of the art of the neighbouring Egyptian empire. The Assyrians developed a style of extremely large schemes of very finely detailed narrative low reliefs in stone for palaces, with scenes of war or hunting. The [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum "British Museum") has an outstanding collection. They produced very little sculpture in the round, except for colossal guardian figures, often the human-headed [lamassu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamassu "Lamassu"), which are sculpted in high relief on two sides of a rectangular block, with the heads effectively in the round, and five legs, so that both views seem complete. Even before dominating the region, they continued the cylinder seal tradition, with designs which are often exceptionally energetic and refined.

## Architecture

The study of ancient Mesopotamian architecture is based on available [archaeological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological "Archaeological") evidence, pictorial representation of buildings, and texts on building practices. Scholarly literature usually concentrates on temples, palaces, city walls and gates, and other monumental buildings, but occasionally one finds works on residential architecture as well. Archaeological surface surveys also allowed for the study of urban form in early Mesopotamian cities.

Brick is the dominant material, as the material was freely available locally, whereas building stone had to be brought a considerable distance to most cities. The [ziggurat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziggurat "Ziggurat") is the most distinctive form, and cities often had large gateways, of which the [Ishtar Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishtar_Gate "Ishtar Gate") from Neo-Babylonian Babylon, decorated with beasts in polychrome brick, is the most famous, now largely in the [Pergamon Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pergamon_Museum "Pergamon Museum") in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin").

The most notable architectural remains from early Mesopotamia are the temple complexes at [Uruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk "Uruk") from the 4th millennium BC, temples and palaces from the [Early Dynastic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Dynastic_Period_(Mesopotamia) "Early Dynastic Period (Mesopotamia)") sites in the [Diyala River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diyala_River "Diyala River") valley such as Khafajah and Tell Asmar, the [Third Dynasty of Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Dynasty_of_Ur "Third Dynasty of Ur") remains at [Nippur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippur "Nippur") (Sanctuary of [Enlil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlil "Enlil")) and [Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur "Ur") (Sanctuary of [Nanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin_(mythology) "Sin (mythology)")), Middle [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age") remains at Syrian-Turkish sites of [Ebla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebla "Ebla"), [Mari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari,_Syria "Mari, Syria"), [Alalakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alalakh "Alalakh"), [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo "Aleppo") and [Kultepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kultepe "Kultepe"), Late Bronze Age palaces at [Hattusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hattusa "Hattusa"), [Ugarit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugarit "Ugarit"), [Ashur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assur "Assur") and [Nuzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuzi "Nuzi").

Iron Age palaces and temples are found at the [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria "Assyria") ([Kalhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalhu "Kalhu")/Nimrud, [Khorsabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khorsabad "Khorsabad"), [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh "Nineveh")), [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia "Babylonia") ([Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon "Babylon")), [Urartian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urartian "Urartian") ([Tushpa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tushpa "Tushpa")/Van, Kalesi, Cavustepe, [Ayanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayanis "Ayanis"), [Armavir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armavir,_Armenia "Armavir, Armenia"), [Erebuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yerevan "Yerevan"), [Bastam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastam "Bastam")) and [Neo-Hittite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Hittite "Neo-Hittite") sites ([Karkamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carchemish "Carchemish"), [Tell Halaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Halaf "Tell Halaf"), [Karatepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karatepe "Karatepe")). Houses are mostly known from Old Babylonian remains at Nippur and Ur. Among the textual sources on building construction and associated rituals, are Gudea's cylinders from the late 3rd millennium, as well as the Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions from the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age "Iron Age").
