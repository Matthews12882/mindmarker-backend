# Byzantine Empire

The **Byzantine Empire**, also known as the **Eastern Roman Empire**, was the continuation of the Eastern part of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") centred on [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople") during [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity") and the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"). Having survived the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Fall of the Western Roman Empire") in the 5th century AD, it endured until the [fall of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople") to the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") in 1453. The term 'Byzantine Empire' was coined only after its demise; [its citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantines "Byzantines") used the term 'Roman Empire' and called themselves 'Romans'.

During the early centuries of the Roman Empire, the western provinces were [Latinised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_(cultural) "Romanization (cultural)"), but the eastern parts kept their [Hellenistic culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_culture "Hellenistic culture"). [Constantine I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great "Constantine the Great") (r. 324–337) legalised [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") and founded the new Eastern capital of Constantinople. [Theodosius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_I "Theodosius I") (r. 379–395) made Christianity the [state religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion "State religion") and Greek gradually replaced Latin for official use. The empire adopted a defensive strategy and, throughout its remaining history, experienced recurring cycles of decline and recovery.

The Byzantine Empire reached its greatest extent under the reign of [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I") (r. 527–565), who briefly reconquered much of Italy and the western [Mediterranean coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea"). A [plague began](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian "Plague of Justinian") around 541, and a [prolonged warfare with Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628 "Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628") placed fiscal and military strain on the empire, contributing to political and strategic challenges in the decades that followed. In the 630s and 640s the [Arab conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests") defeated Byzantine field armies in [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Syria "Roman Syria") and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt "Roman Egypt"), resulting in the permanent loss of those provinces to the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate "Rashidun Caliphate"). In 698, Africa [was lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Carthage_(698) "Battle of Carthage (698)") to the [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate "Umayyad Caliphate"), but the empire stabilised under the [Isaurian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire_under_the_Isaurian_dynasty "Byzantine Empire under the Isaurian dynasty"). It expanded once more under the [Macedonian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_dynasty "Macedonian dynasty"), experiencing [a two-century-long renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Renaissance "Macedonian Renaissance"). Thereafter, periods of civil war and [Seljuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_dynasty "Seljuk dynasty") incursion resulted in the loss of most of [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia"). The empire recovered during the [Komnenian restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komnenian_restoration "Komnenian restoration"), and Constantinople remained the largest and wealthiest city in Europe until the 13th century, when it was overtaken by Paris.

The empire was severely fragmented in 1204, following the [sack of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Constantinople "Sack of Constantinople") during the [Fourth Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Crusade "Fourth Crusade"); its former territories [were then divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partitio_terrarum_imperii_Romaniae "Partitio terrarum imperii Romaniae") into competing Greek [rump states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rump_state "Rump state") and [Latin realms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankokratia "Frankokratia"). Despite the eventual [recovery of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire_under_the_Palaiologos_dynasty "Byzantine Empire under the Palaiologos dynasty") in 1261, the reconstituted empire wielded only regional power during its final two centuries. Its remaining territories were progressively annexed by the Ottomans in [a series of wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars "Byzantine–Ottoman wars") fought in the 14th and 15th centuries. The fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453 brought the empire to an end.

## Nomenclature

The empire's inhabitants, today generally known as [Byzantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantines "Byzantines"), regarded themselves as [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_people#Survival_of_the_Roman_Empire_in_the_east "Roman people") (in Greek, Ῥωμαῖοι or *Romaioi*). Similarly, their Islamic contemporaries called their empire the "land of the Romans" (*Bilād al-Rūm*). After 800 AD, Western Europe called them "Greeks" (*Graeci*), as the Papacy and medieval German emperors regarded themselves as the true inheritors of Roman identity. The adjective "Byzantine", derived from [*Byzantion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantium "Byzantium") (*Byzantium* in Latin), the name of the Greek settlement [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople") was established on, was only used to describe the inhabitants of the city; it did not refer to the empire, called *Rhomanía* (Ῥωμανία or "Romanland") by its citizens.

Following the empire's fall, [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern "Early modern") scholars referred to it by many names, including the "Eastern Empire", the "Low Empire", the "Late Empire", the "Empire of the Greeks", "Empire of Constantinople", and "Roman Empire". The increasing use of "Byzantine" and "Byzantine Empire" may have started with [Theodore Metochites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Metochites "Theodore Metochites") or [Laonikos Chalkokondyles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laonikos_Chalkokondyles "Laonikos Chalkokondyles") whose work was widely propagated by [Hieronymus Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieronymus_Wolf "Hieronymus Wolf"). "Byzantine" was used adjectivally alongside terms such as "Empire of the Greeks" until the 19th century. It is now the primary term, used to refer to all aspects of the empire; some modern historians believe it should not be used because it was originally a prejudicial and inaccurate term.

## History

### Start date

Given the significant overlap in historiographical [periodisations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodization "Periodization") of [Late Roman history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later_Roman_Empire "Later Roman Empire"), [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity"), and Byzantine history, there is no consensus on a foundation date for the Byzantine Empire. Scholarship with links to Greece or [Eastern Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy "Eastern Orthodoxy") has customarily placed it in the early 300s. The growth of the study of "late antiquity" has led to some historians setting a start date in the seventh or eighth centuries. Others believe a "new empire" began during changes c. 300 AD. Geoffrey Greatrex believes that it is impossible to precisely date the foundation of the Byzantine Empire.

### Pre-518: Constantinian, Theodosian, and Leonid dynasties

Between the 3rd and 1st centuries BC, the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic") established [hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemony "Hegemony") over the [eastern Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Mediterranean "Eastern Mediterranean"), while [its government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome#Government "Ancient Rome") developed into the one-person rule of [an emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperors "Roman emperors"). The [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") enjoyed a period of [relative stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana "Pax Romana") until [the 3rd century AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_Third_Century "Crisis of the Third Century"), when external threats and internal crises caused it to splinter, as regional armies acclaimed their generals as "[soldier-emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barracks_emperor "Barracks emperor")". One of these, [Diocletian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocletian "Diocletian") (r. 284–305), recognised that the state was too big to be ruled by a single person. He instituted the [Tetrarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrarchy "Tetrarchy"), a system which divided the empire into eastern and western halves. The Tetrarchy quickly failed, but the division of the empire proved an enduring concept.

[Constantine I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_I "Constantine I") (r. 306–337) secured absolute power in 324. Over the next six years, he rebuilt the city of Byzantium as a new [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_city "Capital city") that he called "New Rome" (later named [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople")). The old capital [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome "Rome") was farther from the prosperous eastern provinces and in a less strategically important location; its esteem had already been somewhat lessened in the eyes of the "soldier-emperors", who ruled from the frontiers, and of the empire's population. [Having been granted citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutio_Antoniniana "Constitutio Antoniniana"), the empire's subjects considered themselves just as Roman as those in the city of Rome. Constantine continued reforms of the empire's military and civil administration and instituted the [gold solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus_(coin) "Solidus (coin)") as a stable currency. He [favoured Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great_and_Christianity "Constantine the Great and Christianity") and became an opponent of paganism. Constantine's dynasty prioritised [a lengthy conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perso-Roman_wars_of_337%E2%80%93361 "Perso-Roman wars of 337–361") against the comparably powerful [Sasanid Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire") and ended in 363 with the death of his nephew [Julian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor) "Julian (emperor)"). The reign of the short [Valentinianic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinianic_dynasty "Valentinianic dynasty"), marked by [wars against the Goths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_Wars#Fourth_century "Gothic Wars"), religious debates, and anti-corruption campaigns, ended in the East with the death of [Valens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valens "Valens") at the [Battle of Adrianople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adrianople "Battle of Adrianople") in 378.

Valens's successor, [Theodosius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_I "Theodosius I") (r. 379–395), secured peace in the east by allowing the [Goths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goths "Goths") to settle in Roman territory; he also twice intervened in the western half, defeating the usurpers [Magnus Maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Maximus "Magnus Maximus") and [Eugenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenius "Eugenius") in 388 and 394, respectively. He [actively condemned paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_pagans_under_Theodosius_I "Persecution of pagans under Theodosius I"), confirmed the primacy of [Nicene Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_Christianity "Nicene Christianity") over [Arianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianism "Arianism") in the East, and established [Christianity as the Roman state religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_as_the_Roman_state_religion "Christianity as the Roman state religion"). He was the last emperor to rule both the western and eastern halves of the empire. After his death, the West was destabilised but the East thrived due to the civilian administrators who continued to hold power. [Theodosius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_II "Theodosius II") (r. 408–450) largely left the rule of the East to officials such as [Anthemius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthemius_(praetorian_prefect) "Anthemius (praetorian prefect)"), who constructed the [Theodosian Walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosian_Walls "Theodosian Walls"). Constantinople had now entrenched itself as the empire's capital.

Aside from Constantinople's walls, Theodosius' reign was also marked by the compilation of the *[Codex Theodosianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Theodosianus "Codex Theodosianus")* and the theological dispute over [Nestorianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorianism "Nestorianism") (a doctrine later deemed [heretical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heretical "Heretical")). His reign also saw the arrival of [Attila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attila "Attila")'s [Huns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huns "Huns"), who ravaged the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans"), leading to a large [tribute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribute "Tribute") being exacted from the eastern empire. Attila switched his attention to the rapidly-deteriorating western empire, and his people fractured after his death in 453. Later, [Leo I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_I_(emperor) "Leo I (emperor)") (r. 457–474) failed in his [468 attempt to reconquer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basiliscus#Military_career "Basiliscus") the [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire"). The warlord [Odoacer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odoacer "Odoacer") deposed [Romulus Augustulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus_Augustulus "Romulus Augustulus") in 476 and after the assassination of his titular successor [Julius Nepos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Nepos "Julius Nepos") in 480, abolished the office of western emperor.

Through a combination of fortune and good political decisions, the Eastern Empire never experienced rebellious barbarian vassals or rule by barbarian warlords—the problems which ensured the downfall of the West. [Zeno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_(emperor) "Zeno (emperor)") (r. 474–491) convinced the problematic [Ostrogoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostrogoth "Ostrogoth") king [Theodoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodoric_the_Great "Theodoric the Great") to take control of Italy from Odoacer; dying when the empire was at peace, he was succeeded by [Anastasius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anastasius_I_Dicorus "Anastasius I Dicorus") (r. 491–518). His belief in [monophysitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophysitism "Monophysitism") brought occasional issues, but Anastasius was a capable administrator and instituted successful financial reforms including the abolition of the [chrysargyron tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collatio_lustralis "Collatio lustralis"). He was the first emperor since Diocletian not to face any serious problems affecting the empire during his reign.

### 518–717: Justinian and Heraclian dynasties

The reign of [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I") was a high point in east Roman history. Following his accession in 527, the legal code was rewritten as the *[Corpus Juris Civilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Juris_Civilis "Corpus Juris Civilis")*, which streamlined Roman law across the empire; he reasserted imperial control over religion and morality through purges of pagans, heretics, and other "deviants"; and having ruthlessly subdued [the 532 Nika revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nika_riots "Nika riots") he rebuilt much of Constantinople, including the [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia "Hagia Sophia"). Justinian I took advantage of the confusion, following Theoderic the Ostrogoth's death, to attempt the reconquest of Italy. The [Vandal Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandal_Kingdom "Vandal Kingdom") in North Africa [was subjugated in late 533](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandalic_War "Vandalic War") by the general [Belisarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belisarius "Belisarius"), who [then invaded Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_War_(535%E2%80%93554) "Gothic War (535–554)"); the [Ostrogothic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostrogothic_Kingdom "Ostrogothic Kingdom") mostly ended in 554.

In the 540s, Justinian began to suffer reversals on multiple fronts. Capitalising on Constantinople's preoccupation with the West, [Khosrow I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_I "Khosrow I") of the Sasanian Empire invaded Byzantine territory and sacked [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch "Antioch") in 540. [A devastating plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian "Plague of Justinian") killed a large proportion of the population and severely reduced the empire's social and financial stability. The most difficult period of the Ostrogothic war, against their king [Totila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totila "Totila"), came during this decade; while divisions among Justinian's advisors undercut the administration's response. He also did not fully heal the divisions in [Chalcedonian Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedonian_Christianity "Chalcedonian Christianity"), as the [fifth ecumenical council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Council_of_Constantinople "Second Council of Constantinople") failed to make a real difference. Justinian died in 565; his reign was more successful than any other emperor, yet he left behind an unstable empire.

[Justin II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_II "Justin II") (r. 565–578) inherited an empire stretched thin both financially and territorially. He was soon at war on many fronts. Fearing the aggressive [Avars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_Avars "Pannonian Avars"), the [Lombards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombards "Lombards") conquered much of northern Italy by 572. The [Sasanian wars restarted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591 "Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591") in the same year, and would not conclude until 591; by this time, the Avars and [Slavs had repeatedly invaded the Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_migrations_to_the_Balkans "Slavic migrations to the Balkans"), causing great instability. [Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_(emperor) "Maurice (emperor)") [campaigned extensively in the region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice%27s_Balkan_campaigns "Maurice's Balkan campaigns") during the 590s, and although he re-established Byzantine control up to the [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube_River "Danube River"), he pushed his troops too far in 602—they mutinied, proclaimed an officer named [Phocas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phocas "Phocas") as emperor, and executed Maurice. The Sasanians seized their moment and [reopened hostilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628 "Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628"); Phocas was unable to cope and soon faced [a major rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclian_revolt "Heraclian revolt") led by [Heraclius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclius "Heraclius"). Phocas lost Constantinople in 610 and was executed; this destructive civil war accelerated the empire's decline.

Under [Khosrow II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_II "Khosrow II"), the Sassanids occupied the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant") and Egypt and advanced into Asia Minor, and the Avars and Slavs raided in the Balkans. The Empire's control of Italy also weakened. After successfully repelling [a siege of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(626) "Siege of Constantinople (626)") in 626, Heraclius won a decisive victory at the [Battle of Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nineveh_(627) "Battle of Nineveh (627)"), eventually defeating the Sassanids later that year. The triumph would prove short-lived. The [Arab conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests") soon saw the [conquest of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Levant "Muslim conquest of the Levant"), [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_conquest_of_Egypt "Arab conquest of Egypt"), and [the Sassanid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia "Muslim conquest of Persia") by the newly formed Arab [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate "Rashidun Caliphate"). By Heraclius' death in 641, the empire had been severely reduced economically and territorially—the loss of the wealthy eastern provinces had deprived the empire of as much as three-quarters of its revenue.

The next century is poorly documented. [Arab raids into Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Byzantine_wars#Arab_attacks_on_Anatolia_and_sieges_of_Constantinople "Arab–Byzantine wars") started quickly, and the Empire responded by holding fortified centres and avoiding battle wherever possible. Although Anatolia was invaded annually, it avoided permanent Arab occupation. The outbreak of the [First Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Fitna "First Fitna") in 656 gave the Empire breathing space, which it used sensibly: some order was restored in the Balkans by [Constans II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constans_II "Constans II") (r. 641–668) following his administrative reorganisation which over time evolved into the "[theme system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_(Byzantine_district) "Theme (Byzantine district)")", a structure that allocated troops to defend specific provinces. [Constantine IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_IV "Constantine IV") (r. 668–685) repelled the Arab efforts to [capture Constantinople in the 670s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(674%E2%80%93678) "Siege of Constantinople (674–678)") using [Greek fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_fire "Greek fire"), but suffered a reversal against the [Bulgars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgars "Bulgars"), who soon established [an empire in the northern Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Bulgarian_Empire "First Bulgarian Empire"). Nevertheless, he had done enough to secure the empire's position, especially as the [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate "Umayyad Caliphate") was undergoing [another civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Fitna "Second Fitna").

Beginning in 695, when Constantine's son [Justinian II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_II "Justinian II") was first deposed, the empire entered an [era of political instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty_Years%27_Anarchy "Twenty Years' Anarchy") that lasted for the next 22 years. While Justinian had stabilised the situation with the divided Arabs, the threat of the reconstituted caliphate was met by [Leo III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_III_the_Isaurian "Leo III the Isaurian") when he [repelled the 717–718 siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(717%E2%80%93718) "Siege of Constantinople (717–718)"), the first serious challenge against Arab expansion.

### 718–867: Isaurian, Nikephorian, and Amorian dynasties

The emperors Leo III (r. 717–741) and [Constantine V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_V "Constantine V") (r. 741–775) were effective leaders, both in government and warfare. They withstood continued Arab attacks, as well as civil war and natural disasters. Constantine's success reestablished the state as a major regional power. Leo's reign produced the *[Ekloge ton nomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekloge_ton_nomon "Ekloge ton nomon")*, a code of law that evolved that of Justinian I. He also continued to reform the theme system in order to lead offensive campaigns against the Muslims, culminating in [a notable victory in 740](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Akroinon "Battle of Akroinon"). Constantine overcame an early civil war against his brother-in-law [Artabasdos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artabasdos "Artabasdos"), created stability against the new [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate"), [campaigned successfully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_V#Repeated_campaigns_against_the_Bulgarians "Constantine V") against the Bulgars, and continued to make administrative and military reforms. Both emperors supported [iconoclast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm "Byzantine Iconoclasm") policies opposing the veneration of [religious images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon "Icon"), which had them later vilified by biased authors, creating issues that continue to impact modern scholarship; Constantine's reign also saw the loss of [Ravenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravenna "Ravenna") to the [Lombards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_Lombards "Kingdom of the Lombards"), and the beginning of a split from the [Roman papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_States "Papal States").

In 780, Empress [Irene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_of_Athens "Irene of Athens") assumed power as regent for her son [Constantine VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_VI "Constantine VI"). Although she was a capable administrator who restored the veneration of icons, the empire was destabilised by her conflict with her son. The Bulgars and Abbasids inflicted numerous defeats on the Byzantine armies, and the papacy crowned [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne "Charlemagne") as Roman emperor in 800. In 802, the unpopular Irene was overthrown by [Nikephoros I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikephoros_I "Nikephoros I"); he reformed the empire's administration but died [in battle against the Bulgars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Pliska "Battle of Pliska") in 811. Military defeats and societal disorder, especially the resurgence of iconoclasm, characterised the next eighteen years.

Stability was somewhat restored during the reign of [Theophilos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophilos_(emperor) "Theophilos (emperor)") (r. 829–842). He capitalised on economic growth to complete construction programmes, including rebuilding the [sea walls of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walls_of_Constantinople#Sea_walls "Walls of Constantinople"), overhaul provincial governance, and wage inconclusive campaigns against the Abbasids. After his death, his empress [Theodora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodora_(wife_of_Theophilos) "Theodora (wife of Theophilos)"), ruling on behalf of her son [Michael III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_III "Michael III"), permanently restored the veneration of icons; the empire prospered under their sometimes-fraught rule. Michael was posthumously vilified by historians loyal to the dynasty of his successor [Basil I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_I "Basil I"), who had him assassinated in 867 and later benefited from successes begun under his predecessor.

### 867–1081: Macedonian and Doukas dynasties

Basil I (r. 867–886) continued Michael's policies. His armies campaigned with mixed results in Italy but [defeated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bathys_Ryax "Battle of Bathys Ryax") the [Paulicians of Tephrike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulician_principality_of_Tephrike "Paulician principality of Tephrike"). His successor [Leo VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_VI_the_Wise "Leo VI the Wise") (r. 886–912) sponsored, compiled, and issued a large body of written works. These included the *[Basilika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilika "Basilika")*, a Greek translation of Justinian I's legal corpus that incorporated Leo's new laws; the *[Tactica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactica_of_Emperor_Leo_VI_the_Wise "Tactica of Emperor Leo VI the Wise")*, a military treatise; and the *[Book of the Eparch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_the_Prefect "Book of the Prefect")*, a manual on Constantinople's trading regulations. Outside these literary, legal, and administrative projects, Leo's reign was less successful: the empire was [defeated by the Bulgarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Boulgarophygon "Battle of Boulgarophygon") and [lost Taormina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Taormina_(902) "Siege of Taormina (902)"), its last outpost on Sicily. He also provoked theological scandal by marrying four times in an attempt to father a legitimate heir.

The early reign of this heir, [Constantine VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_VII "Constantine VII"), was tumultuous, as his mother [Zoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoe_Karbonopsina "Zoe Karbonopsina"), his uncle [Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_(Byzantine_emperor) "Alexander (Byzantine emperor)"), the patriarch [Nicholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Mystikos "Nicholas Mystikos"), members of leading aristocratic families, and the powerful external pressure of [Simeon I of Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simeon_I_of_Bulgaria "Simeon I of Bulgaria") shaped the struggle for power. In 920, the admiral [Romanos I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanos_I_Lekapenos "Romanos I Lekapenos") used his fleet to secure power, crowning himself and demoting Constantine to the position of junior co-emperor. His reign, marked by [the end of the war against Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Bulgarian_war_of_913%E2%80%93927#Peace_treaty "Byzantine–Bulgarian war of 913–927") and successes in the east under the general [John Kourkouas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kourkouas "John Kourkouas"), ended in 944 when his sons deposed him; Constantine then removed them and ruled as sole emperor. Constantine's politically limited sole rule is often associated with the [Macedonian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Renaissance "Macedonian Renaissance"), but many of the works compiled at his court were also intended to legitimise and glorify the [Macedonian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_dynasty "Macedonian dynasty"). [His son and successor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanos_II "Romanos II") died young; under two soldier-emperors, [Nikephoros II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikephoros_II_Phokas "Nikephoros II Phokas") (r. 963–969) and [John I Tzimiskes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_I_Tzimiskes "John I Tzimiskes") (r. 969–976), the army claimed numerous military successes, including the [conquest of Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_conquest_of_Cilicia "Byzantine conquest of Cilicia") and [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Antioch_(968%E2%80%93969) "Siege of Antioch (968–969)"), and a [victory against Bulgaria and the Kievan Rus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sviatoslav%27s_invasion_of_Bulgaria "Sviatoslav's invasion of Bulgaria") in 971. John in particular was an effective soldier-emperor who introduced frontier military reforms and pursued successful fiscal and diplomatic policies.

After John's death, Constantine VII's grandsons [Basil II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_II "Basil II") and [Constantine VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_VIII "Constantine VIII") ruled jointly for half a century, although the latter exercised little real power. Their early reign was occupied by conflicts against two prominent generals, [Bardas Skleros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardas_Skleros "Bardas Skleros") and [Bardas Phokas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardas_Phokas_the_Younger "Bardas Phokas the Younger"), which ended in 989 after Phokas's death and Skleros's submission, and by a power struggle against the eunuch [Basileios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Lekapenos "Basil Lekapenos"), who was dismissed in 985. Basil, who never married or had children, subsequently refused to delegate much authority: he took personal command of the army, sidelined established military families, and promoted officers loyal to him. His reign witnessed [the decades-long campaign against Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_conquest_of_Bulgaria "Byzantine conquest of Bulgaria"), in which the [Battle of Kleidion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kleidion "Battle of Kleidion") in 1014 proved decisive before the final Byzantine conquest in 1018. Diplomatic efforts, critical for this success, also contributed to the annexation of Caucasian territories in the 1020s and coexistence with the new [Fatimid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate "Fatimid Caliphate"). When he died in 1025, Basil's empire stretched from the Danube and southern Italy in the west to the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates "Euphrates") in the east; his rapid expansion was not matched by comparable administrative reforms.

After Constantine VIII's death in 1028, his daughters, the empresses [Zoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoe_Porphyrogenita "Zoe Porphyrogenita") (r. 1028–1050) and [Theodora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodora_Porphyrogenita "Theodora Porphyrogenita") (r. 1042–1056), held the keys to power: four emperors ([Romanos III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanos_III_Argyros "Romanos III Argyros"), [Michael IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_IV_the_Paphlagonian "Michael IV the Paphlagonian"), [Michael V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_V_Kalaphates "Michael V Kalaphates"), and [Constantine IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_IX_Monomachos "Constantine IX Monomachos")) ruled only because of their connection to Zoe, while [Michael VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_VI_Bringas "Michael VI Bringas") (r. 1056–1057) was selected by Theodora. Political instability, budget deficits, expensive military failures, and problems connected to over-extension strained imperial finances, legitimacy, and frontier defence; the empire's strategic focus moved from maintaining hegemony to prioritising defence.

The empire soon came under sustained assault on three fronts, from the [Seljuk Turks in the east](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Seljuk_wars "Byzantine–Seljuk wars"), the [Pecheneg nomads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pechenegs "Pechenegs") in the north, and the [Normans in the west](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_southern_Italy "Norman conquest of southern Italy"). The Byzantine army struggled to confront these enemies, whose raids, settlement patterns, and military organisation made them difficult to defeat through single set-piece battles. In 1071 [Bari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bari "Bari"), the last remaining Byzantine settlement in Italy, was [captured by the Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Bari "Siege of Bari"), while the Seljuks won a decisive victory at the [Battle of Manzikert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Manzikert "Battle of Manzikert"), taking the emperor [Romanos IV Diogenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanos_IV_Diogenes "Romanos IV Diogenes") prisoner. Romanos's capture and the political struggle that followed helped trigger a decade of civil conflict, during which Turkish forces overran much of Anatolia up to the [Sea of Marmara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Marmara "Sea of Marmara").

### 1081–1204: Komnenos and Angelos dynasties

One prominent general, [Alexios I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_I_Komnenos "Alexios I Komnenos"), usurped the throne in 1081. In contrast to the prior turmoil, the three reigns of Alexios (r. 1081–1118), his son [John II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_II_Komnenos "John II Komnenos") (r. 1118–1143), and his grandson [Manuel I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_I_Komnenos "Manuel I Komnenos") (r. 1143–1180) lasted almost a century and [restored the empire's regional authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komnenian_restoration "Komnenian restoration") for the final time. Alexios immediately faced the Normans under [Robert Guiscard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Guiscard "Robert Guiscard") and [repelled them through warfare and diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Norman_wars#First_Norman_invasion_of_the_Balkans_(1081–1085) "Byzantine–Norman wars"). He then targeted the Pechenegs and [decisively defeated them in 1091](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Levounion "Battle of Levounion") with help from the [Cumans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumans "Cumans"); a separate Cuman incursion followed in 1094. Finally, looking to recover Asia Minor from the Seljuks, he approached [Pope Urban II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_II "Pope Urban II") for help c. 1095. The scale and aims of western Christendom's response exceeded Byzantine expectations: the [First Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade "First Crusade") helped restore parts of western Anatolia to Byzantine control, although Alexios and its leaders soon fell out. The rest of his reign was spent [dealing with the Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Devol "Treaty of Devol") and Seljuks, establishing a new, loyal aristocracy to ensure stability, and carrying out fiscal and ecclesiastical reforms.

Alexios' concentration of power in the hands of his [Komnenos dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komnenos_dynasty "Komnenos dynasty") meant the most serious political threats came from within the imperial family—before his coronation, John II had to overcome [his mother Irene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_Doukaina "Irene Doukaina") and [his sister Anna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Komnene "Anna Komnene"), and the primary threat during his reign was [his brother Isaac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Komnenos_(son_of_Alexios_I) "Isaac Komnenos (son of Alexios I)"). John campaigned annually and extensively—he fought the Pechenegs in 1122, the [Hungarians in the late 1120s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Hungarian_War_(1127%E2%80%931129) "Byzantine–Hungarian War (1127–1129)"), and the Seljuks throughout his reign, waging [large campaigns in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Seljuk_wars#Byzantine_counter-attack:_1118–1180 "Byzantine–Seljuk wars") in his final years—but he did not achieve large territorial gains. In 1138, John raised the imperial standard over the Crusader [Principality of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Antioch "Principality of Antioch") to intimidate the city into allying with the Byzantines, but did not attack, fearing that it would provoke western Christendom to respond.

Manuel I used the considerable financial resources left by his father in pursuit of his ambitions, and also to secure the empire's position in an increasingly multilateral geopolitical landscape. Through a combination of [diplomacy and subsidies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_diplomacy "Byzantine diplomacy"), he cultivated a network of allies and client rulers around the empire: the Turks of the [Sultanate of Rum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Rum "Sultanate of Rum"), the [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary_(1000%E2%80%931301) "Kingdom of Hungary (1000–1301)"), the [Cilician Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Kingdom_of_Cilicia "Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia"), Balkan princes, Italian and Dalmatian cities, and most importantly Antioch and the [Crusader States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_States "Crusader States"), marrying [one of their princesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_of_Antioch "Maria of Antioch") in 1161. Manuel managed the difficult passage of the [Second Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Crusade "Second Crusade") through Byzantine territories in 1147, but the campaign's failure was blamed on the Byzantines by western contemporaries. He was less successful militarily: an invasion of [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Sicily "Kingdom of Sicily") was decisively defeated by [King William I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_I_of_Sicily "William I of Sicily") in 1156, leading to tensions with [Frederick Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Barbarossa "Frederick Barbarossa"), the Holy Roman Emperor; two decades later, an invasion of Anatolia was defeated at the [Battle of Myriokephalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Myriokephalon "Battle of Myriokephalon").

Manuel's death exposed the fragility of the political system and the empire soon came under intense pressure. His son [Alexios II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_II_Komnenos "Alexios II Komnenos") was too young to rule, and his troubled regency was overthrown by his uncle [Andronikos I Komnenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronikos_I_Komnenos "Andronikos I Komnenos"); he was replaced by [Isaac II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_II_Angelos "Isaac II Angelos") in 1185. The empire lost ground on several frontiers as ambitious rulers seized their chance: Hungary and the Turks captured Byzantine territories, [an exiled Komnenian prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Komnenos_of_Cyprus "Isaac Komnenos of Cyprus") seized Cyprus; and most injuriously, [a revolt in 1185](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uprising_of_Asen_and_Peter "Uprising of Asen and Peter") caused the foundation of a [resurrected Bulgarian state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Bulgarian_Empire "Second Bulgarian Empire"). Relations with the West deteriorated further after Constantinople sought an understanding with [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin "Saladin") and obstructed the overland army of [Frederick Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Barbarossa "Frederick Barbarossa") during the [Third Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Crusade "Third Crusade"). In 1195, Isaac II was deposed by his brother [Alexios III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_III_Angelos "Alexios III Angelos"); this dynastic quarrel helped make the Fourth Crusade's intervention in Constantinople possible.

The [Fourth Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Crusade "Fourth Crusade") was originally intended to target [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_Sultanate "Ayyubid Sultanate"), but amid strategic difficulties, Isaac II's son [Alexios Angelos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_IV_Angelos "Alexios IV Angelos") convinced the crusaders to restore his father to the throne in exchange for a huge tribute. They [attacked Constantinople in 1203](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(1203) "Siege of Constantinople (1203)"), reinstating Isaac II and his son to the throne. The new rulers swiftly grew unpopular and were deposed by [Alexios V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_V_Doukas "Alexios V Doukas"), an event used by the crusaders as a pretext to [sack the city in April 1204](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Constantinople "Sack of Constantinople"), ransacking the accumulated wealth of Constantinople.

### 1204–1453: Palaiologos dynasty

Byzantine territories fragmented into competing political entities. The crusaders crowned [Baldwin I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_I,_Latin_Emperor "Baldwin I, Latin Emperor") as the ruler of a new [Latin Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Empire "Latin Empire") in Constantinople; it soon suffered [a crushing defeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adrianople_(1205) "Battle of Adrianople (1205)") against the Bulgarians in 1205. It also failed to expand west or east, where three successor states had formed: the [Empire of Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Nicaea "Empire of Nicaea") and the [Empire of Trebizond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Trebizond "Empire of Trebizond") in Asia Minor, and the [Despotate of Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Despotate_of_Epirus "Despotate of Epirus") on the Adriatic. The Venetians acquired many ports and islands, and the [Principality of Achaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Achaea "Principality of Achaea") emerged in southern Greece. Trebizond [lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Sinope "Siege of Sinope") the key port of [Sinope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinop,_Turkey "Sinop, Turkey") in 1214 and thereafter played little role away from the southeastern Black Sea. For a time, Epirus appeared well placed to reclaim Constantinople from the Latins, and its ruler [Theodore Doukas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Komnenos_Doukas "Theodore Komnenos Doukas") crowned himself emperor, but he suffered a critical defeat at the [Battle of Klokotnitsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Klokotnitsa "Battle of Klokotnitsa") in 1230, and Epirote power waned.

Nicaea, ruled by the [Laskarid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laskaris "Laskaris") and drawing on both Byzantine refugees and local populations, blocked the Latins and the Seljuks of Rum from expanding east and west respectively. [John III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_III_Doukas_Vatatzes "John III Doukas Vatatzes") (r. 1221–1254) strengthened Nicaea militarily, economically, and diplomatically. He discouraged imports and promoted local production, and he made many diplomatic treaties, especially after [Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire "Mongol Empire") armies [ravaged Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasion_of_Bulgaria_and_Serbia "Mongol invasion of Bulgaria and Serbia") and [defeated Rum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_K%C3%B6se_Da%C4%9F "Battle of Köse Dağ") between 1237 and 1243. This chaos was an opportunity for John, and he fought many successful campaigns against the states disrupted by the [Mongol invasions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions "Mongol invasions"). Soon after his death, [his grandson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_IV_Laskaris "John IV Laskaris") was usurped by [Michael VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_VIII_Palaiologos "Michael VIII Palaiologos"), founder of the [Palaiologos dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaiologos "Palaiologos"), who [recaptured Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquest_of_Constantinople "Reconquest of Constantinople") in 1261.

Michael desired to restore the empire's glory through a rebuilding programme in Constantinople, clever diplomatic alliances, and expansionist wars in Europe. He staved off the threatening [Charles I of Anjou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_Anjou "Charles I of Anjou") first by recognising papal primacy and certain Catholic doctrines at the 1274 [Second Council of Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Council_of_Lyon "Second Council of Lyon"), and then by aiding the [Sicilian Vespers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicilian_Vespers "Sicilian Vespers") against Charles in 1282. However, his religious concessions were despised by most of the populace, and were repudiated by his successor [Andronikos II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronikos_II_Palaiologos "Andronikos II Palaiologos") (r. 1282–1328). He and his grandson [Andronikos III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronikos_III_Palaiologos "Andronikos III Palaiologos") (r. 1328–1341) led several campaigns to restore imperial influence, succeeding in Epirus and Thessaly. Their policies also weakened the state, including the dismissal of the fleet in 1285, the hiring of the mercenary [Catalan Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_Company "Catalan Company"), which turned on the Byzantines in the 1300s, and their civil war between 1320 and 1328. A disastrous civil war between 1341 and 1354 caused long-term economic difficulties, while the [Ottoman Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Turks "Ottoman Turks") gradually expanded.

The diminished Byzantine state survived for another century through diplomacy and favourable external developments. The Ottomans gradually subjugated Anatolia and simultaneously expanded into Europe from 1354, taking [Philippopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippopolis_(Thrace) "Philippopolis (Thrace)") in 1363, [Adrianopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrianopolis "Adrianopolis") in 1369, and [Thessalonica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessalonica "Thessalonica") in 1387. Dynastic struggles increasingly depended on Venetian, Genoese, and Ottoman backing. After [Manuel II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_II_Palaiologos "Manuel II Palaiologos") (r. 1391–1425) refused to pay homage to Sultan [Bayezid I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayezid_I "Bayezid I") in 1394, [Constantinople was besieged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(1394%E2%80%931402) "Siege of Constantinople (1394–1402)") until the rampaging warlord [Timur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur "Timur") decisively [defeated Bayezid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ankara "Battle of Ankara") in 1402, with the city perilously close to surrender.

After Ankara, Manuel II gained nearly two decades of relative peace as [Ottoman succession struggles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Interregnum "Ottoman Interregnum") and later Anatolian campaigns occupied the sultans. In 1421, his unsuccessful backing of the claimant [Mustafa Çelebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_%C3%87elebi "Mustafa Çelebi") led to a [renewed Ottoman assault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(1422) "Siege of Constantinople (1422)"). Although [John VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_VIII_Palaiologos "John VIII Palaiologos") (r. 1425–1448) reconciled with the Catholic West at the [Council of Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Florence "Council of Florence"), his empire steadily diminished. In 1452, Sultan [Mehmed II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_II "Mehmed II") resolved to capture Constantinople, and laid siege early the following year. On 29 May 1453, [the city was captured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople"), the last emperor, [Constantine XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_XI_Palaiologos "Constantine XI Palaiologos"), died in battle, and the Byzantine Empire ended.

## Structures of the state

### Governance

Diocletian and Constantine's 4th-century reforms reorganised the empire's provinces into overarching [Dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_diocese "Roman diocese") and then into [Praetorian prefectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_prefecture "Praetorian prefecture"), separating the army from the civil administration. The central government, led by the [emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperor "Roman emperor") from the time of the earlier [Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana "Pax Romana") and into the late [Palaiologan era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire_under_the_Palaiologos_dynasty "Byzantine Empire under the Palaiologos dynasty"), typically focused on the military, foreign relations, administering the law, and collecting taxes. A person usually ascended into the position of emperor [after a ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_the_Byzantine_emperor "Coronation of the Byzantine emperor"). The [senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_senate "Byzantine senate") evolved into a ceremonial body within the imperial court.

Cities had been a collection of self-governing communities with central government and church representatives from the 5th century. However, constant warfare significantly altered this, as regular raids and ongoing conflict led to power centralising due to the empire's fight for survival. After the 7th century, the prefectures were abandoned, and in the 9th century, the provinces were divided into administrative units called [*themes* (or *themata*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_(Byzantine_district) "Theme (Byzantine district)"), governed solely by a military commander (*[strategos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategos "Strategos")*).

### Law

Theodosius II (r. 408–450) formalised [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law "Roman law") by appointing five jurists as principal authorities and compiling legislation issued since Constantine's reign into the [Codex Theodosianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Theodosianus "Codex Theodosianus"). This process culminated in the *[Corpus Juris Civilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Juris_Civilis "Corpus Juris Civilis")* under Justinian I (r. 527–565), who commissioned a complete standardisation of imperial decrees since Hadrian's time and resolved conflicting legal opinions of the jurists. The result became the definitive legal authority. This body of law covered [civil matters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system) "Civil law (legal system)") and also [public law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_law "Public law"), including imperial power and administrative organisation. After 534, Justinian issued the [Novellae (New Laws)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novellae_Constitutiones "Novellae Constitutiones") in Greek, which marked a transition from Roman to Byzantine law. Legal historian Bernard Stolte distinguishes Roman law as this because Western Europe inherited law through the Latin texts of the *Corpus Juris Civilis* only.

Zachary Chitwood argues that the *Corpus Juris Civilis* was largely inaccessible in Latin, particularly in the provinces. Following the 7th-century Arab conquests, people began questioning the development and application of law, leading to stronger ties between law and Christianity. This context influenced Leo III (r. 717–741) to develop the *[Ekloge ton nomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekloge_ton_nomon "Ekloge ton nomon")*, which placed an emphasis on humanity. The *Ekloge* inspired practical legal texts like the *Farmers' Law, Seamen's Law,* and *Soldiers' Law*, which Chitwood suggests were used daily in the provinces as companions to the *Corpus Juris Civilis*. During the Macedonian dynasty, efforts to reform law began with the publication of the *Procheiron* and the *Eisagoge*, which aimed to define the emperor's power under prevailing laws, and to replace the *Ekloge* due to its association with [iconoclasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconoclasm "Iconoclasm"). Leo VI (r. 886–912) completed a [complete codification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_(law) "Codification (law)") of Roman law in Greek through the *[Basilika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilika "Basilika")*, a work of 60 books which became the foundation of Byzantine law. In 1345, Constantine Harmenopoulos compiled the *[Hexabiblos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_Harmenopoulos "Constantine Harmenopoulos")*, a six-volume law book derived from various Byzantine legal sources.

### Christianity and the Church

Christianity, bolstered by [Constantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great "Constantine the Great")'s support, began shaping all aspects of life in the early Byzantine Empire. Despite the transition, the historian [Anthony Kaldellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Kaldellis "Anthony Kaldellis") views Christianity as "bringing no economic, social, or political changes to the state other than being more deeply integrated into it". When the Roman state in the West collapsed politically, cultural differences began to divide the Christian churches of the East and West. Internal disputes within the Eastern churches led to the migration of monastic communities to Rome, exacerbating tensions between Rome and Constantinople. These disputes, particularly in Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean, eventually split the church into three branches: [Chalcedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedonian_Christianity "Chalcedonian Christianity"), Monophysite (Coptic), and [Nestorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East "Church of the East"). The Chalcedonian group maintained dominance within the empire's territories, while the Monophysite and Nestorian branches largely fell under Muslim rule in the 7th century.

Eastern patriarchs frequently sought the Papacy's mediation in doctrinal and practical matters, but the pope's authority was not universally acknowledged, even in nearby regions like Northern Italy. By 600, the Slavic settlement of the Balkans disrupted communication between Rome and Constantinople, further widening the divide. The Arab and Lombard invasions, and the increased [Frankish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks "Franks") presence, deepened this estrangement and intensified disputes over jurisdiction and authority between the two spiritual centres. Differences in ritual and theology, such as the use of [unleavened bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azymite "Azymite") and the [Filioque clause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filioque "Filioque"), as well as divergences in ecclesiology—[plenitudo potestatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenitudo_potestatis "Plenitudo potestatis") versus the authority of [Ecumenical Councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Councils "Ecumenical Councils")—and issues of mutual respect, contributed to the separation of Western Christianity from Eastern Christianity. This separation began by 597 and culminated in 1054 during the [East–West Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism "East–West Schism").

## Warfare

### Military evolution

In the late 6th century, following Justinian I's wars, seven mobile [field armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_armies "Field armies") called *[comitatenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comitatenses "Comitatenses")*, numbering around 150,000 troops, were deployed around the empire; they remained the finest armies in Europe. They were aided by twenty-five frontier garrisons of approximately 195,000 lower-quality *[limitanei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limitanei "Limitanei")* troops. Additional troops included [subsidised allied forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foederati "Foederati") and [imperial guard units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_guard "Imperial guard") like the *[Scholae Palatinae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholae_Palatinae "Scholae Palatinae")*. Naval forces were limited: [flotillas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flotilla "Flotilla") were based at key locations, while 30,000 oarsmen were assembled to row 500, mostly requisitioned, [transports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_transport "Maritime transport") to support the [Vandalic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandalic_War "Vandalic War") in Africa in 533.

The losses suffered in the 7th-century [Arab conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests "Early Muslim conquests") led to fundamental changes. The field armies were withdrawn into the core Anatolian territories and assigned to settle in specific districts, which became known as *themata* and eventually replaced the [old provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province#Late_imperial_period "Roman province"). The thematic armies, supported by the proceeds of their districts, came to resemble a provincial [militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militia "Militia") with a small professional core, aided by foreign mercenaries and imperial regiments at Constantinople. To defend against [its new Muslim enemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Caliphate_navy "Early Caliphate navy"), the navy was similarly reorganised into several provincialised fleets. It became the dominant power in the eastern Mediterranean, with [dromons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dromon "Dromon") equipped with [Greek fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_fire "Greek fire") proving crucial on several occasions.

As the 8th-century empire stabilised, the thematic militias proved rebellious and only suitable for defensive operations. The professional *[tagmata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagma_(military) "Tagma (military)")* regiments, first introduced in the mid-700s and consisting of native Byzantine units alongside foreign forces such as the [Varangian Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varangian_Guard "Varangian Guard"), had completely replaced them by the 11th century. The mobile *tagmata*, suitable for offensive warfare, evolved new tactical and strategic structures; the late 10th-century army, perhaps the highest-quality force the empire produced, numbered approximately 140,000, up from below 100,000 in the late 700s. However, its defensive capacities were neglected, especially during the 11th-century civil wars, leading to the loss of Anatolia to the Seljuks. The navy had also been reduced, as the empire increasingly relied on potentially hostile powers such as [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice "Republic of Venice").

Post-1081 reforms re-established an effective army; the institution of [feudal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudal "Feudal")-like *[pronoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronoia "Pronoia")* grants provided revenue to individuals in exchange for soldiers. The new army heavily relied on foreign mercenaries alongside indigenous Byzantine troops, but the financial demands of a standing army proved too much for the Byzantine state, which succumbed to the [Fourth Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Crusade "Fourth Crusade") in 1204. The [army of the Palaiologan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_army_(Palaiologan_era) "Byzantine army (Palaiologan era)"), which retook Constantinople in 1261, was generally composed of a similar mix of mercenaries and indigenous troops, but it had lost all offensive capability by the late 1200s. The empire's continued survival depended on foreign armies; attempts in the 1340s to rebuild the fleet, unwisely disbanded in 1284, were forcibly halted by [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Genoa "Republic of Genoa"). No post-1204 Byzantine field army fielded more than 5,000 troops, and less than 8,000 defended [the final siege of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople "Fall of Constantinople") in 1453.

### Diplomacy

Byzantine strategy was primarily defensive, aside from the brief period of aggression between the ninth and eleventh centuries, because of the empire's habitual lack of resources. To avoid risky and expensive military campaigns, the Byzantines [engaged in extensive diplomatic efforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_diplomacy "Byzantine diplomacy"). These took various forms, including: formal embassies, client management, alliance or peace negotiations, political marriages, [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda "Propaganda") and bribery, or even [espionage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espionage "Espionage") and assassination.

Defensively oriented Byzantine diplomacy was intended to protect the *[oikoumenē](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumene "Ecumene")*, the civilised Christian world which the empire rightfully ruled. The decline of the key [*limitrophe* system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limitrophe_states "Limitrophe states"), wherein client states along the borders served as intermediaries between the empire and other large enemies, exposed the empire to attack. By the eleventh century, Byzantine diplomacy was more bilateral and balanced. Although it lost some important advantages post-1204, diplomacy, including the still-influential Orthodox church, was nevertheless a central element in the empire's lengthy survival until 1453.

## Society

### Demography

Scholars associate the Roman, Hellenic, and Christian imperial identities with the general population, but there is ongoing debate about how these and other regional identities blended together.

As many as 27 million people lived in the empire at its peak in 540, but this fell to 12 million by 800. Although plague and territorial losses to Arab Muslim invaders weakened the empire, it eventually recovered and by the near end of the [Macedonian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_dynasty "Macedonian dynasty") in 1025, the population is estimated to have been as high as 18 million. A few decades after the recapture of Constantinople in 1282, the empire's population was in the range of 3–5 million; by 1312, the number had dropped to 2 million. By the time the Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople, there were only 50,000 people in the city, one-tenth of its population in its prime.

Slavery remained legal but declined in economic importance as many former slave roles became free professions and the state increasingly promoted intermediate forms of dependency such as the coloni. From the 4th century, imperial legislation progressively limited owners' powers and restricted the enslavement of children, prisoners of war, and eventually Christians.

### Cuisine and dining

Feasting was central to the culture. By the 10th century, dining shifted from [reclining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triclinium "Triclinium") to tables with clean linen. The introduction of the [fork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fork "Fork") and [salad dressing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salad_dressing "Salad dressing") (with [oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_oil "Food oil") and [vinegar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinegar "Vinegar")) further shaped Italian and Western traditions. Classical Greco-Roman era foods were common such as the condiment *[garos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garum "Garum")* (similar to fermented [fish sauces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_sauce "Fish sauce") today) as well as the still popular [baklava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baklava "Baklava"). Fruits like [aubergine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aubergine "Aubergine") and [orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_(fruit) "Orange (fruit)"), unknown during classical times, were added to diets. Foods that have continued into the modern era include the cured meat [paston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastirma "Pastirma"), [Feta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feta "Feta") cheese, salt roe similar to the modern [boutargue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boutargue "Boutargue"), Black sea [caviar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caviar "Caviar"), [tiropita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiropita "Tiropita"), [dolmades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolma "Dolma"), and the soup *[trachanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarhana "Tarhana")*. There were famed medieval sweet wines such as the [Malvasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malvasia "Malvasia") from [Monemvasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monemvasia "Monemvasia"), the [Commandaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commandaria "Commandaria"), and the eponymous [Rumney wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumney_wine "Rumney wine") which were drunk, as were [millet beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_beer "Millet beer") (known as *boza*) and [retsina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retsina "Retsina").

### Recreation

[Chariot races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot_racing "Chariot racing") were held from the early era until 1204, becoming one of the world's longest continuous sporting events. [Mimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mime_artist "Mime artist"), the [pantomime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantomime "Pantomime") and some wild animal shows were prominent until the 6th century. Because Christian bishops and pagan philosophers did not like these activities, the state's funding for them ceased, leading to their decline and a move to private entertainment and sporting. A Persian version of polo introduced by the Crusaders called [Tzykanion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzykanisterion "Tzykanisterion") was played by the nobility and urban aristocracy in major cities during the middle and late eras, as was the sport of jousting introduced from the West. Over time, [game boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_game "Board game") like [tavli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabula_(game) "Tabula (game)") became increasingly popular.

### Language

[Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language") and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") were the primary languages of the late Roman Empire, with the former prevalent in the west and the latter in the east. Although Latin was historically important in the military, legal system, and government, its use declined in Byzantine territories from 400 AD. Greek had begun to replace it even in those functions by the time of [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I "Justinian I") (r. 527–565), who may have tried to arrest Latin's decline. Its extinction in the east was thereafter inevitable. A similar process of linguistic [Hellenization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization "Hellenization") occurred in Asia Minor, whose inhabitants had mostly abandoned their indigenous languages for Greek by early Byzantine times. Still, much of the population of the early empire would have known neither Latin nor Greek, especially in rural areas—their languages included [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language "Armenian language") in [Byzantine Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Armenia "Byzantine Armenia"), [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic "Aramaic") dialects such as [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language") in Mesopotamia and the Levant, [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_language "Coptic language") in Egypt, [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_language "Phoenician language") on the Levant coast and in [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage "Carthage"), and [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages "Berber languages") in rural North Africa.

By the mid-6th century, [Geoffrey Horrocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Horrocks_(philologist) "Geoffrey Horrocks (philologist)") estimates that Greek was the native language of about one-third of the population, while it was spoken by 80% of the aristocracy. The empire lost its linguistic diversity in the wars of the 7th and 8th centuries, becoming overwhelmingly Greek-speaking. During this troubled period, classical [Attic Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_Greek "Attic Greek")—one of the [linguistic registers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_(sociolinguistics) "Register (sociolinguistics)") the Byzantine Greeks inherited—fell out of use, while the [everyday vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular "Vernacular") registers were still used. As the empire gained some stability from the 9th century onwards, and especially after the [Komnenian restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komnenian_restoration "Komnenian restoration"), Attic Greek came back into fashion for written works. In a phenomenon called [diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia "Diglossia"), the gap between vernacular spoken Greek, which was rarely written in published works, and literary registers only spoken in formal contexts, became very wide.

During the [Palaiologan period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire_under_the_Palaiologos_dynasty "Byzantine Empire under the Palaiologos dynasty"), although classically written works remained the normal style, Western-inspired writers began to use more vernacular elements, especially for [romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_(prose_fiction) "Romance (prose fiction)") or near-contemporary histories. One example is the *[Chronicle of the Morea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronicle_of_the_Morea "Chronicle of the Morea")*, probably written by a French immigrant who was ignorant of formal Greek literature and who incorporated spoken Greek into his work. All such written vernacular was in verse form, becoming the ancestor of [modern Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek "Modern Greek") poetry, while prose remained classically written.

## Economy

The empire's geographic and maritime advantages reduced the costs of transporting goods and facilitated trade, making it a key driver of economic growth from antiquity and through the post-classical period. Infrastructure, including roads, public buildings, and the legal system, supported trade and other economic activities. Regions like Asia Minor, the Aegean islands, Egypt, the Levant, and Africa thrived as mature economic centres despite political challenges and military insecurities. From the mid-6th century onward, plagues, invasions, and wars caused populations and economies to decline, leading to the collapse of the ancient economy. Major cities like Constantinople, Antioch, [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"), and [Thessaloniki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaloniki "Thessaloniki") continued to support substantial populations exceeding 100,000, while the countryside transitioned into fortified settlements. These rural areas developed into hamlets and villages, reflecting an economic shift between historical periods towards more efficient land use.

Low population density prompted emperors to encourage migration and resettlement, stimulating agriculture and demographic growth. By the 9th century, the economy began to revive, marked by increased agricultural production and urban expansion. Advances in science, technical knowledge, and literacy gave the empire a competitive edge over its neighbours. The 11th and 12th centuries witnessed consistent and rapid population growth, marking the peak of this revival. Italian merchants, particularly the Venetians, Genoese, and Pisans, took control of international trade, thus reducing the influence of native merchants. The political system grew increasingly extractive and authoritarian, contributing to the empire's collapse in 1204.

The fall of Constantinople during the Fourth Crusade in 1204 destroyed centuries of its wealth. Large landholdings were confiscated, and the empire fragmented into smaller rump states ruled by competing factions, making governance inefficient and increasing the costs of doing business. The state gradually lost control over trade practices, price regulations, the outflow of precious metals, and possibly even the minting of coins. Italian merchants further dominated trade as the events of 1204 opened the Black Sea to Western merchants, permanently altering the empire's fortunes. Farmers and manufacturers increasingly produced goods for local use and were affected by the insecurity of constant warfare. Despite these challenges, the empire's mixed economy (characterised by state interventions, public works, and market liberalisation) remained a model of medieval economic adaptability, even as it deteriorated under external pressures.

## Arts and sciences

### Art and architecture

Subjects in [Byzantine art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_art "Byzantine art") were primarily [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_art "Christian art") and typically non-naturalistic in their representation. Emerging from both the [earliest Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christian_art_and_architecture "Early Christian art and architecture") and [Late Antique art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Antique_art "Late Antique art"), many early examples were lost amid the [Roman Persecutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians_in_the_Roman_Empire "Persecution of Christians in the Roman Empire"); the fragmented mosaics of the 3rd-century [Dura-Europos church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dura-Europos_church "Dura-Europos church") are a unique exception. Such [Byzantine mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_mosaics "Byzantine mosaics"), known for their [gold ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_ground "Gold ground") style, became a hallmark of the empire, displaying both secular and sacred themes in diverse places, including churches ([Basilica of San Vitale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_San_Vitale "Basilica of San Vitale")), the circus ([Hippodrome of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodrome_of_Constantinople "Hippodrome of Constantinople")), and the [Great Palace of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Palace_of_Constantinople "Great Palace of Constantinople"). The early 6th-century reign of Justinian I saw systemic developments: [religious art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_art "Religious art") came to dominate, and once-popular public marble and bronze [monumental sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monumental_sculpture "Monumental sculpture") fell out of favour due to [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagan "Pagan") associations. Justinian commissioned the monumental [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia "Hagia Sophia") church, and its influential elements became architectural hallmarks for the empire: the immense size, large [dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome "Dome"), innovative use of [pendentives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendentive "Pendentive") and highly decorative interior were imitated as far north as the [Cathedral of Saint Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Saint_Sophia,_Novgorod "Cathedral of Saint Sophia, Novgorod") in [Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novgorod "Novgorod") and the [Saint Sophia Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Sophia_Cathedral,_Kyiv "Saint Sophia Cathedral, Kyiv") in [Kiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiev "Kiev"). The Hagia Sophia's creators, the engineer-architects [Isidore of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Miletus "Isidore of Miletus") and [Anthemius of Tralles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthemius_of_Tralles "Anthemius of Tralles"), are uniquely esteemed; most Byzantine artists were unrecorded and typically deemed to have little importance.

Smaller-scale art flourished throughout the entire Byzantine period: costly [ivory carvings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_carving "Ivory carving")—often as diptychs ([Barberini ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barberini_ivory "Barberini ivory")) or triptychs ([Harbaville Triptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbaville_Triptych "Harbaville Triptych"))—featured imperial commemorations or religious scenes and were particularly valued, as were [metalwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalwork "Metalwork") and [enamels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_enamel "Byzantine enamel"). Other costly objects included [illuminated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_illuminated_manuscripts "Byzantine illuminated manuscripts"), which were lavishly illustrated for a wide range of texts, and [silks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_silk "Byzantine silk"), often dyed in the prized [imperial purple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_purple "Imperial purple"); both became highly popular in Western Europe. The rise of small, portable [icon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon "Icon") paintings, used for both public and private religious worship, grew increasingly controversial. During two periods of [Byzantine Iconoclasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm "Byzantine Iconoclasm") (726–843), possibly influenced by [Islamic prohibitions on religious images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aniconism_in_Islam "Aniconism in Islam"), icons were suppressed and enormous amounts of figurative religious art was destroyed. [Iconoclasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconoclast "Iconoclast") condemned their use, likening them to pagan [idolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idolatry "Idolatry") and ascribing recent [Umayyad defeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Arab_wars_(780%E2%80%931180)#Raids_under_the_last_Umayyads_and_the_rise_of_Iconoclasm "Byzantine–Arab wars (780–1180)") as [divine retribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_retribution "Divine retribution") for their use. [Iconophiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconophile "Iconophile") eventually prevailed, maintaining their essential use for [veneration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneration "Veneration"), considered distinct from [worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worship "Worship"), and found precedent in [gospel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel "Gospel") references.

[Post-iconoclast Macedonian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_art_(Byzantine) "Macedonian art (Byzantine)") (867–1056) saw a [cultural renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Renaissance "Macedonian Renaissance"), and many artworks from this period survive. Subjects and styles became standardised, particularly [cross-in-square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-in-square "Cross-in-square") churches, and already-existing frontality and [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry "Symmetry") evolved into a dominant artistic aesthetic, observable in the small [Pala d'Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pala_d%27Oro "Pala d'Oro") enamel and the large mosaics of the [Hosios Loukas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosios_Loukas "Hosios Loukas"), [Daphni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphni_Monastery "Daphni Monastery"), and [Nea Moni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nea_Moni_of_Chios "Nea Moni of Chios") monasteries. The subsequent Komnenos-Angelos periods (1081–1204) saw increased imperial patronage, alongside figurative artwork of increased emotional expression (*Dead Christ and Mourners*, c. 1164). Byzantine artistic influence spread widely to [Norman Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman%E2%80%93Arab%E2%80%93Byzantine_culture "Norman–Arab–Byzantine culture") (the *[Madrid Skylitzes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid_Skylitzes "Madrid Skylitzes")*) and Venice (the mosaics of [St Mark's Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Basilica "St Mark's Basilica")). Serbian churches flourished, as three successive [schools of architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Serbian_architecture "Medieval Serbian architecture")—[Raška](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ra%C5%A1ka_architectural_school "Raška architectural school") (1170–1282), [Byzantine Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbo-Byzantine_architecture "Serbo-Byzantine architecture") (1282–1355), and [Morava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morava_architectural_school "Morava architectural school") (1355–1489)—combined a [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture "Romanesque architecture") aesthetic with increasingly voluminous decorations and domes. As smaller [Palaeologan artworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeologan_Renaissance#Art_and_architecture "Palaeologan Renaissance") (1261–1453) gained [relic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic "Relic") status in Western Europe—many looted in the 1204 Fourth Crusade—they greatly influenced the [Italo-Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italo-Byzantine "Italo-Byzantine") style of [Cimabue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimabue "Cimabue"), [Duccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duccio "Duccio"), and later [Giotto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giotto "Giotto"); the latter is traditionally regarded by art historians as the inaugurator of [Italian Renaissance painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance_painting "Italian Renaissance painting").

### Literature

[Byzantine literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_literature "Byzantine literature") concerns all [Greek literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_literature "Greek literature") from the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"). Although the empire was [linguistically diverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_the_Roman_Empire "Languages of the Roman Empire"), the vast majority of extant texts are in [medieval Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Greek "Medieval Greek"), in two [diglossic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia "Diglossia") variants: a scholarly form based on [Attic Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_Greek "Attic Greek"), and a [vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular "Vernacular") based on [Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek "Koine Greek"). Most contemporary scholars consider all medieval Greek texts to be literature, but some offer varying constraints. The literature's early period (c. 330–650) was dominated by the competing cultures of [Hellenism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"), [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") and [Paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome "Religion in ancient Rome"). The [Greek Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers#Greek_Fathers "Church Fathers")—educated in an Ancient Greek [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric") tradition—sought to synthesise these influences. Important early writers include [John Chrysostom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chrysostom "John Chrysostom"), [Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Dionysius_the_Areopagite "Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite") and [Procopius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procopius "Procopius"), all of whom aimed to reinvent older forms to fit the empire. Theological [miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle "Miracle") stories were particularly innovative and popular; the *[Sayings of the Desert Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayings_of_the_Desert_Fathers "Sayings of the Desert Fathers")* (*Apophthegmata Patrum*) were copied in nearly every Byzantine monastery. During the [Byzantine Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Dark_Ages "Byzantine Dark Ages") (c. 650–800), production of literature mostly stopped, though some important theologians were active, such as [Maximus the Confessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximus_the_Confessor "Maximus the Confessor"), [Germanus I of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanus_I_of_Constantinople "Germanus I of Constantinople") and [John of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_of_Damascus "John of Damascus").

The subsequent cultural Macedonian Renaissance (c. 800–1000; the "Encyclopedism period") saw a renewed proliferation of literature and revived the earlier Hellenic-Christian synthesis. Works by [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer"), [Ancient Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ancient_Greek_philosophers "List of ancient Greek philosophers") and [tragedians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_tragedy "Greek tragedy") were translated, and [hagiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagiography "Hagiography") was heavily reorganised. After this early flowering of monastic literature, there was a dearth until [Symeon the New Theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symeon_the_New_Theologian "Symeon the New Theologian") in the late 10th century. A new generation (c. 1000–1250), including Symeon, [Michael Psellos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Psellos "Michael Psellos") and [Theodore Prodromos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Prodromos "Theodore Prodromos"), rejected the Encyclopedist emphasis on order, and were interested in individual-focused ideals variously concerning [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mysticism "Christian mysticism"), [authorial voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorial_voice "Authorial voice"), heroism, humour and love. This included the Hellenistic-inspired [Byzantine romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_romance "Byzantine romance") and [chivalric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chivalry "Chivalry") approaches in rhetoric, historiography and the influential epic *[Digenes Akritas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digenes_Akritas "Digenes Akritas")*. The empire's final centuries saw a renewal of hagiography and increased Western influence, leading to mass Greek to Latin translations. Authors such as [Gemistos Plethon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon "Gemistos Plethon") and [Bessarion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessarion "Bessarion") exemplified a new focus on human [vices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice "Vice") alongside the preservation of classical traditions, the latter greatly influenced the [Italian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance "Italian Renaissance").

### Music

[Byzantine music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_music "Byzantine music") is eclectically descended from early [Christian plainsong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plainsong "Plainsong"), [Jewish music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_music "Jewish music"), and a variety of [ancient music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_music "Ancient music"); its exact connections to [ancient Greek music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_system_of_ancient_Greece "Musical system of ancient Greece") remain uncertain. It included both [sacred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_music "Sacred music") and [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_music "Secular music") traditions, but the latter is little known, whereas the former remains the central music of [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox "Eastern Orthodox") liturgy into the 21st century. The empire's church music, known as Byzantine [chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chant "Chant"), was exclusively unaccompanied [monodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monody "Monody") vocal music, sung in Greek. From the 8th century, chant melodies were governed by the *[Oktōēchos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okt%C5%8D%C4%93chos "Oktōēchos")* framework, a set of eight [modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_(music) "Mode (music)")—[echos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echos "Echos") (ἦχος; lit. 'sound')—which each provide predetermined [motivic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(music) "Motif (music)") formulae for composition. These formulae were chosen for proper [text stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_stress "Text stress") and occasionally for [text painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_painting "Text painting"), then collated through [centonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centonisation "Centonisation") into [hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymn "Hymn") or [psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm "Psalm").

Byzantine chant was central to the [Byzantine Rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Rite "Byzantine Rite"); the earliest music was not [notated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_notation "Musical notation"), including early mono[strophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strophic "Strophic") short hymns like the [troparion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troparion "Troparion"). Proto-[Ekphonetic notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekphonetic_notation "Ekphonetic notation") (9th century onwards) marked simple [recitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recitation "Recitation") patterns. The [neumatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neumatic "Neumatic") Palaeo-Byzantine notation system emerged in the 10th century, and the Middle Byzantine "Round Notation" from the mid-12th century onwards is the first fully [diastematic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/diastematic "wikt:diastematic") scheme. Several major forms developed alongside [well-known composers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Byzantine_composers "List of Byzantine composers"): the long [kontakion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kontakion "Kontakion") (5th century onwards), popularised by [Romanos the Melodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanos_the_Melodist "Romanos the Melodist"); the also-extensive [kanōn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_(hymnography) "Canon (hymnography)") (late 7th century onwards), developed by [Andrew of Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_of_Crete "Andrew of Crete"); and the shorter [sticheron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sticheron "Sticheron") (at least 8th century onwards), championed by [Kassia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kassia "Kassia"). By the Palaiologan period, the dominance of strict compositional rules lessened and [John Koukouzeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Koukouzeles "John Koukouzeles") led a new school favouring a more [ornamental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornament_(music) "Ornament (music)") "kalophonic" style which deeply informed post-empire Neo-Byzantine music.

Secular music, often state-sponsored, was ubiquitous in daily life and featured in a variety of ceremonies, festivals, and theatre. Secular vocal music was rarely notated, and extant manuscripts date much later, suggesting the tradition was passed through [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition") and likely [improvised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvised "Improvised"). Prohibited for liturgical use, a wide variety of Byzantine [instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_instrument "Musical instrument") flourished in secular contexts, although no notated instrumental music survives. It is uncertain to what extent instrumentalists improvised or if they doubled vocalists monophonically or [heterophonically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterophony "Heterophony"). Among the best known instruments are the [hydraulic organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_organ "Hydraulic organ"), used for circus and imperial court events; the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece "Music of ancient Greece")-descended [aulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulos "Aulos"), a [wind instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_instrument "Wind instrument"); the [tambouras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tambouras "Tambouras"), a plucked [string instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_instrument "String instrument"); and mostly popularly, the [Byzantine lyra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_lyra "Byzantine lyra"). Prominent genres included [acclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acclamatio "Acclamatio") chants of laudation or salutation; the celebratory [Acritic songs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acritic_songs "Acritic songs"); symposia instrumental [banquets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banquet "Banquet"), based on ancient [symposiums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_(ancient_Greece) "Symposium (ancient Greece)"); and [dance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_music "Dance music").

### Science and technology

The scholars of the empire played a principal role in transmitting classical knowledge to the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_contributions_to_the_Islamic_world "Greek contributions to the Islamic world") and [Renaissance Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance "Italian Renaissance"), as well as producing commentaries that helped expand scientific knowledge. This medieval Greek scholarship was not only based on scientific treatises from antiquity but also drew from Islamic, Latin, and Hebrew works, which helped spearhead new developments as late as the 11th and 12th centuries. Although the Empire is sometimes not associated with scientific innovation or major discoveries, its scientific contributions have also been described as underestimated. Incomplete assessments of Byzantine texts and the challenges of applying modern definitions of science to pre-modern contexts are factors in these ongoing debates.

Key people passed on important traditions that underpinned this scholarship, especially in the realms of philosophy, geometry, astronomy, and grammar. For example, the Hagia Sophia architect [Isidore of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Miletus "Isidore of Miletus") (c. 530), compiled [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes")' works which [Leo the Mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_the_Mathematician "Leo the Mathematician") (c. 850) incorporated into formal courses, and is why the [Archimedes Palimpsest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes_Palimpsest "Archimedes Palimpsest") is known today. [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus") and his critiques of [Aristotelian physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_physics "Aristotelian physics"), the pharmacologist [Pedanius Dioscorides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedanius_Dioscorides "Pedanius Dioscorides"), and Ptolematic geography and astronomy had an important influence on western science, as seen with [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy")'s influence on [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus "Nicolaus Copernicus") and Philoponus on [Bonaventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonaventure "Bonaventure"), [Gersonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gersonides "Gersonides"), [Buridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Buridan "Jean Buridan"), [Oresme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_Oresme "Nicole Oresme") and [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei").

Military innovations included the [riding stirrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirrup "Stirrup") which provided stability for mounted archers and dramatically transformed the army; a specialised type of [horseshoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe "Horseshoe"); the [lateen sail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateen "Lateen"), which improved a ship's responsiveness to wind; and [Greek fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_fire "Greek fire")—an incendiary weapon capable of burning even when doused with water, first appearing around the time of the [Siege of Constantinople (674–678)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_(674%E2%80%93678) "Siege of Constantinople (674–678)"). In [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_medicine "Byzantine medicine"), the empire pioneered the concept of the hospital as an institution offering medical care and the possibility of a cure for the patients, rather than merely being a place to die.

## Legacy

### Political aftermath

After Constantinople fell, the Ottomans quickly absorbed the remaining independent territories, including [Acciaiuoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acciaioli_family "Acciaioli family") Athens in 1458, Morea in 1460, Trebizond in 1461, and [Gattilusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gattilusio "Gattilusio") [Lesbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbos "Lesbos") in 1462. They dismantled the Empire's political and secular institutions, leaving the impoverished Church to manage what would be later called the *[Rum Millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rum_Millet "Rum Millet")*, primarily as a tool for taxing its followers. As the sole sovereign Orthodox state, Russia developed the [Third Rome doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow,_third_Rome "Moscow, third Rome"), emphasising its cultural heritage as distinct from Western Europe, because the latter had inherited much of the empire's secular learning. The [Danubian Principalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danubian_Principalities "Danubian Principalities") became a haven for Orthodox Christians and [Phanariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phanariots "Phanariots") Greeks who sought to recreate a Byzantine Greek Empire. In modern Greece, members of the *Rum Millet* [increasingly identified as Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_the_Greeks#Romans_(Ῥωμαῖοι) "Names of the Greeks"), eventually leading to a [successful war of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence "Greek War of Independence") in the 19th century. The modern Greek state nearly doubled its territory through the pursuit of the [Megali Idea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megali_Idea "Megali Idea")—a vision of reclaiming the former lands of the eastern empire—achieving limited success during the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War "Crimean War") but making significant gains during the [Balkan Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_Wars "Balkan Wars").

Since the 15th century, Byzantine history has been deeply politicised, woven into nationalist, colonialist, and imperialist narratives. This politicisation appears not only in Greece but also in Bulgarian, Romanian, Serbian, Hungarian, and Turkish nationalism, as well as in former French and Russian imperialist agendas. In the English-speaking world, interpretations of Byzantine history frequently surface in political debates, alongside the growing appreciation for its legacy. The complexity of this history makes it a sensitive topic, especially regarding Greece's role in Europe's evolving sense of identity and the origin stories of many European nations.

### Cultural aftermath

The Byzantine Empire distinctively blended Roman political traditions, Greek literary heritage, and Christianity, creating the civilisational framework that laid the foundation for medieval Europe. The Empire preserved European civilisation by acting as a shield against forces from Eurasian Steppe people such as the Avars, Bulgars, Cumans, Huns, Pechenegs, and Turks.

The empire's legal codes significantly influenced the civil law traditions of continental Europe, Russia, Latin America, Ethiopia, and even the English-speaking common law countries; and possibly influenced Islamic legal traditions as well. It also preserved and transmitted classical learning and manuscripts, making important contributions to the intellectual revival which fuelled [Italian humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism "Renaissance humanism").

The Byzantine Empire played a pivotal role in shaping Christianity by supporting early Church fathers and the decisions of Church councils; developing the institution of [monasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_monasticism "Christian monasticism"); and fostering the [Orthodox tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy "Eastern Orthodoxy") which continues to define much of Eastern European identity. It was also instrumental in preserving the Greek language and is credited with developing the [Glagolitic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glagolitic_script "Glagolitic script"), which later evolved into the [Cyrillic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic_script "Cyrillic script") and [Old Church Slavonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Church_Slavonic "Old Church Slavonic"). These innovations provided the first literary language for the Slavs and formed the educational foundation for all Slavic nations.
