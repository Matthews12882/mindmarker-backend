# Fire

**Fire** is the rapid [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation "Oxidation") of a [fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel "Fuel") in the [exothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic "Exothermic") chemical process of [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion "Combustion"), releasing [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat "Heat"), [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light "Light"), and various [reaction products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_(chemistry) "Product (chemistry)").
[Flames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame "Flame"), the most visible portion of the fire, are produced in the combustion reaction when the fuel reaches its [ignition point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignition_point "Ignition point") temperature. Flames from [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon "Hydrocarbon") fuels consist primarily of carbon dioxide, water vapor, oxygen, and nitrogen. If hot enough, the gases may become [ionized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization "Ionization") to produce [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_(physics) "Plasma (physics)"). The [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color "Color") and [intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensity_(heat_transfer) "Intensity (heat transfer)") of the flame depend on the type of fuel and composition of the surrounding gases.

Fire, in its most common form, has the potential to result in [conflagration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflagration "Conflagration"), which can lead to permanent physical damage. Fire directly affects land-based ecological systems worldwide. The benefits of fire include stimulating plant growth and maintaining ecological balance. Its harms include hazards to life and property, atmospheric pollution, and water contamination. When fire removes [protective vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_erosion#Vegetative_cover "Soil erosion"), heavy [rainfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain "Rain") can cause [soil erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_erosion#Rainfall_and_surface_runoff "Soil erosion"). The burning of vegetation releases [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen") into the atmosphere, unlike other plant nutrients such as [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium "Potassium") and [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus "Phosphorus"), which remain in the [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_ash "Wood ash") and are quickly recycled into the soil. This loss of nitrogen produces a long-term reduction in the fertility of the soil. However, it can be recovered by *[nitrogen-fixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen-fixing "Nitrogen-fixing")* plants—some of them of agricultural value, such as [clover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clover "Clover"), [peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea "Pea"), and [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean "Bean"), by decomposition of animal waste and corpses, and by natural phenomena such as [lightning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightning "Lightning").

Fire is one of the four [classical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_(classical_element) "Fire (classical element)") and has been used [throughout human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_of_fire_by_early_humans "Control of fire by early humans") for purposes that now include cooking, generating heat and light, clearing land for agriculture, [signaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal "Signal"), [propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propulsion "Propulsion"), [smelting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smelting "Smelting"), [forging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forging "Forging"), [incineration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incineration "Incineration") of waste, [cremation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremation "Cremation"), [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_worship "Fire worship"), weapons, and destruction, including in [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War "War").

Various technologies and strategies have been devised to [prevent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_prevention "Fire prevention"), manage, [mitigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_protection "Fire protection"), and extinguish fires, with professional [firefighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firefighter "Firefighter") playing a leading role. For example, most home fires start from unattended [cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking "Cooking"), although cigarettes are another major cause. [Smoke detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke_detectors "Smoke detectors") and [sprinkler systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_sprinkler_system "Fire sprinkler system") greatly reduce harm from home fires.

## Etymology

The word *fire* comes from [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") *fȳr* and has cognates in many [Germanic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages "Germanic languages") and other [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages "Indo-European languages"). The [Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic "Proto-Germanic") nominative form is reconstructed as \**fōr*, descending from [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European "Proto-Indo-European") \**péh2wr*. An alternative spelling existed in [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English "Middle English"): *fier*; still preserved in *fiery*.
The word *ignite* is derived from the classical [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") *ignis* meaning fire. The Greek term for fire, *pyr*, is used in words such as [pyroclastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_(disambiguation) "Pyroclastic (disambiguation)") or [pyrotechnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrotechnic "Pyrotechnic").

## History

### Fossil record

The fossil record of fire first appears with the establishment of a land-based flora in the [Middle Ordovician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ordovician "Middle Ordovician") period, [470](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=470) million years ago. These land plants contributed large amounts of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen") to the atmosphere when they released it as a waste product. When this concentration rose above 13%, it made a [wildfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire "Wildfire") possible. Wildfire is first recorded in the [Late Silurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Silurian "Late Silurian") fossil record, [420](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=420) million years ago, by fossils of [charred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal "Charcoal") plants. Apart from a controversial gap in the [Late Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Devonian "Late Devonian"), charcoal is present ever since. The level of atmospheric oxygen is closely correlated with the amount of charcoal in the fossil record, clearly pointing to oxygen as the key factor in the prevalence of wildfire. Fire also became more abundant when grasses became the dominant component of many ecosystems, around [6 to 7](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=6–7) million years ago, providing excellent [tinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinder "Tinder") for more rapid spread of fire. This widespread emergence of wildfire may have initiated a [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") process, whereby they produced a warmer, drier climate more conducive to fire. Fire made it possible for humans to live in colder places and dark caves. It also protected humans against dangerous animals. It caused nutritional changes, and it enabled people to eat with more variety.

### Human control

The period of history characterized by the influence of human-caused fire activity on Earth has been dubbed the [pyrocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrocene "Pyrocene"). This epoch includes the burning of [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel "Fossil fuel"), especially for technological uses.

#### Early human control

The ability to control fire was a dramatic change in the habits of early humans. [Making fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Making_fire "Making fire") to generate heat and light made it possible for people to [cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking "Cooking") food, simultaneously increasing the variety and availability of nutrients and reducing disease by killing pathogenic microorganisms in the food. The heat produced would also help people stay warm in cold weather, enabling them to live in cooler climates. Fire also kept nocturnal predators at bay.

Evidence of occasional cooked food is found from [1](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=1.0) million years ago, suggesting it was used in a controlled fashion. The oldest clear evidence of deliberate firemaking dates to around 400,000 years ago at the [Hoxnian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoxnian "Hoxnian")-aged Barnfield site in England, where [pyrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrite "Pyrite") appears to have been struck against flint to create fire. Evidence becomes widespread around 50 to 100 thousand years ago, suggesting regular use from this time; resistance to [air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution "Air pollution") started to evolve in human populations at a similar point in time. The use of fire became progressively more sophisticated, as it was used to create charcoal and to control wildlife from tens of thousands of years ago.

By the [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution "Neolithic Revolution"), during the introduction of grain-based agriculture, people all over the world used fire as a tool in [landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape "Landscape") management. These fires were typically [controlled burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn "Controlled burn") or "cool fires", as opposed to uncontrolled "hot fires", which damage the soil. Hot fires destroy plants and animals and endanger communities. This is especially a problem in the forests of today where traditional burning is prevented to encourage the growth of timber crops. Cool fires are generally conducted in the spring and autumn. They clear undergrowth, burning up [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass "Biomass") that could trigger a hot fire should it get too dense. They provide a greater variety of environments, which encourages game and plant diversity. Humans make dense, impassable forests traversable.

Another human use of fire in landscape management is to clear land for agriculture. [Slash-and-burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash-and-burn "Slash-and-burn") agriculture is still common across much of tropical Africa, Asia, and South America. For small farmers, controlled fires are a convenient way to clear overgrown areas and release nutrients from standing vegetation back into the soil. However, this useful strategy is also problematic. A growing population, forest fragmentation, and a warming climate are making the Earth's surface more prone to ever-larger escaped fires. These harm ecosystems and human infrastructure, cause health problems, and send spirals of carbon and soot into the atmosphere that may encourage even more warming – and thus feed back into more fires. Globally today, as much as 5 million square kilometres – an area more than half the size of the United States – burns in a given year.

#### Later human control

Throughout much of history, cultures attempted to explain nature and the properties of matter by proposing a set of four (or five) [classical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_element "Classical element"), of which [fire was one of the components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_(classical_element) "Fire (classical element)"). As scientific understanding developed following the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), this philosophy was replaced by a set of chemical elements and their interactions. Instead, the classical elements found an equivalency in the [states of matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_matter "State of matter"): solid, liquid, gas, and plasma.

During the 17th century, a study of combustion was made by [Jan Baptist van Helmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont "Jan Baptist van Helmont") who discovered that burning charcoal released a *gas sylvestris*, or wild spirit. This was subsequently incorporated into [Phlogiston theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlogiston_theory "Phlogiston theory") by [Johann Joachim Becher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Joachim_Becher "Johann Joachim Becher") in 1667 and more formally by [Georg Ernst Stahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Ernst_Stahl "Georg Ernst Stahl") in 1697; a concept that would strongly influence alchemical thinking for nearly two centuries. It was [Antoine Lavoisier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier "Antoine Lavoisier") who demonstrated that combustion did not involve the release of a substance, but rather something was being taken up. In 1777, Lavoisier proposed a new theory of combustion based on the reaction of a material with a component of air, which he termed oxygène. By 1791, Lavoisier's chemical concepts had been widely adopted by young scientists, and the Phlogiston theory had been rejected.

Fire has been used for centuries as a method of torture and execution, as evidenced by [death by burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_by_burning "Death by burning") as well as torture devices such as the [iron boot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boot_(torture) "Boot (torture)"), which could be heated over an open fire to the agony of the wearer.

There are numerous modern applications of fire. In its broadest sense, fire is used by nearly every human being on Earth in a controlled setting every day. Users of [internal combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_combustion "Internal combustion") vehicles employ fire every time they drive. Thermal [power stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_station "Power station") provide [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity "Electricity") for a large percentage of humanity by igniting fuels such as [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal "Coal"), [oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil "Oil") or [natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas "Natural gas"), then using the resultant heat to boil water into [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam "Steam"), which then drives [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine "Turbine").

#### Use in war

The use of fire in [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_warfare "Conventional warfare") has a long [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history "Military history"). Fire was the basis of all [early thermal weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_thermal_weapons "Early thermal weapons"), including [incendiary devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incendiary_device "Incendiary device"), heated projectiles, and the use of smoke. This class of weapons was particularly evident during naval battles and [siege warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_warfare "Siege warfare"). The [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") fleet used [Greek fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_fire "Greek fire") to attack ships and men.

The invention of [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder "Gunpowder") in China led to the [fire lance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_lance "Fire lance"), a flame-thrower dating to around 1000 CE, a precursor to [projectile weapons driven by burning gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_firearm "History of the firearm"). The earliest modern [flamethrowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flamethrower "Flamethrower") were used by infantry in the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), first used by German troops against entrenched French troops near Verdun in February 1915. They were later successfully mounted on armoured vehicles in the Second World War.

Hand-thrown [incendiary bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incendiary_bombs "Incendiary bombs") improvised from glass bottles, later known as [Molotov cocktails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molotov_cocktails "Molotov cocktails"), were deployed during the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War "Spanish Civil War") in the 1930s. During that war, incendiary bombs were deployed against [Guernica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Guernica "Bombing of Guernica") by Fascist [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviazione_Legionaria "Aviazione Legionaria") and Nazi [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condor_Legion "Condor Legion") air forces that had been created specifically to support [Franco's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Franco "Francisco Franco") [Nationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francoist_Spain "Francoist Spain").

Incendiary bombs were dropped by [Axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_Powers "Axis Powers") and [Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II "Allies of World War II") during the Second World War, notably on [Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry_Blitz "Coventry Blitz"), [Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Tokyo_(10_March_1945) "Bombing of Tokyo (10 March 1945)"), [Rotterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_bombing_of_Rotterdam "German bombing of Rotterdam"), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz "The Blitz"), [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Hamburg_in_World_War_II "Bombing of Hamburg in World War II") and [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Dresden_in_World_War_II "Bombing of Dresden in World War II"). In the latter two cases, [firestorms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firestorm "Firestorm") were deliberately caused, in which a ring of fire surrounding each city was drawn inward by an [updraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_draft "Vertical draft") created by a central cluster of fires. The United States Army Air Force extensively used incendiaries against Japanese targets in the latter months of the war, devastating entire cities constructed primarily of wood and paper houses. The incendiary fluid [napalm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napalm "Napalm") was used in July 1944, towards the end of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), although its use did not gain public attention until the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War").

#### Productive use for energy

Burning [fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel "Fuel") converts chemical energy into heat energy; [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood "Wood") has been used as fuel since [prehistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory "Prehistory"). The [International Energy Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Energy_Agency "International Energy Agency") states that nearly 80% of the world's power has consistently come from [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel "Fossil fuel") such as [petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum "Petroleum"), [natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas "Natural gas"), and [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal "Coal") in the past decades. The fire in a [power station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_station "Power station") is used to heat water, creating steam that drives [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine "Turbine"). The turbines then spin an [electric generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator "Electric generator") to produce electricity. Fire is also used to provide [mechanical work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_(physics) "Work (physics)") directly by [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion "Thermal expansion"), in both [external](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_combustion_engine "External combustion engine") and [internal combustion engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_combustion_engine "Internal combustion engine").

The [unburnable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burn "Burn") solid remains of a combustible material left after a fire is called *clinker* if its [melting point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point "Melting point") is below the flame temperature, so that it fuses and then solidifies as it cools, and *ash* if its melting point is above the flame temperature.

## Physical properties

### Chemistry

Fire is a chemical process in which a [fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel "Fuel") and an [oxidizing agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizing_agent "Oxidizing agent") react, yielding [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") and [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water "Water"). This process, known as a [combustion reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion_reaction "Combustion reaction"), does not proceed directly and involves [intermediates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_intermediate "Reaction intermediate"). Although the oxidizing agent is typically [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen"), other compounds can fulfill the role. For instance, [chlorine trifluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_trifluoride "Chlorine trifluoride") is able to ignite [sand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand "Sand").

Fires start when a [flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammable "Flammable") or a combustible material, in combination with a sufficient quantity of an [oxidizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizer "Oxidizer") such as oxygen gas or another oxygen-rich compound (though non-oxygen oxidizers exist, such as chlorine), is exposed to a source of heat or ambient [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature "Temperature") above the [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point "Flash point") for the [fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel "Fuel")/oxidizer mix, and can sustain a rate of rapid oxidation that produces a [chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_reaction "Chain reaction"). This is commonly called the [fire tetrahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_triangle "Fire triangle"). Fire cannot exist without all of these elements in place and in the right proportions. For example, a flammable liquid will start burning only if the fuel and oxygen are in the right proportions. Some fuel-oxygen mixes may require a [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst "Catalyst"), a substance that is not consumed, when added, in any [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical "Chemical") reaction during combustion, but which enables the reactants to combust more readily.

Once ignited, a chain reaction occurs whereby fires can sustain their own heat through the continued release of heat energy during combustion and may propagate, provided there is a continuous supply of an oxidizer and fuel. If the oxidizer is oxygen from the surrounding air, the presence of a force of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity"), or of some similar force caused by acceleration, is necessary to produce [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection "Convection"), which removes combustion products and brings a supply of oxygen to the fire. Without gravity, a fire rapidly surrounds itself with its own combustion products and non-oxidizing gases from the air, which exclude oxygen and [extinguish](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/extinguish "wikt:extinguish") the fire. Because of this, the risk of fire in a [spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft "Spacecraft") is small when it is [coasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_maneuver "Orbital maneuver") in inertial flight. This does not apply if oxygen is supplied to the fire by some process other than thermal convection.

Fire can be [extinguished](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_protection "Fire protection") by removing any one of the elements of the fire tetrahedron. Consider a natural gas flame, such as from a stove-top burner. Any of the following can extinguish the fire:

* turning off the gas supply, which removes the fuel source;
* covering the flame completely, which smothers the flame as the combustion both uses the available oxidizer (the oxygen in the air) and displaces it from the area around the flame with CO2;
* [application of an inert gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_extinguisher#Halons,_Halon-replacement_clean_agents_and_carbon_dioxide "Fire extinguisher") such as [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide"), smothering the flame by displacing the available oxidizer;
* application of water, which removes heat from the fire faster than the fire can produce it (similarly, blowing hard on a flame will displace the heat of the currently burning gas from its fuel source, to the same end); or
* application of a retardant chemical such as [Halon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halomethane#Fire_extinguishing "Halomethane") ([largely banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_extinguisher#Halons,_Halon-replacement_clean_agents_and_carbon_dioxide "Fire extinguisher") in some countries as of 2023[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fire&action=edit)) to the flame, which retards the chemical reaction itself until the rate of combustion is too slow to maintain the chain reaction.

In contrast, fire is intensified by increasing the overall combustion rate. Methods to do this include balancing the input of fuel and oxidizer to [stoichiometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry "Stoichiometry") proportions, increasing fuel and oxidizer input in this balanced mix, increasing the ambient temperature so the fire's own heat is better able to sustain combustion, or providing a catalyst, a non-reactant medium in which the fuel and oxidizer can more readily react.

### Flame

A [diffusion flame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_flame "Diffusion flame") is a mixture of reacting gases and solids emitting visible, [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared "Infrared"), and sometimes [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet") light, the [frequency spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_spectrum "Frequency spectrum") of which depends on the [chemical composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_composition "Chemical composition") of the burning material and intermediate reaction products. During the burning of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon "Hydrocarbon"), for example wood, or the incomplete [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion "Combustion") of gas, [incandescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescent "Incandescent") solid particles called [soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot "Soot") produce the familiar red-orange glow of "fire". This light has a [continuous spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_spectrum "Continuous spectrum"). Complete combustion of gas has a dim blue color due to the emission of single-wavelength radiation from various electron transitions in the excited molecules formed in the flame.

Usually oxygen is involved, but [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") burning in [chlorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine "Chlorine") also produces a flame, producing [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride "Hydrogen chloride") (HCl). Other possible combinations producing flames, amongst many, are [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine "Fluorine") with hydrogen, and [hydrazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrazine "Hydrazine") with [dinitrogen tetroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinitrogen_tetroxide "Dinitrogen tetroxide"). Hydrogen and hydrazine/[UDMH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UDMH "UDMH") flames are similarly pale blue, while burning [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron "Boron") and its compounds, evaluated in mid-20th century as a [high energy fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zip_fuel "Zip fuel") for [jet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine "Jet engine") and [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine "Rocket engine"), emits intense green flame, leading to its informal nickname of "Green Dragon".

The glow of a flame is complex. [Black-body radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-body_radiation "Black-body radiation") is emitted from soot, gas, and fuel particles, though the soot particles are too small to behave like perfect blackbodies. There is also [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon "Photon") emission by de-excited [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom") and [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") in the gases. Much of the radiation is emitted in the visible and infrared bands. The color depends on temperature for the black-body radiation, and on chemical makeup for the [emission spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_spectra "Emission spectra").

The common distribution of a flame under normal gravity conditions depends on [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection "Convection"), as soot tends to rise to the top of a general flame, as in a [candle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle "Candle") in normal gravity conditions, making it yellow. In [microgravity or zero gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weightlessness "Weightlessness"), such as an environment in [outer space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_space "Outer space"), convection no longer occurs, and the flame becomes spherical, with a tendency to become more blue and more efficient (although it may go out if not moved steadily, as the CO2 from combustion does not disperse as readily in microgravity, and tends to smother the flame). There are several possible explanations for this difference, the most likely being that the temperature is sufficiently uniform that soot does not form and complete combustion occurs.

Experiments by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration "National Aeronautics and Space Administration") reveal that [diffusion flames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_flame "Diffusion flame") in microgravity allow more soot to be completely oxidized after they are produced than diffusion flames on Earth, because of a series of mechanisms that behave differently in micro gravity when compared to normal gravity conditions. These discoveries have potential applications in [applied science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science "Applied science") and [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_industry "Private industry"), especially concerning [fuel efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_efficiency "Fuel efficiency").

#### Typical adiabatic temperatures

The adiabatic flame temperature of a given fuel-oxidizer pair is the temperature at which the gases achieve stable combustion.

* [Oxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen")–[dicyanoacetylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicyanoacetylene "Dicyanoacetylene") 4,990 °C (9,000 °F)
* [Oxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen")–[acetylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylene "Acetylene") 3,997 °C (7,200 °F)
* [Oxyhydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyhydrogen "Oxyhydrogen") 3,473 °C (6,300 °F)
* [Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air "Air")–[acetylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylene "Acetylene") 2,500 °C (4,500 °F)
* [Blowtorch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blowtorch "Blowtorch") (air–[MAPP gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAPP_gas "MAPP gas")) 2,020 °C (3,700 °F)
* [Bunsen burner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunsen_burner "Bunsen burner") (air–[natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas "Natural gas")) 1,300 to 1,600 °C (2,400 to 2,900 °F)
* [Candle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle "Candle") (air–[paraffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraffin_wax "Paraffin wax")) 1,000 °C (1,800 °F)

## Fire science

Fire science is a branch of [physical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_science "Physical science") which includes fire behavior, dynamics, and [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion "Combustion"). Applications of fire science include [fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_protection "Fire protection"), [fire investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_investigation "Fire investigation"), and [wildfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire "Wildfire") management.

### Ecology

Every natural ecosystem on land has its own [fire regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_regime "Fire regime"), and the organisms in those ecosystems are adapted to or dependent upon that fire regime. Fire creates a mosaic of different [habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat "Habitat") patches, each at a different stage of [succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession "Ecological succession"). Different species of plants, animals, and microbes specialize in exploiting a particular stage, and by creating these different types of patches, fire allows a greater number of species to exist within a landscape. [Changes in climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") can significantly modify the risk for wildfires in a region.

### Firefighting

[Fire fighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_fighting "Fire fighting") services are provided in most developed areas to extinguish or contain uncontrolled fires. Trained [firefighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firefighter "Firefighter") use [fire apparatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_apparatus "Fire apparatus"), water supply resources such as [water mains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_main "Water main") and [fire hydrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_hydrant "Fire hydrant"), or they might use A and B class foam depending on what is feeding the fire.

The early detection of a wildfire outbreak can be performed by a [fire lookout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_lookout "Fire lookout") observing from a [tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_lookout_tower "Fire lookout tower") constructed for that purpose. The use of these towers peaked in 1938 and has been in decline since then; most fire surveillance work is now performed using [infrared sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_sensor "Infrared sensor") and aircraft. [Fire suppression aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_firefighting "Aerial firefighting") guided by a lookout can be used to help manage wildfires. These are primarily used in support of ground crews

### Management, prevention and protection systems

Controlling a fire to optimize its size, shape, and intensity is generally called *fire management*, and the more advanced forms of it, as traditionally (and sometimes still) practiced by skilled cooks, [blacksmiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacksmith "Blacksmith"), [ironmasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironmaster "Ironmaster"), and others, are highly [skilled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skill "Skill") activities. They include knowledge of which fuel to burn; how to arrange the fuel; how to stoke the fire both in early phases and in maintenance phases; how to modulate the heat, flame, and smoke as suited to the desired application; how best to bank a fire to be revived later; how to choose, design, or modify stoves, fireplaces, bakery ovens, or industrial [furnaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_furnace "Industrial furnace"); and so on. Detailed expositions of fire management are available in various books on blacksmithing, skilled [camping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camping "Camping") or [military scouting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconnaissance "Reconnaissance"), and [domestic arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_economics "Home economics").

Wildfire prevention programs around the world may employ techniques such as *wildland fire use* and *prescribed or [controlled burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn "Controlled burn")*. *Wildland fire use* refers to any fire of natural causes that is monitored but allowed to burn. *Controlled burns* are fires ignited by government agencies under less dangerous weather conditions.

Fire prevention is intended to reduce sources of ignition. Fire prevention also includes education on how to avoid causing fires. Buildings, especially schools and tall buildings, often conduct [fire drills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_drill "Fire drill") to inform and prepare citizens on how to react to a building fire. Purposely starting destructive fires constitutes [arson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arson "Arson") and is a crime in most jurisdictions.

Model [building codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_code "Building code") require [passive fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_fire_protection "Passive fire protection") and [active fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_fire_protection "Active fire protection") systems to minimize damage resulting from a fire. A common form of active fire protection is [fire sprinklers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_sprinkler "Fire sprinkler"). To maximize passive fire protection of buildings, building materials and furnishings in most developed countries are tested for [fire-resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-resistance_rating "Fire-resistance rating"), combustibility and [flammability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammability "Flammability"). [Upholstery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upholstery "Upholstery"), [carpeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpeting "Carpeting") and [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastics "Plastics") used in vehicles and vessels are also tested.

Studies have found that functioning [smoke detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke_detectors "Smoke detectors") reduce the risk of [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death "Death") in a fire by 50%, and [sprinkler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_sprinkler "Fire sprinkler") systems can reduce mortality by 100%. Fire-resistant clothing, beds, and sofas have also been shown to reduce injuries resulting from fires. In the United States, the leading cause of residential fires is [cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking "Cooking") equipment, particularly cooking left unattended. It is estimated that [cigarettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cigarettes "Cigarettes") and other smoking materials are responsible for about 28% of home fires involving the ignition of upholstered furniture, and for approximately 58% of the fatalities in such fires. Fires started by cigarettes are also more lethal and have higher mortality rates than fires that spread from open flames. The adoption of [fire-safe cigarettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-safe_cigarette "Fire-safe cigarette") has been associated with a 45% reduction in fires caused by cigarettes.

Where fire prevention and fire protection have failed to prevent damage, [fire insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_insurance "Fire insurance") can mitigate the financial impact.

## In culture

Fire has been an importance element of human culture since the [Lower Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Paleolithic "Lower Paleolithic"). Archaeological evidence demonstrates that [fire worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_worship "Fire worship") has been widely practiced since [prehistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory "Prehistory"), with dedicated structures found dating from at least the [Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic "Chalcolithic") period. The religion of [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism") is closely linked to this practice. In some societies [fire was a deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fire_deities "List of fire deities"), while others viewed it as the [manifestation of the divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophany "Theophany"). The fire in a [hearth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearth "Hearth") was perceived as symbolic of the Heavenly Fire, and thus is considered a sacred component by fire-worshipping cultures. The origin of fire became a subject of mythology. In ancient Greek culture, the Titan god [Prometheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus "Prometheus") was responsible for [stealing heavenly fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theft_of_fire "Theft of fire") and giving it to humanity.

The use of a [pyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyre "Pyre") as a [funerary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funeral "Funeral") practice dates back to at least the Ancient Roman period in the West, and to about 4,000 years ago on the Indian subcontinent. [Cremation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremation "Cremation") of corpses is a tradition long practiced in some cultures, including Hindu. After early religious resistance in some countries, in the 19th century this practice became more widespread and is now commonplace. In some nations, suicide by [self-immolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-immolation "Self-immolation") remains common.

The symbology of fire remains important to the present day. Where wood is plentiful, the [bonfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonfire "Bonfire") can be used for celebration purposes, in many cases as part of a tradition. An example is [Guy Fawkes Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Fawkes_Night "Guy Fawkes Night") in England. The [barbecue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbecue "Barbecue") is a fire-based cultural tradition in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). The fiery ignition of [fireworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fireworks "Fireworks") has become a modern tradition to celebrate the [New Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Year "New Year")'s arrival. In contrast, [book burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_burning "Book burning") has been used as a form of protest, whether for political, religious, or moral reasons. The act of "burning in [effigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effigy "Effigy")" has a similar role, as in the annual [burning of Judas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burning_of_Judas "Burning of Judas") ritual.

Humans lack an instinctual fascination with fire, yet in modern societies, adults can become drawn to it out of curiosity. In societies that rely on daily fire use, children lose interest in fire around age 7 due to regular exposure. [Arson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arson "Arson") is the act of intentionally setting fire to a property. A separate but related behavior is [pyromania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyromania "Pyromania"), which is classified as an [impulse-control disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impulse-control_disorder "Impulse-control disorder") where individuals repeatedly fail to resist impulses to start fires deliberately. In contrast is [pyrophobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrophobia "Pyrophobia"), an irrational fear of fire. This [anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_disorder "Anxiety disorder") is a less common phobia.
