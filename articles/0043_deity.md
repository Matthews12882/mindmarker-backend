# Deity

A **deity** is a [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural "Supernatural") being considered to have authority over some aspect of the universe and/or [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life "Life"), with many deities also regarded as [sacred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred "Sacred") and as worthy of [worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worship "Worship"). A deity is "a spirit or being believed to have created, or to control some part of the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") or life, for which such a deity is often worshipped". The *[Oxford Dictionary of English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_English "Oxford Dictionary of English")* explains *deity* as anything revered as [divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine "Divine"); while [C. Scott Littleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Scott_Littleton "C. Scott Littleton") defines a deity as "a being with powers greater than those of ordinary humans, but who interacts with humans, positively or negatively, in ways that carry humans to new [levels of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_consciousness "Higher consciousness"), beyond the grounded preoccupations of ordinary life". English-speakers call a male deity a **god**, and a female deity a **goddess**.

Belief in the existence of at least one deity, who may interact with the world, is called [theism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism "Theism"). Theistic beliefs can be categorized by how many deities their adherents worship. [Monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") accepts only one deity, whereas [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism") religions accept multiple deities. [Henotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism "Henotheism") religions accept one supreme deity without denying other deities, considering them as aspects of the same divine principle. [Nontheistic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nontheistic_religion "Nontheistic religion") deny any supreme eternal [creator deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity "Creator deity"), but may accept a [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_(religion) "Pantheon (religion)") of deities which live, die and may be reborn like any other being.

Although most monotheistic religions traditionally envision their god as [omnipotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipotence "Omnipotence"), [omnipresent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipresence "Omnipresence"), [omniscient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omniscience "Omniscience"), [omnibenevolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnibenevolence "Omnibenevolence"), and [eternal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortality "Immortality"), none of these qualities are essential to the definition of a "deity" and various cultures have conceptualized their deities differently.
Some deities may incorporate [superintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence "Superintelligence").
[Some philosophical forms of monotheism or henotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_theism "Classical theism") go a step further and precisely [identify God with Being itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_simplicity "Divine simplicity") as a metaphysical grounding for super-human attributes. Monotheistic religions typically refer to their god in masculine terms,
while other religions refer to their deities in a variety of ways—male, female, [hermaphroditic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermaphrodite "Hermaphrodite"), or genderless.

Many cultures—including the ancient [Mesopotamians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia"), [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"), [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"), [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome"), and [Germanic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples "Germanic peoples")—have personified [natural phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_natural_phenomena "List of natural phenomena"), variously as either deliberate causes or effects. Some [Avestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avesta "Avesta") and [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") deities were viewed as ethical concepts.

## Etymology

The English language word *deity* derives from [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French "Old French") *deité*,[*[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") *deitatem* (nominative *deitas*) or "divine nature", coined by [Augustine of Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo") from *[deus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deus "Deus")* ("god"). Deus is related through a common [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language "Proto-Indo-European language") (PIE) origin to *[\*deiwos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyeus "Dyeus")*. This root yields the ancient Indian word *[Deva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Hinduism) "Deva (Hinduism)")* meaning "to gleam, a shining one", from \*div- "to shine", as well as [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") *dios* "[divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity "Divinity")" and [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus "Zeus"); and Latin *deus* "god" ([Old Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Latin "Old Latin") *deivos*). Deva is masculine, and the related feminine equivalent is [devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devi "Devi"). Etymologically, the cognates of *Devi* are Latin *dea* and Greek *thea*. In [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian "Old Persian"), *[daiva-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daeva "Daeva")* means "[demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demon "Demon"), evil god", while in [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") it means the opposite, referring to the "heavenly, divine, terrestrial things of high excellence, exalted, shining ones".

The closely linked term "[god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_(word) "God (word)")" refers to "supreme being, deity", according to Douglas Harper, and is derived from [Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic_language "Proto-Germanic language") \**guthan*, from PIE \**ghut-*, which means "that which is invoked". *Guth* in the [Irish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language "Irish language") means "voice". The term \**ghut-* is also the source of [Old Church Slavonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Church_Slavonic "Old Church Slavonic") *zovo* ("to call"), Sanskrit *huta-* ("invoked", an epithet of [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra")), from the root \**gheu(e)-* ("to call, invoke."),

An alternate etymology for the term "god" comes from the Proto-Germanic [Gaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaut "Gaut"), which traces it to the PIE root \**ghu-to-* ("poured"), derived from the root \**gheu-* ("to pour, pour a [libation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libation "Libation")"). The term \**gheu-* is also the source of the Greek *khein* "to pour". Originally the word "god" and its other Germanic cognates were [neuter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender "Grammatical gender") nouns but shifted to being generally masculine after [Christianisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianisation_of_the_Germanic_peoples "Christianisation of the Germanic peoples") in which the [god is typically seen as male](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_of_God_in_Christianity "Gender of God in Christianity"). In contrast, all ancient [Indo-European cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_society "Proto-Indo-European society") and [mythologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_religion "Proto-Indo-European religion") recognized both masculine and feminine deities.

## Definitions

There is no universally accepted consensus on what a deity is, and concepts of deities vary considerably across cultures. [Huw Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huw_Owen "Huw Owen") states that the term "deity or god or its equivalent in other languages" has a bewildering range of meanings and significance. It has ranged from "infinite transcendent being who created and lords over the universe" (God), to a "finite entity or experience, with special significance or which evokes a special feeling" (god), to "a concept in religious or philosophical context that relates to nature or magnified beings or a supra-mundane realm", to "numerous other usages".

A deity is typically conceptualized as a supernatural or divine concept, manifesting in ideas and knowledge, in a form that combines excellence in some or all aspects, wrestling with weakness and questions in other aspects, [heroic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero "Hero") in outlook and actions, yet tied up with emotions and desires. In other cases, the deity is a principle or reality such as the idea of "soul". The [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishad "Upanishad") of Hinduism, for example, characterize [Atman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atman_(Hinduism) "Atman (Hinduism)") (soul, self) as *deva* (deity), thereby asserting that the *deva* and eternal supreme principle ([Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman")) is part of every living creature, that this soul is spiritual and divine, and that to realize self-knowledge is to know the supreme.

[Theism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism "Theism") is the belief in the existence of one or more deities. [Polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism") is the belief in and worship of multiple deities, which are usually assembled into a [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_(religion) "Pantheon (religion)") of gods and [goddesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddess "Goddess"), with accompanying [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual "Ritual"). In most polytheistic religions, the different gods and goddesses are representations of forces of nature or [ancestral principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_worship "Ancestral worship"), and can be viewed either as autonomous or as aspects or [emanations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism "Emanationism") of a creator God or [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_(religion) "Transcendence (religion)") [absolute principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_(philosophy) "Absolute (philosophy)") ([monistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism") theologies), which manifests [immanently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanence "Immanence") in nature.

[Monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") is the belief that only one deity exists.[*[excessive citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#Bundling_citations "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] A monotheistic deity is usually described as [omnipotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipotence "Omnipotence"), [omnipresent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipresence "Omnipresence"), [omniscient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omniscience "Omniscience"), [omnibenevolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnibenevolence "Omnibenevolence") and [eternal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortality "Immortality"). However, not all deities have been regarded this way and an entity does not need to be almighty, omnipresent, omniscient, omnibenevolent or eternal to qualify as a deity. Monotheistic religions typically refer to their god in masculine terms. A similar concept, [monolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolatry "Monolatry"), while accepting that many deities exist, believes that only one of these deities may be validly worshipped. [Henotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism "Henotheism"), accepts the existence of more than one deity, but considers all deities as equivalent representations or aspects of the same divine principle, the highest.

[Deism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deism "Deism") is the belief that only one deity exists, who created the universe, but does not usually intervene in the resulting world.[*[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*] Deism was particularly popular among western intellectuals during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. [Pantheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism "Pantheism") is the belief that the universe itself is God or that everything composes an all-encompassing, [immanent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanence "Immanence") deity. [Pandeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandeism "Pandeism") is an intermediate position between these, proposing that the creator became a pantheistic universe. [Panentheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panentheism "Panentheism") is the belief that [divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity "Divinity") pervades the universe, but that it also [transcends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_(religion) "Transcendence (religion)") the universe. [Agnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism "Agnosticism") is the position that it is impossible to know for certain whether a deity of any kind exists. [Atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism") is the non-belief in the existence of any deity.

## Prehistoric

Scholars infer the probable existence of deities in the prehistoric period from inscriptions and prehistoric arts such as [cave drawings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_painting "Cave painting"), but it is unclear what these sketches and paintings are and why they were made. Some engravings or sketches show animals, hunters or rituals. It was once common for archaeologists to interpret virtually every prehistoric female figurine as a representation of a single, primordial goddess, the ancestor of historically attested goddesses such as [Inanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inanna "Inanna"), [Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishtar "Ishtar"), [Astarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astarte "Astarte"), [Cybele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybele "Cybele"), and [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite "Aphrodite"); this approach has now generally been discredited. Modern archaeologists now generally recognize that it is impossible to conclusively identify any prehistoric figurines as representations of any kind of deities, let alone goddesses. Nonetheless, it is possible to evaluate ancient representations on a case-by-case basis and rate them on how likely they are to represent deities. The [Venus of Willendorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_of_Willendorf "Venus of Willendorf"), a female figurine found in Europe and dated to about 25,000 BCE has been interpreted by some as an exemplar of a prehistoric female deity. A number of probable representations of deities have been discovered at ['Ain Ghazal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayn_Ghazal_(archaeological_site) "Ayn Ghazal (archaeological site)") and the works of art uncovered at [Çatalhöyük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk "Çatalhöyük") reveal references to what is probably a complex mythology.

## Religions and cultures

### Sub-Saharan African

Diverse African cultures developed theology and concepts of deities over their history. In [Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigeria "Nigeria") and neighboring [West African countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa "West Africa"), for example, two prominent deities (locally called *[Òrìṣà](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orisha "Orisha")*) are found in the [Yoruba religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_religion "Yoruba religion"), namely the god [Ogun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogun "Ogun") and the goddess [Osun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oshun "Oshun"). Ogun is the primordial masculine deity as well as the archdivinity and guardian of occupations such as tools making and use, metal working, hunting, war, protection and ascertaining equity and justice. Osun is an equally powerful primordial feminine deity and a multidimensional guardian of fertility, water, maternal, health, social relations, love and peace. Ogun and Osun traditions were brought into the Americas on [slave ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_ship "Slave ship"). They were preserved by the Africans in their plantation communities, and their festivals continue to be observed.

In [Southern African cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa "Southern Africa"), a similar masculine-feminine deity combination has appeared in other forms, particularly as the Moon and Sun deities. One Southern African cosmology consists of *Hieseba* or *Xuba* (deity, god), *Gaune* (evil spirits) and *Khuene* (people). The *Hieseba* includes *Nladiba* (male, creator sky god) and *Nladisara* (females, Nladiba's two wives). The Sun (female) and the Moon (male) deities are viewed as offspring of *Nladiba* and two *Nladisara*. The Sun and Moon are viewed as manifestations of the supreme deity, and worship is timed and directed to them. In other African cultures the Sun is seen as male, while the Moon is female, both symbols of the godhead. In [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe "Zimbabwe"), the supreme deity is [androgynous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androgyny "Androgyny") with male-female aspects, envisioned as the giver of rain, treated simultaneously as the god of darkness and light and is called *Mwari Shona*. In the [Lake Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Victoria "Lake Victoria") region, the term for a deity is *Lubaale*, or alternatively *Jok*.

### Ancient Near Eastern

#### Egyptian

[Ancient Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") culture revered numerous deities. Egyptian records and inscriptions list the names of many whose nature is unknown and make vague references to other unnamed deities. [Egyptologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptology "Egyptology") [James P. Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Peter_Allen "James Peter Allen") estimates that more than 1,400 deities are named in Egyptian texts, whereas Christian Leitz offers an estimate of "thousands upon thousands" of Egyptian deities. Their terms for deities were *nṯr* (god), and feminine *nṯrt* (goddess); however, these terms may also have applied to any being – spirits and deceased human beings, but not demons – who in some way were outside the sphere of everyday life. Egyptian deities typically had an associated cult, role and mythologies.

Around 200 deities are prominent in the [Pyramid texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_Texts "Pyramid Texts") and ancient temples of Egypt, many [zoomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoomorphism "Zoomorphism"). Among these, were *Min* (fertility god), *Neith* (creator goddess), *Anubis*, *Atum*, *Bes*, *Horus*, *Isis*, *Ra*, *Meretseger*, *Nut*, *Osiris*, *Shu*, *Sia* and *Thoth*. Most Egyptian deities represented natural phenomenon, physical objects or social aspects of life, as hidden immanent forces within these phenomena. The deity *Shu*, for example represented air; the goddess *Meretseger* represented parts of the earth, and the god *Sia* represented the abstract powers of perception. Deities such as *Ra* and *Osiris* were associated with the judgement of the dead and their care during the afterlife. Major gods often had multiple roles and were involved in multiple phenomena.

The first written evidence of deities are from early 3rd millennium BCE, likely emerging from prehistoric beliefs. However, deities became systematized and sophisticated after the formation of an Egyptian state under the [Pharaohs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaoh "Pharaoh") and their treatment as [sacred kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_king "Sacred king") who had exclusive rights to interact with the gods, in the later part of the 3rd millennium BCE. Through the early centuries of the common era, as Egyptians interacted and traded with neighboring cultures, foreign deities were adopted and venerated.

#### Levantine

The ancient [Canaanites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaanites "Canaanites") were polytheists who believed in a pantheon of deities, the chief of whom was the god [El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_(deity) "El (deity)"), who ruled alongside his consort [Asherah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asherah "Asherah") and their [seventy sons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sons_of_God "Sons of God"). [Baal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baal "Baal") was the god of storm, rain, vegetation and fertility, while his consort [Anat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anat "Anat") was the goddess of war and [Astarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astarte "Astarte"), the [West Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Semitic_languages "West Semitic languages") equivalent to [Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishtar "Ishtar"), was the goddess of love. The people of the [Kingdoms of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_(Samaria) "Kingdom of Israel (Samaria)") and [Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah "Kingdom of Judah") originally believed in these deities, alongside their own [national god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_god "National god") Yahweh. El later became [syncretized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism") with [Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh "Yahweh"), who took over El's role as the head of the pantheon, with Asherah as his divine consort and the "sons of El" as his offspring. During the later years of the [Kingdom of Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah "Kingdom of Judah"), a [monolatristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monolatry "Monolatry") faction rose to power insisting that only Yahweh was fit to be worshipped by the people of Judah. Monolatry became enforced during the reforms of [King Josiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah "Josiah") in 621 BCE. Finally, during the national crisis of the [Babylonian captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity "Babylonian captivity"), some [Judahites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ioudaios "Ioudaios") began to teach that deities aside from Yahweh were not just unfit to be worshipped, but did not exist. The "sons of El" were demoted from deities to [angels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angels_in_Judaism "Angels in Judaism").

#### Mesopotamian

Ancient [Mesopotamian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") in southern [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq") had numerous *[dingir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dingir "Dingir")* (deities, gods and goddesses). Mesopotamian deities were almost exclusively anthropomorphic. They were thought to possess extraordinary powers and were often envisioned as being of tremendous physical size. They were generally immortal, but a few of them, particularly [Dumuzid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumuzid_the_Shepherd "Dumuzid the Shepherd"), [Geshtinanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geshtinanna "Geshtinanna"), and [Gugalanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gugalanna "Gugalanna") were said to have either died or visited the underworld. Both male and female deities were widely venerated.

In the Sumerian pantheon, deities had multiple functions, which included presiding over procreation, rains, irrigation, agriculture, destiny, and justice. The gods were fed, clothed, entertained, and worshipped to prevent natural catastrophes as well as to prevent social chaos such as pillaging, rape, or atrocities. Many of the Sumerian deities were [patron guardians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutelary_deity "Tutelary deity") of [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state "City-state").

The most important deities in the Sumerian pantheon were known as the [Anunnaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anunnaki "Anunnaki"), and included deities known as the "seven gods who decree": [An](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anu "Anu"), [Enlil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlil "Enlil"), [Enki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enki "Enki"), [Ninhursag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninhursag "Ninhursag"), [Nanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin_(mythology) "Sin (mythology)"), [Utu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utu "Utu") and [Inanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inanna "Inanna"). After the conquest of Sumer by [Sargon of Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad "Sargon of Akkad"), many Sumerian deities were [syncretized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism") with [East Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Semitic "East Semitic") ones. The goddess Inanna, syncretized with the East Semitic Ishtar, became popular, with temples across Mesopotamia.

The Mesopotamian mythology of the first millennium BCE treated [Anšar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anshar "Anshar") (later [Aššur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashur_(god) "Ashur (god)")) and [Kišar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kishar "Kishar") as primordial deities. [Marduk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marduk "Marduk") was a significant god among the Babylonians. He rose from an obscure deity of the third millennium BCE to become one of the most important deities in the Mesopotamian pantheon of the first millennium BCE. The Babylonians worshipped Marduk as creator of heaven, earth and humankind, and as their [national god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_god "National god"). Marduk's iconography is zoomorphic and is most often found in Middle Eastern archaeological remains depicted as a "snake-dragon" or a "human-animal hybrid".

### Indo-European

#### Germanic

In [Germanic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages "Germanic languages"), the terms cognate with '[god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_(word) "God (word)")' such as [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English_language "Old English language"): *god* and [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_language "Old Norse language"): *guð* were originally neuter. In [Norse mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_mythology "Norse mythology"), *[Æsir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86sir "Æsir")* (singular *áss* or *ǫ́ss*) are the principal group of gods, while the term *ásynjur* (singular *ásynja*) refers specifically to the female *Æsir*. These terms, states John Lindow, may be ultimately rooted in the Indo-European root for "breath" (as in "life giving force"), and are cognate with [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English_language "Old English language"): *os* (a [heathen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_paganism "Anglo-Saxon paganism") god) and [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language "Gothic language"): *anses*.

Another group of deities found in Norse mythology are termed as *[Vanir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanir "Vanir")*, and are associated with fertility. The *Æsir* and the *Vanir* [went to war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86sir%E2%80%93Vanir_War "Æsir–Vanir War"), according to the Nordic sources. The account in *[Ynglinga saga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ynglinga_saga "Ynglinga saga")* describes the Æsir–Vanir War ending in truce and ultimate reconciliation of the two into a single group of gods, after both sides chose peace, exchanged ambassadors (hostages), and intermarried.

The Norse mythology describes the cooperation after the war, as well as differences between the *Æsir* and the *Vanir* which were considered scandalous by the other side. The goddess [Freyja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freyja "Freyja") of the *Vanir* taught magic to the *Æsir*, while the two sides discover that while *Æsir* forbid [mating between siblings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest_between_siblings "Incest between siblings"), *Vanir* accepted such mating.

[Temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathen_hof "Heathen hof") hosting [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphic_wooden_cult_figurines_of_Central_and_Northern_Europe "Anthropomorphic wooden cult figurines of Central and Northern Europe") of Germanic gods (such as [Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor "Thor"), [Odin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odin "Odin") and [Freyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freyr "Freyr")), as well as pagan worship rituals, continued in [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia "Scandinavia") into the 12th century, according to historical records. It has been proposed that over time, Christian equivalents were substituted for the Germanic deities to help suppress [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Nordic_religion "Old Nordic religion") as part of the [Christianisation of the Germanic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianisation_of_the_Germanic_peoples "Christianisation of the Germanic peoples"). Worship of the Germanic gods has been revived in the modern period as part of the [new religious movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement "New religious movement") of [Heathenry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathenry_(new_religious_movement) "Heathenry (new religious movement)").

#### Greek

The [ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") revered both gods and goddesses. These continued to be revered through the early centuries of the common era, and many of the Greek deities inspired and were adopted as part of much larger pantheon of Roman deities. The Greek religion was polytheistic, but had no centralized church, nor any sacred texts. The deities were largely associated with myths and they represented natural phenomena or aspects of human behavior.

Several Greek deities probably trace back to more ancient Indo-European traditions, since the gods and goddesses found in distant cultures are mythologically comparable and are [cognates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate "Cognate"). [Eos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eos "Eos"), the Greek goddess of the dawn, for instance, is cognate to Indic *[Ushas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushas "Ushas")*, Roman *[Aurora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora_(mythology) "Aurora (mythology)")* and Latvian *[Auseklis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auseklis "Auseklis")*. [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus "Zeus"), the Greek king of gods, is cognate to Latin *[Iūpiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology) "Jupiter (mythology)")*, Old German *[Ziu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%BDr "Týr")*, and Indic *[Dyaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyaus_Pita "Dyaus Pita")*, with whom he shares similar mythologies. Other deities, such as [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite "Aphrodite"), originated from the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East "Near East").

Greek deities varied locally, but many shared panhellenic themes, celebrated similar festivals, rites, and ritual grammar. The most important deities in the Greek pantheon were the [Twelve Olympians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Olympians "Twelve Olympians"): Zeus, [Hera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera "Hera"), [Poseidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poseidon "Poseidon"), [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena "Athena"), [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo "Apollo"), [Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis "Artemis"), Aphrodite, [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes "Hermes"), [Demeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demeter "Demeter"), [Dionysus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysus "Dionysus"), [Hephaestus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestus "Hephaestus"), and [Ares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ares "Ares"). Other important Greek deities included [Hestia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hestia "Hestia"), [Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades "Hades") and [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles "Heracles"). These deities later inspired the *[Dii Consentes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dii_Consentes "Dii Consentes")* galaxy of Roman deities.

Besides the Olympians, the Greeks also worshipped various local deities. Among these were the goat-legged god [Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(mythology) "Pan (mythology)") (the guardian of shepherds and their flocks), [Nymphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nymph "Nymph") ([nature spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nature_deities "List of nature deities") associated with particular landforms), [Naiads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naiad "Naiad") (who dwelled in springs), [Dryads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dryad "Dryad") (who were spirits of the trees), [Nereids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nereid "Nereid") (who inhabited the sea), river gods, [satyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyr "Satyr") (a class of lustful male nature spirits), and others. The dark powers of the underworld were represented by the [Erinyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erinyes "Erinyes") (or Furies), said to pursue those guilty of crimes against blood-relatives.

The Greek deities, like those in many other Indo-European traditions, were anthropomorphic. [Walter Burkert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Burkert "Walter Burkert") describes them as "persons, not abstractions, ideas or concepts". They had fantastic abilities and powers; each had some unique expertise and, in some aspects, a specific and flawed personality. They were not omnipotent and could be injured in some circumstances. Greek deities led to cults, were used politically and inspired [votive offerings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Votive_offerings "Votive offerings") for favors such as bountiful crops, healthy family, victory in war, or peace for a loved one recently deceased.

#### Roman

The Roman pantheon had numerous deities, both Greek and non-Greek. The more famed deities, found in the mythologies and the 2nd millennium CE European arts, have been the anthropomorphic deities syncretized with the Greek deities. These include the six gods and six goddesses: Venus, Apollo, Mars, Diana, Minerva, Ceres, Vulcan, Juno, Mercury, Vesta, Neptune, Jupiter (Jove, Zeus); as well Bacchus, Pluto and Hercules. The non-Greek major deities include Janus, Fortuna, Vesta, Quirinus and Tellus (mother goddess, probably most ancient). Some of the non-Greek deities had likely origins in more ancient European culture such as the ancient Germanic religion, while others may have been borrowed, for political reasons, from neighboring trade centers such as those in the [Minoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoan_civilization "Minoan civilization") or [ancient Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt") civilization.

The Roman deities, in a manner similar to the ancient Greeks, inspired community festivals, rituals and sacrifices led by *flamines* (priests, pontifs), but priestesses (Vestal Virgins) were also held in high esteem for maintaining sacred fire used in the votive rituals for deities. Deities were also maintained in home shrines (*lararium*), such as Hestia honored in homes as the goddess of fire hearth. This Roman religion held reverence for sacred fire, and this is also found in Hebrew culture (Leviticus 6), Vedic culture's Homa, ancient Greeks and other cultures.

Ancient Roman scholars such as Varro and Cicero wrote treatises on the nature of gods of their times. Varro stated, in his *Antiquitates Rerum Divinarum*, that it is the superstitious man who fears the gods, while the truly religious person venerates them as parents. Cicero, in his *[Academica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academica_(Cicero) "Academica (Cicero)")*, praised Varro for this and other insights. According to Varro, there have been three accounts of deities in the Roman society: the mythical account created by poets for theatre and entertainment, the civil account used by people for veneration as well as by the city, and the natural account created by the philosophers. The best state is, adds Varro, where the civil theology combines the poetic mythical account with the philosopher's. The Roman deities continued to be revered in Europe through the era of Constantine, and past 313 CE when he issued the Edict of Toleration.

### Native American

#### Inca

The [Inca culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire "Inca Empire") has believed in *[Viracocha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viracocha "Viracocha")* (also called *Pachacutec*) as the [creator deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity "Creator deity"). *Viracocha* has been an abstract deity to Inca culture, one who existed before he created space and time. All other deities of the Inca people have corresponded to elements of nature. Of these, the most important ones have been *[Inti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inti "Inti")* ([sun deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_deity "Solar deity")) responsible for agricultural prosperity and as the father of the first Inca king, and *Mama Qucha* the goddess of the sea, lakes, rivers and waters. *Inti* in some mythologies is the son of *Viracocha* and *Mama Qucha*.

> **Inca Sun deity festival**
>
> Oh creator and Sun and Thunder,
> be forever copious,
> do not make us old,
> let all things be at peace,
> multiply the people,
> and let there be food,
> and let all things be fruitful.

— Inti Raymi prayers

Inca people have revered many male and female deities. Among the feminine deities have been *Mama Kuka* (goddess of joy), *Mama Ch'aska* (goddess of dawn), *Mama Allpa* (goddess of harvest and earth, sometimes called *Mama Pacha* or *[Pachamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pachamama "Pachamama")*), [Mama Killa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mama_Killa "Mama Killa") ([moon goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_lunar_deities "List of lunar deities")) and *Mama Sara* (goddess of grain). During and after the imposition of Christianity during [Spanish colonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire "Spanish Empire"), the Inca people retained their original beliefs in deities through [syncretism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism"), where they overlay the Christian God and teachings over their original beliefs and practices. The male deity *Inti* became accepted as the Christian God, but the Andean rituals centered around Inca deities have been retained and continued thereafter into the modern era by the Inca people.

#### Maya and Aztec

In [Maya culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization"), *[Kukulkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kukulkan "Kukulkan")* has been the supreme [creator deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity "Creator deity"), also revered as the god of [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation "Reincarnation"), water, fertility and wind. The Maya people built [step pyramid temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_pyramids "Mesoamerican pyramids") to honor *Kukulkan*, aligning them to the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun")'s position on the spring [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox "Equinox"). Other deities found at Maya archaeological sites include *[Xib Chac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chac-Xib-Chac "Chac-Xib-Chac")*—the benevolent male rain deity, and *[Ixchel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixchel "Ixchel")*—the benevolent female earth, weaving and pregnancy goddess. The [Maya calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_calendar "Maya calendar") had 18 months, each with 20 days (and five unlucky days of *Uayeb*); each month had a presiding deity, who inspired social rituals, special trading markets and community festivals.

A deity with aspects similar to *Kulkulkan* in the Aztec culture has been called *[Quetzalcoatl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetzalcoatl "Quetzalcoatl")*. However, states Timothy Insoll, the Aztec ideas of deity remain poorly understood. What has been assumed is based on what was constructed by [Christian missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mission "Christian mission"). The deity concept was likely more complex than these historical records. In [Aztec culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_culture "Aztec culture"), there were hundred of deities, but many were henotheistic [incarnations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation "Incarnation") of one another (similar to the [avatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar "Avatar") concept of Hinduism). Unlike Hinduism and other cultures, Aztec deities were usually not anthropomorphic, and were instead zoomorphic or hybrid icons associated with spirits, natural phenomena or forces. The Aztec deities were often represented through ceramic figurines, revered in home shrines.

### Polynesian

The [Polynesian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians "Polynesians") developed a theology centered on numerous deities, with clusters of islands having different names for the same idea. There are great deities found across the Pacific Ocean. Some deities are found widely, and there are many local deities whose worship is limited to one or a few islands or sometimes to isolated villages on the same island.

The [Māori people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people "Māori people"), of what is now New Zealand, called the supreme being as *[Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_Matua_Kore "Io Matua Kore")*, who is also referred elsewhere as *Iho-Iho*, *Io-Mataaho*, *Io Nui*, *Te Io Ora*, *Io Matua Te Kora* among other names. The *Io* deity has been revered as the original uncreated creator, with power of life, with nothing outside or beyond him.Other deities in the Polynesian pantheon include *[Tangaloa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangaroa "Tangaroa")* (god who created men), *[La'a Maomao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La%27a_Maomao "La'a Maomao")* (god of winds), *[Tu-Matauenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%ABmatauenga "Tūmatauenga")* or *[Ku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%AB "Kū")* (god of war), *[Tu-Metua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu-metua "Tu-metua")* (mother goddess), *[Kane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81ne "Kāne")* (god of procreation) and *[Rangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangi_and_Papa "Rangi and Papa")* (sky god father).

The Polynesian deities have been part of a sophisticated theology, addressing questions of creation, the nature of existence, guardians in daily lives as well as during wars, natural phenomena, good and evil spirits, priestly rituals, as well as linked to the journey of the souls of the dead.

### Abrahamic

#### Christianity

Christianity is a monotheistic religion in which most mainstream congregations and denominations accept the concept of the Holy [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity "Trinity"). Modern orthodox Christians believe that the Trinity is composed of three equal, [cosubstantial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consubstantiality "Consubstantiality") persons: [God the Father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Father "God the Father"), [God the Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Son "God the Son"), and the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit "Holy Spirit"). The first person to describe the persons of the Trinity as *homooúsios* (ὁμοούσιος; "of the same substance") was the [Church Father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers "Church Fathers") Origen. Although most early Christian theologians (including [Origen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origen "Origen")) were [Subordinationists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subordinationism "Subordinationism"), who believed that the Father was superior to the Son and the Son superior to the Holy Spirit, this belief was condemned as heretical by the [First Council of Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea "First Council of Nicaea") in the fourth century, which declared that all three persons of the Trinity are equal. Christians regard the universe as an element in God's actualization and the Holy Spirit is seen as the divine essence that is "the unity and relation of the [Father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Father "God the Father") and the Son". According to George Hunsinger, the doctrine of the Trinity justifies worship in a Church, wherein Jesus Christ is deemed to be a full deity with the [Christian cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross "Christian cross") as his icon.

The theological examination of Jesus Christ, of divine grace in incarnation, his non-transferability and completeness has been a historic topic. For example, the [Council of Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon "Council of Chalcedon") in 451 CE declared that in "one person Jesus Christ, fullness of deity and fullness of humanity are united, the union of the natures being such that they can neither be divided nor confused". Jesus Christ, according to the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament"), is the self-disclosure of the one, true God, both in his teaching and in his person; Christ, in Christian faith, is considered the incarnation of God.

#### Islam

[Ilah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilah "Ilah"), **ʾIlāh** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language"): إله; plural: آلهة **ʾālihah**), is an [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") word meaning "god". It appears in the name of the monotheistic god of Islam as [Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah "Allah") (**[al-Lāh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah "Allah")**). which literally means "the god" in Arabic. Islam is strictly monotheistic and the first statement of the *[shahada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahada "Shahada")*, or Muslim confession of faith, is that "there is no **ʾilāh** (deity) but Allah (God)", who is perfectly unified and utterly indivisible.

The term [Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah "Allah") is used by Muslims for God. The Persian word *[Khuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuda "Khuda")* ([Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"): خدا) can be translated as god, lord or king, and is also used today to refer to [God in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam") by Persian, [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu "Urdu"), [Tat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tat_language_(Caucasus) "Tat language (Caucasus)") and [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_languages "Kurdish languages") speakers. The [Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages "Turkic languages") word for god is [Tengri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengri "Tengri"); it exists as *Tanrı* in [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language "Turkish language").

#### Judaism

Judaism affirms the existence of one God (Yahweh, or YHWH), who is not abstract, but He who revealed himself throughout Jewish history particularly during the Exodus and the Exile. Judaism reflects a monotheism that gradually arose, was affirmed with certainty in the sixth century "Second Isaiah", and has ever since been the axiomatic basis of its theology.

The classical presentation of Judaism has been as a monotheistic faith that rejected deities and related idolatry. However, states Breslauer, modern scholarship suggests that idolatry was not absent in biblical faith, and it resurfaced multiple times in Jewish religious life. The rabbinic texts and other secondary Jewish literature suggest worship of material objects and natural phenomena through the medieval era, while the core teachings of Judaism maintained monotheism.[*[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*]

According to Aryeh Kaplan, God is always referred to as "He" in Judaism, "not to imply that the concept of sex or gender applies to God", but because "there is no neuter in the Hebrew language, and the Hebrew word for God is a masculine noun" as he "is an active rather than a passive creative force".

#### Mandaeism

In [Mandaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeism "Mandaeism"), *[Hayyi Rabbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayyi_Rabbi "Hayyi Rabbi")* (lit=The Great Life), or 'The Great Living God', is the supreme God from which all things [emanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism "Emanationism"). He is also known as 'The First Life', since during the creation of the material world, [Yushamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yushamin "Yushamin") emanated from Hayyi Rabbi as the "Second Life." "The principles of the Mandaean doctrine: the belief of the only one great God, Hayyi Rabbi, to whom all absolute properties belong; He created all the worlds, formed the soul through his power, and placed it by means of angels into the human body. So He created [Adam and Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_and_Eve "Adam and Eve"), the first man and woman." Mandaeans recognize God to be the eternal, creator of all, the one and only in domination who has no partner.

### Indian

In [Indian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions "Indian religions"), deities have been envisioned as manifesting within the temple of every living being's body, as sensory organs and mind. Deities are envisioned as a form of existence ([Saṃsāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra "Saṃsāra")) after [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation "Reincarnation"), for human beings who gain merit through an ethical life, where they become [guardian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutelary_deity "Tutelary deity") and live blissfully in [heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven "Heaven"), but are also subject to death when their merit is lost.

#### Hinduism

The concept of "deity" varies in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"), it being a diverse system of thought with beliefs spanning [henotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism "Henotheism"), [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism"), [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"), [panentheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panentheism "Panentheism"), [pantheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism "Pantheism") and [monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism") among others.

In the ancient [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") texts of Hinduism, a deity is often referred to as [Deva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Hinduism) "Deva (Hinduism)") (god) or [Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devi "Devi") (goddess). The root of these terms mean "heavenly, divine, anything of excellence". Deva is masculine, and the related feminine equivalent is [devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devi "Devi"). In the earliest Vedic literature, all [supernatural beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural_being "Supernatural being") are called [Asuras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asura "Asura"). Over time, those with a benevolent nature become deities and are referred to as *Sura*, Deva or Devi.

Devas or deities in Hindu texts differ from Greek or Roman [theodicy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodicy "Theodicy"), states Ray Billington, because many Hindu traditions believe that a human being has the potential to be reborn as a *deva* (or *devi*), by living an ethical life and building up saintly *[karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma "Karma")*. Such a *deva* enjoys heavenly bliss, till the merit runs out, and then the [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul "Soul") is reborn again into [Saṃsāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra "Saṃsāra"). Thus deities are henotheistic manifestations, embodiments and consequence of the virtuous, the noble, the saint-like living in many Hindu traditions.

#### Jainism

Cosmology in Jainism incorporates a meaningful [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality")-driven reality, including four realms of existence (*gati*), one of them being *[deva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Jainism) "Deva (Jainism)")* (celestial beings, gods). A human being can choose and live an ethical life, such as being non-violent ([ahimsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa "Ahimsa")) against all living beings, and thereby gain merit and be reborn as *deva*.

Jain texts reject a creator or trans-cosmic God, one who stands outside of the universe and lords over it, but they state that the world is full of *devas* who are in human-image with sensory organs, with the power of reason, conscious, compassionate and with finite life. Jainism believes in the existence of the [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul "Soul") (Self, [atman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C4%ABva_(Jainism) "Jīva (Jainism)")) and considers it to have "god-quality", whose knowledge and liberation is the ultimate spiritual goal in both religions. Jains also believe that the spiritual nobleness of perfected souls ([Jina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arihant_(Jainism) "Arihant (Jainism)")) and *devas* make them worship-worthy beings, with powers of guardianship and guidance to better *[karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_in_Jainism "Karma in Jainism")*. In Jain temples or festivals, the Jinas and Devas are revered.

#### Buddhism

Deities are an essential part of Buddhist teachings about cosmology, [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_in_Buddhism "Rebirth in Buddhism"), and [saṃsāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_(Buddhism) "Saṃsāra (Buddhism)"). Buddhist deities (such as *[devas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Buddhism) "Deva (Buddhism)")* and *[bodhisattvas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva "Bodhisattva")*) are believed to reside in a pleasant, heavenly realm within [Buddhist cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_cosmology "Buddhist cosmology"), which is typically subdivided into twenty six sub-realms.

*Devas* are numerous and live in the heavenly realm, which they are believed to be reincarnated into as the result of leading an ethical life and accumulating very good [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma "Karma"). A *deva* does not need to work, and is able to enjoy in the heavenly realm all pleasures found on Earth. However, devas are believed to still be mortal, as they die and are reborn like all other beings, and the pleasures of the heavently realm lead to attachment (*[upādāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up%C4%81d%C4%81na "Upādāna")*), lack of spiritual pursuits, and therefore no [nirvana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana "Nirvana"). Nonetheless, the vast majority of Buddhist [lay people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity "Laity") in countries practicing [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada "Theravada") have historically pursued Buddhist rituals and practices because they are motivated by their potential rebirth into the *deva* realm. The *deva* realm in Buddhist practice in Southeast Asia and East Asia include gods found in Hindu traditions such as [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra") and [Brahma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma "Brahma"), and concepts in [Hindu cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_cosmology "Hindu cosmology") such as [Mount Meru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Meru "Mount Meru").

[Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana "Mahayana") Buddhism also includes different kinds of deities, such as numerous [Buddhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhahood "Buddhahood"), [bodhisattvas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva "Bodhisattva") and [fierce deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fierce_deities "Fierce deities"). Buddhists do not believe in a [creator deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity "Creator deity").

### Philippine

[Anitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_Philippine_folk_religions "Indigenous Philippine folk religions"), composed of an array of indigenous religions from the Philippines, has multiple pantheons of deities. There are more than a hundred different [ethnic groups in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_the_Philippines "Ethnic groups in the Philippines"), each having their own supreme deity or deities. Each supreme deity or deities normally rules over a pantheon of deities, contributing to the sheer diversity of deities in Anitism.The supreme deity or deities of ethnic groups are almost always the most notable.

For example, [Bathala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathala "Bathala") is the Tagalog supreme deity, Mangechay is the Kapampangan supreme deity, [Malayari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayari "Mayari") is the Sambal supreme deity, Melu is the Blaan supreme deity, Kaptan is the Bisaya supreme deity, and so on.

### East Asian

#### Musok

[Musok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musok "Musok") is a folk polytheistic religion in [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea"), involving the veneration of many deities known as *[Sillyeong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sillyeong "Sillyeong")* ([Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 신령; [Hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja "Hanja"): 神靈). Sillyeong are supernatural being possessing divine power. They are generally invisible to the human eye but are said to occasionally appear in the form of humans or natural objects. These entities—which include [nature spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_spirits "Nature spirits"), [heroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero "Hero"), and [deified ancestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josang "Josang")—are central to Korean shamanism and are summoned by [mudangs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudang "Mudang") during rituals known as [Gut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gut_(ritual) "Gut (ritual)"). The pantheon of sillyeongs, which has changed over time, is termed sindang (신당; 神堂; lit. 'Divine Shrine'), with over 130 sillyeongs having been identified.

#### Shinto

Shinto is [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"), involving the veneration of many deities known as *kami*, or sometimes as *jingi*. In Japanese, no distinction is made here between singular and plural, and hence the term *kami* refers both to individual *kami* and the collective group of *kami*. Although lacking a direct English translation, the term *kami* has sometimes been rendered as "god" or "spirit". The historian of religion [Joseph Kitagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kitagawa "Joseph Kitagawa") deemed these English translations "quite unsatisfactory and misleading", and various scholars urge against translating *kami* into English. In Japanese, it is often said that there are [eight million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_and_fictitious_numbers#Specific_values_used_as_indefinite "Indefinite and fictitious numbers") *kami*, a term which connotes an infinite number, and Shinto practitioners believe that they are present everywhere. They are not regarded as [omnipotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipotence "Omnipotence"), [omniscient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omniscience "Omniscience"), or necessarily [immortal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortality "Immortality").

#### Taoism

Taoism is a polytheistic religion. The gods and immortals（神仙） believed in by Taoism can be roughly divided into two categories, namely "gods" and "[xian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xian_(Taoism) "Xian (Taoism)")" (immortals). Among them,"Gods" are also called deities and there are many kinds, that is, god of heaven（天神), god of ground（地祇), wuling（物灵: animism, the spirit of all things), god of netherworld（地府神灵), god of human body（人体之神), god of human ghost（人鬼之神）etc. Among these "gods" such as god of heaven（天神), god of ground（地祇), god of netherworld（阴府神灵), god of human body（人体之神） are innate beings.In China, "gods" are often referred to together with "xian". "Xian" (immortals) is acquired the cultivation of the Tao，persons with vast supernatural powers, unpredictable changes and immortality.

### Iranian

#### Zoroastrianism

[Ahura Mazda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahura_Mazda "Ahura Mazda") ([/əˌhʊrəˌmæzdə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")); is the [Avestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avestan_language "Avestan language") name for the creator and sole God of [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism"). The literal meaning of the word *[Ahura](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Ahura "wikt:Ahura")* is "mighty" or "lord" and  *[Mazda](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Mazda "wikt:Mazda")* is *[wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom "Wisdom")*. [Zoroaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster "Zoroaster"), the founder of Zoroastrianism, taught that Ahura Mazda is the most powerful being in all of the existence and the only deity who is worthy of the highest veneration. Nonetheless, Ahura Mazda is not omnipotent because his [evil twin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evil_twin "Evil twin") brother [Angra Mainyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angra_Mainyu "Angra Mainyu") is nearly as powerful as him. Zoroaster taught that the *[daevas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daeva "Daeva")* were evil spirits created by Angra Mainyu to sow evil in the world and that all people must choose between the goodness of Ahura Mazda and the evil of Angra Mainyu. According to Zoroaster, Ahura Mazda will eventually defeat Angra Mainyu and good will triumph over evil once and for all. Ahura Mazda was the most important deity in the ancient [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire"). He was originally represented anthropomorphically, but, by the end of the [Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire"), Zoroastrianism had become fully aniconic.

## Local, regional, and universal deities

A deity may originate as a local deity, whose realm or influence applies to a specific geographic place.
Some local deities may become or merge with trans-regional deities
or even universal ones. Within the Ancient Greek context,  [Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(god) "Pan (god)") started as a local Arcadian deity whose influence spread.
The [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") adopted many regional deities
from places such as Greece ([Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo "Apollo"), for example), Asia ([Cybele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybele "Cybele"), [Mithras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithras "Mithras")) and Egypt ([Isis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isis "Isis"), [Serapis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serapis "Serapis")) in the process of building a multi-ethnic polity. The  [Romans spread the worship of several non-Roman deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome "Religion in ancient Rome") and  [syncretised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_syncretism "Religious syncretism") some of their features. Roman authorities eventually favoured [Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh "Yahweh"), first associated with a mountain in northwestern Arabia.
prior to his becoming a tribal deity of the peripatetic Hebrews
and thus eventually the supreme god of Christianity.

## Skeptical interpretations

Attempts to rationally explain belief in deities extend at least as far back as [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"). The Greek philosopher [Democritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democritus "Democritus") argued that the concept of deities arose when human beings observed natural phenomena such as lightning, [solar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse "Solar eclipse"), and the changing of the seasons. Later, in the third century BCE, the scholar [Euhemerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euhemerus "Euhemerus") argued in his book *Sacred History* that the gods were originally flesh-and-blood mortal kings, [posthumously deified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apotheosis "Apotheosis"), and that religion was therefore the continuation of these kings' mortal reigns, a view now known as [Euhemerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euhemerism "Euhemerism"). [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud") suggested that God-concepts are a projection of one's father.

A tendency to believe in deities and other supernatural beings may be an integral part of human consciousness. Children are naturally inclined to believe in supernatural entities such as gods, spirits, and demons, even without being introduced into a particular religious tradition. Humans have an overactive agency-detection system, which has a tendency to conclude that events are caused by intelligent entities, even if they really are not. This is a system which may have evolved to cope with threats to the survival of human ancestors: in the wild, a person who perceived intelligent and potentially dangerous beings everywhere was more likely to survive than a person who failed to perceive actual threats, such as wild animals or human enemies. Humans are also inclined to think teleologically and to ascribe meaning and significance to their surroundings, traits which may lead people to believe in a creator-deity. This may have developed as a side-effect of human social intelligence, the ability to discern what other people are thinking.

Stories of encounters with supernatural beings are especially likely to be retold, passed on, and embellished due to their descriptions of standard [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological "Ontological") categories (person, artifact, animal, plant, natural object) with counterintuitive properties (humans that are invisible, houses that remember what happened in them, etc.). As belief in deities spread, humans may have attributed anthropomorphic thought-processes to them, leading to the idea of leaving offerings to the gods and praying to them for assistance, ideas which are seen in all cultures around the world.

[Sociologists of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_religion "Sociology of religion") have proposed that the personality and characteristics of deities may reflect a culture's sense of self-esteem and that a culture projects its revered values into deities and in spiritual terms. The cherished, desired or sought human personality is congruent with the personality it defines to be gods. Lonely and fearful societies tend to invent wrathful, violent, submission-seeking deities, while happier and secure societies tend to invent loving, non-violent, compassionate deities. [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim") states that gods represent an extension of human social life to include supernatural beings. According to Matt Rossano, God-concepts may be a means of enforcing [morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality "Morality") and building more cooperative community-groups.
