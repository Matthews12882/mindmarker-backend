# Augustus

**Augustus** (born **Gaius Octavius**; 23 September 63 BC – 19 August AD 14), also known as **Octavian** ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): *Octavianus*), was the founder of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") and the first [Roman emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperor "Roman emperor") from 27 BC until his death in AD 14. The [reign of Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Augustus "Reign of Augustus") initiated an [imperial cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_imperial_cult "Roman imperial cult") and an era of [imperial peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_peace "Imperial peace") (the *[Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana "Pax Romana")* or *[Pax Augusta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ara_Pacis "Ara Pacis")*) when the [Roman world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") was largely free of armed conflict. The [principate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principate "Principate"), a style of government in which the emperor showed nominal deference to [the Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Senate "Roman Senate"), was established during his reign and lasted until the [Crisis of the Third Century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_Third_Century "Crisis of the Third Century").

Octavian [was born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_life_of_Augustus "Early life of Augustus") into an [equestrian branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equites "Equites") of the [*gens* Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavia_gens "Octavia gens"). Octavian's great-uncle, the [*dictator*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictator_perpetuo "Dictator perpetuo") [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar"), named him as his [primary heir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance_law_in_ancient_Rome "Inheritance law in ancient Rome") in his will, and after [Caesar's assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar%27s_assassination "Caesar's assassination") in 44 BC, Octavian inherited his estate and [assumed his name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_of_Augustus "Rise of Augustus"). He fought for the loyalty of [Caesar's legions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_army_of_the_late_Republic "Roman army of the late Republic"). He was made a senator during a [state emergency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Mutina "War of Mutina") and seized power by marching on Rome in 43 BC, becoming its youngest elected [consul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_consul "Roman consul"). He, [Mark Antony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Antony "Mark Antony"), and [Marcus Lepidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Lepidus "Marcus Lepidus") formed [a triumvirate regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Triumvirate "Second Triumvirate") with [legally sanctioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Titia "Lex Titia") powers to [outlaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proscriptions_of_the_Second_Triumvirate "Proscriptions of the Second Triumvirate") and oppose [Caesar's assassins and their allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar%27s_assassins_and_their_allies "Caesar's assassins and their allies"). Following their victory at the [Battle of Philippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Philippi "Battle of Philippi") in 42 BC, the triumvirate divided the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic") among themselves and ruled as *de facto* oligarchs. Competing ambitions eventually tore their alliance apart; Octavian had Lepidus exiled in 36 BC for [opposing him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellum_Siculum "Bellum Siculum") in [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Sicily "Roman Sicily"), while [Marcus Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Agrippa "Marcus Agrippa"), Octavian's naval commander, [defeated Antony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Actium "War of Actium") in [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece "Roman Greece") at the [Battle of Actium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Actium "Battle of Actium") in 31 BC. Antony and his wife [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra"), the [Ptolemaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty "Ptolemaic dynasty") queen of [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom "Ptolemaic Kingdom"), [killed themselves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Cleopatra "Death of Cleopatra") during [Octavian's invasion of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavian%27s_invasion_of_Egypt "Octavian's invasion of Egypt"), which then became Octavian's [personal property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt "Roman Egypt").

After the demise of the triumvirate, Augustus reached an accord with the remaining Roman elite: he would restore the façade of a [free republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_Roman_Republic "Crisis of the Roman Republic"), centered around the Senate, the [executive magistrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_magistrate "Roman magistrate") and the [legislative assemblies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_assemblies "Roman assemblies"). But his [control of the military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_history_of_the_Roman_military "Political history of the Roman military") and half of [Rome's provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome%27s_provinces "Rome's provinces") meant he maintained [autocratic power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legatus_Augusti_pro_praetore "Legatus Augusti pro praetore") legitimized by his appointment as [commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief "Commander-in-chief") of most [Roman armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Roman_army "Imperial Roman army"). To avoid the appearance of [monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_monarchy "Roman monarchy") or [dictatorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_dictator "Roman dictator"), he eventually refused to stand for reelection to the consulship, but the Senate [granted him the powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_reforms_of_Augustus "Constitutional reforms of Augustus") of the [tribunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribunate "Tribunate") and [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_censor "Roman censor") and the titles *[princeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeps "Princeps")* ('first citizen'), *[augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_(title) "Augustus (title)")* ('the revered'), and *[pater patriae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pater_patriae "Pater patriae")* (lit. 'father of the country'), and named the month of August after him. After the death of Lepidus, Augustus also assumed the title of *[pontifex maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifex_maximus "Pontifex maximus")* ('supreme pontiff').

Augustus dramatically [enlarged the Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Augustus "Wars of Augustus"), annexing Egypt, [Dalmatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalmatia_(Roman_province) "Dalmatia (Roman province)"), [Pannonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonia "Pannonia"), [Noricum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noricum "Noricum"), and [Raetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raetia "Raetia"), expanding possessions in [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_(Roman_province) "Africa (Roman province)"), and completing the conquest of [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania "Hispania"). His expansionism, however, suffered [a major setback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Teutoburg_Forest "Battle of the Teutoburg Forest") in [Germania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germania "Germania"). Beyond the frontiers, he secured the empire with a buffer region of [client states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client_state "Client state") and negotiated peace treaties with the [Parthian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire") and [Kingdom of Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush"). He reformed the Roman system of [taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_in_ancient_Rome "Taxation in ancient Rome") and [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_reform_of_Augustus "Coinage reform of Augustus"), developed [networks of roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads "Roman roads") with an [official courier system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursus_publicus "Cursus publicus"), established a standing professional army, established the [Praetorian Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_Guard "Praetorian Guard") as well as official [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohortes_urbanae "Cohortes urbanae") and [fire-fighting services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigiles "Vigiles") for the city of Rome, and renovated much of the city during his reign. Augustus was a [writer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustan_literature_(ancient_Rome) "Augustan literature (ancient Rome)") and [patron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patronage_in_ancient_Rome "Patronage in ancient Rome") of [poets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustan_poetry "Augustan poetry") such as [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil "Virgil"), and has been featured in [various works of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_depictions_of_Augustus "Cultural depictions of Augustus") from ancient to modern times. He died in AD 14 at age 75 from natural causes, and the Senate posthumously [deified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_deification "Roman deification") him. Persistent rumors have claimed his wife [Livia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livia "Livia") poisoned him. He was [succeeded as emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Roman_emperors "List of Roman emperors") by his stepson and adoptive son [Tiberius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius "Tiberius").

## Name

Augustus ([/ɔːˈɡʌstəs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*aw-GUST-əs*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key")) was known by many names throughout his life:

* **Gaius Octavius**: ([/ɒkˈteɪviəs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*ok-TAY-vee-əs*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); Latin: [[ˈɡaːiʊs ɔkˈtaːwiʊs]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin "Help:IPA/Latin")). According to [Suetonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius "Suetonius"), Octavius received the [cognomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognomen "Cognomen") **Thurinus** (Latin: [[tʰuːˈriːnʊs]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin "Help:IPA/Latin"), 'of [Thurii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thurii "Thurii")') in his infancy to commemorate his father's victory over followers of [Spartacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartacus "Spartacus") at Thurii. [Marcus Junius Brutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Junius_Brutus "Marcus Junius Brutus"), the assassin of Octavian's adoptive father [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar"), rejected Octavian's claim to [testamentary adoption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testamentary_adoption "Testamentary adoption") by Caesar by referring to him as *Octavius*.
* **Gaius Julius Caesar**: After Julius Caesar named Octavius his heir in 44 BC, Octavius took Caesar's [nomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomen_gentilicium "Nomen gentilicium") and cognomen. Historians often distinguished him from the late Caesar by adding *Octavianus* (Latin: [[ɔktaːwiˈaːnʊs]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin "Help:IPA/Latin")) after the name, denoting that he was a former member of the [gens Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gens_Octavia "Gens Octavia"). There is no evidence that Augustus did this himself, although some of his contemporaries called him Gaius Octavius, Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus, or "young Caesar". Historians usually refer to him as Octavian ([/ɒkˈteɪviən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*ok-TAY-vee-ən*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key")) for the period between 44 and 27 BC.
* **Imperator Caesar**: Octavian's early coins and inscriptions all refer to him as Gaius Caesar, but by 38 BC he had replaced *Gaius* with the victory title *[imperator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperator "Imperator")* ('commander'). His [family line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio-Claudian_family_tree "Julio-Claudian family tree") continued the use of the name *Caesar*, a cognomen for one branch of the [Julian family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_gens "Julia gens"), and eventually this formed a standard [imperial title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar_(title) "Caesar (title)").
* **Imperator Caesar Augustus**: In 27 BC the Senate granted him the honorific *[Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_(title) "Augustus (title)")* (Latin: [[au̯ˈɡʊstʊs]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin "Help:IPA/Latin")) ('the revered'). Historians use this name, or its converse Augustus Caesar, to refer to him from 27 BC until his death in AD 14.

## Early life

Octavian was born as Gaius Octavius in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome "Rome") on 23 September 63 BC, at a family property on the [Palatine Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatine_Hill "Palatine Hill"). His father, [Gaius Octavius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Octavius_(father_of_Augustus) "Gaius Octavius (father of Augustus)"), came from a moderately wealthy [equestrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equites "Equites") family of the *[gens Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gens_Octavia "Gens Octavia")*. He ascended the [cursus honorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursus_honorum "Cursus honorum") and served as a [proconsular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proconsul "Proconsul") [governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_governor "Roman governor") of [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(Roman_province) "Macedonia (Roman province)"). His family was from [Velitrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velitrae "Velitrae"), near Rome, where his son spent [part of his childhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_life_of_Augustus "Early life of Augustus"). The younger Octavius's mother, [Atia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atia_(mother_of_Augustus) "Atia (mother of Augustus)"), was a niece of Julius Caesar.

After Octavius's father died in 59 BC or 58 BC, his mother married [Lucius Marcius Philippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Marcius_Philippus_(consul_56_BC) "Lucius Marcius Philippus (consul 56 BC)"), who was elected as [consul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_consul "Roman consul") in 56 BC. When Octavius's grandmother [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Minor_(grandmother_of_Augustus) "Julia Minor (grandmother of Augustus)"), sister of Julius Caesar, died in 52 or 51 BC, Octavius delivered her funeral oration, his first public appearance. A Greek [slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_ancient_Rome "Slavery in ancient Rome") tutor named Sphaerus [educated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_ancient_Rome "Education in ancient Rome") him in reading, writing, arithmetic, and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek"). Octavius later freed Sphaerus and gave him a state funeral in 40 BC. As a teenager, he studied [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_philosophy "Ancient philosophy") under [Areios of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areios_Didymos "Areios Didymos") and [Athenodorus of Tarsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenodorus_of_Tarsus "Athenodorus of Tarsus"), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin "Classical Latin") rhetoric under [Marcus Epidius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Epidius "Marcus Epidius"), and Greek rhetoric under [Apollodorus of Pergamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollodorus_of_Pergamon "Apollodorus of Pergamon").

Julius Caesar had formed [an informal alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Triumvirate "First Triumvirate") with [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey "Pompey") and [Crassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crassus "Crassus") in 60 BC, but by 49 BC it had fallen apart and Pompey and Caesar were fighting a protracted [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar%27s_Civil_War "Caesar's Civil War"). In 47 BC, after Octavius donned the *[toga virilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toga_virilis "Toga virilis")* and became an adult citizen, Caesar had him elected as [pontiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiff "Pontiff"), replacing the slain [Lucius Ahenobarbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Domitius_Ahenobarbus_(consul_54_BC) "Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus (consul 54 BC)"). The following year, Octavius presided over the [Greek games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_games "Greek games") commemorating the opening of Caesar's [Temple of Venus Genetrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Venus_Genetrix "Temple of Venus Genetrix"). He wished to join Caesar's staff for the [African campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thapsus "Battle of Thapsus") but gave way when his mother Atia protested over his poor health. Caesar allowed Octavius to proceed next to his chariot during his [triumph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_triumph "Roman triumph") celebrating the campaign and awarded Octavius with military decorations as if he had been present. In 45 BC Octavius traveled to [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania "Hispania") to join [Caesar's Spanish campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar%27s_Spanish_campaign "Caesar's Spanish campaign") against [Pompey the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey_the_Younger "Pompey the Younger"). On 13 September 45 BC Caesar deposited a new will with the [Vestal Virgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestal_Virgin "Vestal Virgin") naming Octavius as his principal heir.

## Rise to power

### Heir to Caesar

In 44 BC, Octavius was at [Apollonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonia_(Illyria) "Apollonia (Illyria)"), [Illyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria "Illyria"), when Julius Caesar was made Rome's first *[dictator perpetuo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictator_perpetuo "Dictator perpetuo")* ('dictator in perpetuity') in February, and then [assassinated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Julius_Caesar "Assassination of Julius Caesar") on the [Ides of March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ides_of_March "Ides of March") (15 March). Octavius consulted with Caesar's officers in Macedonia before sailing for [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Italy "Roman Italy") to ascertain his political fortunes. Caesar had no living legitimate children under [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law "Roman law"). His will made Octavius his main heir with the condition that he assume the dead [dictator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_dictator "Roman dictator")'s name. After landing near [Brundisium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brundisium "Brundisium") in southern Italy, Octavius received a copy of the will, which bequeathed him three-quarters of Caesar's estate. Against the advice of his stepfather Philippus, Octavius [accepted Caesar's will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_of_Augustus "Rise of Augustus") on 8 May 44 BC. He purported that Caesar [adopted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_in_ancient_Rome "Adoption in ancient Rome") him as his son and assumed the name Gaius Julius Caesar. His stepfather, [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero "Cicero"), and other contemporaries referred to him as *Octavianus*.

Octavian could not rely on his limited funds to make a successful entry into politics. After a warm welcome by Caesar's soldiers at Brundisium, he demanded a portion of the funds allotted by Caesar for his eastern war against the [Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthians "Parthians"). This amounted to 700 million [sesterces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesterces "Sesterces") stored at Brundisium, the staging ground in Italy for military operations in the east. Octavian made another bold move when, without official permission, he appropriated the annual tribute from Rome's [province of Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Asia "Province of Asia") to Italy. He also began to recruit Caesar's veterans and men designated for the Parthian war. On his march to Rome through Italy, Octavian's presence and newly acquired riches won over many, including Caesar's veterans stationed in [Campania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campania "Campania"). By June, he had gathered an army of 3,000 men, paying each a bonus of 500 *[denarii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denarii "Denarii")*, which was more than twice a soldier's annual pay.

### Growing tensions

Arriving in Rome on 6 May 44 BC, Octavian found consul [Mark Antony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Antony "Mark Antony"), Caesar's former colleague, in an uneasy truce with Caesar's assassins. A general amnesty on 17 March pardoned the assassins in exchange for recognition of Caesar's legal acts. Soon afterwards, Antony succeeded in driving most of them out of Rome with an inflammatory eulogy at Caesar's funeral, mounting public opinion against the assassins.

Mark Antony amassed political support, but had lost the support of many Romans and Caesarians when he opposed the motion to elevate Caesar to divine status. Octavian challenged him as the leader of the Caesarians. To halt Octavian from dispersing 300 sesterces [per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita "Per capita") to the urban plebs in accordance with Caesar's will, Antony refused to give Octavian the money due him as Caesar's heir. He also blocked the [curiate assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curiate_assembly "Curiate assembly") from hearing Octavian's attempts to legitimize his supposed adoption by Caesar, to have Caesar [formally deified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formally_deified "Formally deified") and to reinstate Caesar's golden throne for public view at [games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludi_Romani "Ludi Romani") staged in April and June. During Caesar's victory games, Octavian distributed some of the funds in Caesar's will and combined this with his own money, enhancing his popularity while damaging Antony's.

During the summer of 44 BC, Octavian won the support of more veterans and also senators who perceived Antony as a threat to the state. Antony had [lictors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lictor "Lictor") drag Octavian away from a hearing over the reinstatement of private property seized by Caesar in 49 BC. Octavian then claimed Antony threatened his life as retribution for distributing money to the plebs in Caesar's will. Caesar's veterans convinced Antony to publicly reconcile with Octavian in the [Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Jupiter_Optimus_Maximus "Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus"). Thereafter, Antony's bellicose edicts against the assassins [Marcus Junius Brutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Junius_Brutus "Marcus Junius Brutus") and [Gaius Cassius Longinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Cassius_Longinus "Gaius Cassius Longinus") alienated him from the moderate Caesarian senators, who feared a renewed civil war. In September, [Marcus Tullius Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Tullius_Cicero "Marcus Tullius Cicero"), now a political ally of Octavian, began to give a [series of speeches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippicae "Philippicae") portraying Antony as a [threat to the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_Roman_Republic "Crisis of the Roman Republic").

### First conflict with Antony

With opinion in Rome turning against him and his consulship concluding, Antony illegally passed a law that would assign him the [province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province "Roman province") of [Cisalpine Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisalpine_Gaul "Cisalpine Gaul") in northern Italy. Octavian meanwhile built up a private army in Italy by recruiting Caesarian veterans, and in early November entered Rome with this private force to challenge Antony. However, they vacated the city shortly afterwards, due to some veterans choosing to quit once it became clear they were involved in a Caesarian squabble rather than a revenge campaign against Caesar's assassins. Nevertheless, on 28 November, Octavian won over two of Antony's legions with the enticing offer of monetary gain. Antony then left Rome for Cisalpine Gaul, which was to be handed to him on 1 January 43 BC. However, the province had earlier been assigned to the assassin [Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimus_Junius_Brutus_Albinus "Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus"), who now refused to yield to Antony. Antony [besieged him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Mutina "War of Mutina") at [Mutina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutina "Mutina"). This provided an opportunity for Octavian, whose private army was at hand.

Cicero defended Octavian against Antony's taunts, and had him inducted as a senator on 1 January 43 BC. Octavian was given the power to vote alongside the former consuls, the privilege to stand for election at an earlier age than usual, and *imperium [pro praetore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promagistrate "Promagistrate")* which legitimized his command. Octavian accompanied the consuls to relieve the siege of Mutina. He assumed the [fasces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasces "Fasces") on 7 January, a date that he would later commemorate as the beginning of his public career. Antony retreated to [Transalpine Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transalpine_Gaul "Transalpine Gaul") after his forces were defeated at the battles of [Forum Gallorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Forum_Gallorum "Battle of Forum Gallorum") and [Mutina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mutina "Battle of Mutina") in April. Both consuls were killed, however, leaving Octavian in sole command of their armies. These victories earned him his first acclamation as *[imperator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperator "Imperator")*, a title reserved for victorious commanders.

Largely ignoring Octavian, the Senate heaped many rewards on Decimus Brutus and attempted to give him command of the consular legions. In response, Octavian stayed in the [Po Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po_Valley "Po Valley") and refused to pursue Antony. In July, an embassy of [centurions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centurion "Centurion") sent by Octavian entered Rome; they demanded the now-vacant consulship for Octavian, with Cicero as co-consul, and the rescission of the decree declaring Antony a public enemy. When this was refused, Octavian marched on Rome, where he encountered no military opposition. On 19 August 43 BC, aged 19, he became consul alongside his relative [Quintus Pedius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Pedius "Quintus Pedius"). Pedius [passed legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Pedia "Lex Pedia") creating a special tribunal for Caesar's assassins and their alleged associates; Octavian presided over the trial and had them convicted and exiled in absentia. Octavian also induced the curiate assembly to have him [adrogated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrogate "Adrogate") into Caesar's family, legitimizing his claim of testamentary adoption. Meanwhile, Antony formed an alliance with [Marcus Aemilius Lepidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aemilius_Lepidus "Marcus Aemilius Lepidus"), then governor of [Gallia Narbonensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallia_Narbonensis "Gallia Narbonensis"). The Senate branded Lepidus, a fellow Caesarian, as a public enemy for joining Antony, but they reversed the pair's outlawing at Pedius's behest while Octavian marched north to fight Decimus Brutus and meet with Antony.

### Second Triumvirate

In a meeting near [Bononia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bologna "Bologna") in October 43 BC, Octavian joined with Antony and Lepidus to form [the triumvirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Triumvirate "Second Triumvirate"), ostensibly for the stability of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic "Roman Republic"), and on 27 November the *[lex Titia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Titia "Lex Titia")* legitimized their agreement for five years. The [triumvirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumvirate_(ancient_Rome) "Triumvirate (ancient Rome)") gave the men consular power, the right to appoint magistrates, and allowed their division among themselves of the provinces not under the control of the *liberatores* in the east. Octavian had previously been engaged to [Servilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servilia_(wife_of_Lepidus) "Servilia (wife of Lepidus)"), daughter of [Servilius Isauricus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servilius_Isauricus "Servilius Isauricus"), but instead became engaged to [Claudia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudia_(wife_of_Octavian) "Claudia (wife of Octavian)"), stepdaughter of Antony, to solidify their political union. Octavian also relinquished the consulship to Antony's ally [Publius Ventidius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publius_Ventidius "Publius Ventidius").

#### Proscriptions

The triumvirs then set in motion [proscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proscription_of_the_Second_Triumvirate "Proscription of the Second Triumvirate"), targeting some 300 men [as outlaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enemy_of_the_people "Enemy of the people"), divided roughly evenly between senators and equestrians. Thousands more had their properties confiscated. Contemporary Roman historians provide conflicting reports as to which triumvir was most responsible for the [proscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proscription_in_Ancient_Rome "Proscription in Ancient Rome") and killing. However, the sources agree that the proscriptions enabled all three to eliminate political enemies.

The triumvirs initiated the proscriptions partly to raise money to pay the salaries of their troops for the upcoming conflict against Caesar's assassins Marcus Brutus and Gaius Cassius, but the main intention was the removal of wartime rivals. The triumvirs seized the proscripts' property. However much money was raised was insufficient, so the triumvirs introduced a range of new taxes to fund their war. They reinstituted [property taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributum "Tributum") and created new imposts on slaves, before also demanding property assessments for taxes on rich women that were reduced after a public protest of women in Rome.

#### Battle of Philippi and division of territory

On 1 January 42 BC, with Lepidus as consul, the Senate posthumously recognized Julius Caesar as a divinity of the Roman state, *[divus Iulius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divus_Iulius "Divus Iulius")*. Octavian was able to further his cause by emphasizing that he was *divi filius* ('son of the divine'). Antony and Octavian then led twenty-eight legions east against Brutus and Cassius, whom they defeated after two [battles at Philippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battles_at_Philippi "Battles at Philippi") in Macedonia in October 42 BC. Brutus and Cassius both died by suicide. Claiming responsibility for both victories, Antony branded Octavian a coward for handing over his direct military control to [Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Vipsanius_Agrippa "Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa"). Octavian was bedridden with illness during the first battle, allegedly removing himself from command over the camp per his doctor's advice, but captured Brutus's camp during the second battle.

After Philippi, the triumvirs again divided the provinces. Lepidus was suspected of colluding with [Sextus Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextus_Pompey "Sextus Pompey"), the renegade general whom the anti-Caesarian Senate had given [command over all Mediterranean coastlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praefectus_classis "Praefectus classis") in 43 BC. Cisalpine Gaul was combined with [Italia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italia_(Roman_province) "Italia (Roman province)") and given to Octavian along with the provinces of [Hispania Citerior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania_Citerior "Hispania Citerior") and [Hispania Ulterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania_Ulterior "Hispania Ulterior") that Lepidus had to forfeit. Antony travelled east to [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt "Ptolemaic Egypt") where he allied himself with [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra"), a Roman client ruler, former lover of Julius Caesar, and mother of Caesar's son [Caesarion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesarion "Caesarion"). In addition to the eastern provinces, Antony controlled [Gallia Comata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallia_Comata "Gallia Comata") and took Gallia Narbonensis from Lepidus, who was left with the [province of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Africa "Province of Africa").

Octavian was left to settle tens of thousands of discharged veterans in Italy. Those who fought for the assassins also required settlement for their pacification. With no remaining public land, Octavian chose to confiscate land from citizens, instead of alienating the soldiers who could mount a real threat to the regime in Italy. The settlements affected some eighteen cities, with entire populations fully or partially evicted.

#### Perusine War, marriage alliances, and Brundisium

These veteran settlements brought Octavian widespread dissatisfaction. The disaffected rallied to Mark Antony's brother [Lucius Antonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Antonius_(brother_of_Mark_Antony) "Lucius Antonius (brother of Mark Antony)"), who was supported by a majority in the Senate. Meanwhile, Octavian asked for a divorce from Claudia, Antony's stepdaughter. He returned Claudia to her mother, [Fulvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulvia "Fulvia"), claiming that their marriage had never been consummated. Fulvia and Lucius Antonius then raised an army in Italy to fight for Antony's rights against Octavian. Lucius even briefly took Rome, forcing Lepidus and his two legions to flee the city. However, the Roman army still depended on the triumvirs for their salaries. Lucius and his allies ended up in a defensive siege at [Perusia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perusia "Perusia"), where Octavian [forced their surrender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perusine_War "Perusine War") in February 40 BC. Octavian spared Lucius, while Fulvia fled to [Sicyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicyon "Sicyon") in Greece and died shortly afterwards. On 15 March, the anniversary of Julius Caesar's assassination, Octavian had 300 Roman senators and equestrians executed for allying with Lucius. Perusia was also sacked, though it is unclear who started the fires. These reprisals sullied Octavian's reputation.

Sextus Pompey affirmed his control of [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily "Sicily") as part of an agreement with the triumvirate in 40 BC, and gained control of [Sardinia and Corsica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinia_and_Corsica "Sardinia and Corsica") in 39. Both Antony and Octavian sought an alliance with him. Octavian established a temporary alliance in 40 BC when he married [Scribonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribonia_(wife_of_Octavian) "Scribonia (wife of Octavian)"), an aunt of Sextus's wife. A year later, Scribonia gave birth to Octavian's only surviving child, [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_the_Elder "Julia the Elder"), on the same day that he divorced her to marry [Livia Drusilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livia_Drusilla "Livia Drusilla"). When Livia began her affair with Octavian, she was already married to [Tiberius Claudius Nero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius_Claudius_Nero_(father_of_Tiberius_Caesar) "Tiberius Claudius Nero (father of Tiberius Caesar)"), had a son [Tiberius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius "Tiberius") with Nero, and was pregnant with their second child. She gave birth to her second son, [Drusus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero_Claudius_Drusus "Nero Claudius Drusus"), several months after her divorce from Nero and marriage to Octavian. Livia later had another child with Octavian, who was born [prematurely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preterm_birth "Preterm birth") and did not survive.

While in Egypt, Antony had engaged in an affair with Cleopatra and had fathered three children with her. Antony's Gallic provinces fell into Octavian's hands after the death of Antony's legate [Quintus Calenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Calenus "Quintus Calenus") in 40 BC. Aware of his deteriorating relationship with Octavian, Antony left Cleopatra; he sailed to Italy in 40 BC with a large force to oppose Octavian, laying siege to Brundisium, but the men's revolting armies forced them to reconcile. In late 40, the triumvirs divided the empire between Antony in the east, Octavian in the west, and Lepidus in Africa. Now in a stronger position due to the [Parthian threat in Antony's provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_invasion_of_40_BC "Parthian invasion of 40 BC"), Octavian gave his sister, [Octavia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavia_Minor "Octavia Minor"), in marriage to Antony.

#### War with Sextus Pompey and exile of Lepidus

Before the battles of Philippi, Octavian had sent [Salvidienus Rufus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvidienus_Rufus "Salvidienus Rufus") to remove Sextus Pompey from Sicily, but after Rufus's defeat, the triumvirs recognized Sextus's Mediterranean command at Brundisium in 40 BC. When Sextus resumed his blockade, a starving angry mob in Rome blamed Octavian and Antony and attacked them in early 39 BC; Antony's forces rescued Octavian and dispersed the mob. Another temporary peace agreement was reached in 39 BC at [Misenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pact_of_Misenum "Pact of Misenum"). Sextus lifted the blockade on Italy once Octavian granted him [Sardinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinia "Sardinia"), [Corsica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsica "Corsica"), [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Sicily "Roman Sicily"), and the [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese "Peloponnese") and ensured him a future position in the consulship.

The agreement between the triumvirate and Sextus began to crumble when Octavian divorced Scribonia and married Livia in 38 BC. After Antony refused to relinquish the Peloponnese, Sextus reimposed his blockade, starting food riots at Rome. Sextus's naval commander [Menas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menas_(freedman) "Menas (freedman)") defected, handing over Corsica and Sardinia. However, after Octavian's naval forces were defeated at [Cumae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumae "Cumae"), Octavian lacked the resources to confront Sextus alone, so he sought Antony's help, extending their terms for another five-year period beginning in 37 BC.

In supporting Octavian, Antony expected to gain support for his own campaign against the Parthians. At [Tarentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranto "Taranto") in mid-37 BC, Antony provided 120 ships for Octavian to use against Sextus, while Octavian was to send 20,000 legionaries to Antony for use [against Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony%27s_Atropatene_campaign "Antony's Atropatene campaign"). Two years later Octavian sent only a tenth of those promised, which Antony viewed as a provocation. Meanwhile, Octavian tasked Agrippa with creating the artificial harbor [Portus Julius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portus_Julius "Portus Julius") for the training and shipbuilding of [Octavian's naval fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classis_Misenensis "Classis Misenensis").

Octavian and Lepidus launched a joint operation against Sextus in Sicily in 36 BC. Octavian was [shipwrecked in Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Taormina "History of Taormina"), but Agrippa defeated Sextus [at Mylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mylae_(36_BC) "Battle of Mylae (36 BC)") in August before almost destroying Sextus's forces at [Naulochus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Naulochus "Battle of Naulochus") in September. Sextus fled to the east, but Antony had him executed at [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus "Miletus") in 35 BC.

As Lepidus and Octavian accepted the surrender of Sextus's troops, Lepidus attempted to claim Sicily for himself. However, Lepidus's troops deserted him after Octavian bribed them. Octavian forced Lepidus into retirement but allowed him to remain *[pontifex maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifex_maximus "Pontifex maximus")* ('supreme pontiff'). Octavian protected Roman citizens' rights to property, settled his discharged soldiers outside Italy, and returned 30,000 slaves to their former Roman owners after they had fled to join Sextus's army and navy. To ensure his family's safety once he returned to Rome, he had the Senate grant him, his wife, and his sister [tribunician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribune_of_the_plebs "Tribune of the plebs") immunity, or *[sacrosanctitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrosanctitas "Sacrosanctitas")*.

After defeating Sextus, [Octavian campaigned in Illyricum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavian_campaigned_in_Illyricum "Octavian campaigned in Illyricum") (in what is now Croatia). During the first campaign in 35 BC he destroyed Segesta (modern [Siscia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siscia "Siscia")) and was wounded by a collapsing [siege ramp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_military_engineering "Roman military engineering") when he besieged Metulum (along the [Kolpa River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolpa_River "Kolpa River")). The Senate lauded these efforts, though Octavian postponed a triumph for his victories, and only later acknowledged the contributions of commanders Agrippa and [Statilius Taurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statilius_Taurus "Statilius Taurus").

#### War with Antony and Cleopatra

In 36 BC, Octavian declared the civil wars at an end and suggested that he and Antony resign as triumvirs; Antony refused. [Antony's Parthian campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony%27s_Parthian_campaign "Antony's Parthian campaign") in 36 BC turned into a debacle and ruined his reputation. The mere 2,000 legionaries sent by Octavian to Antony, traveling with his wife Octavia, were hardly enough to replenish his lost forces. On the other hand, Cleopatra, with her enormous wealth, could restore his army to full strength. Her and Antony's third child, [Ptolemy Philadelphus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_Philadelphus_(son_of_Cleopatra) "Ptolemy Philadelphus (son of Cleopatra)"), was born in 36 BC, so in 35 BC Antony decided to send Octavia back to Rome. Octavian attacked Antony for rejecting his Roman spouse for a foreign queen. He also sought to convince the Senate that Antony had ambitions to diminish the preeminence of Rome. When Octavian assumed the consulship of 33 BC, he opened the Senate with a vehement attack on Antony's grants of titles and territories to his relatives and Cleopatra, later known as the [Donations of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donations_of_Alexandria "Donations of Alexandria").

In early 32 BC, amid an intense [war of propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_propaganda "History of propaganda") with Octavian, Antony divorced Octavia. The new consuls [Gaius Sosius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Sosius "Gaius Sosius") and [Gnaeus Ahenobarbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnaeus_Domitius_Ahenobarbus_(consul_32_BC) "Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus (consul 32 BC)") supported Antony and threatened to revoke Octavian's triumviral authority. This prompted Octavian to enter the [Senate house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curia_Julia "Curia Julia") and denounce Antony and Sosius; both consuls and many senators then fled Rome for Antony. However, two of Antony's key supporters, [Munatius Plancus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munatius_Plancus "Munatius Plancus") and [Marcus Titius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Titius "Marcus Titius"), defected to Octavian in autumn. They offered him vital information about Antony's will, which Antony published after marching on the [Temple of Vesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Vesta "Temple of Vesta"). The will would have given away Roman-conquered territories as kingdoms for his sons to rule and designated [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria") as the site of [a tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Antony_and_Cleopatra "Tomb of Antony and Cleopatra") for him and Cleopatra.

In late 32 BC, the Senate revoked Antony's upcoming consulship and [declared war on Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Actium "War of Actium"). Octavian used [emergency powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emergency_decrees "Roman emergency decrees") to have men of military age throughout the Republic swear an oath of loyalty to him. In early 31 BC, as Antony and Cleopatra moved to Greece, Octavian's forces under Agrippa transited the Adriatic Sea, and cut off their main force from their supply routes in the [Ionian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Sea "Ionian Sea"). Octavian then landed in [Epirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Epirus "Ancient Epirus"), and proceeded to march south. Trapped on land and sea, Antony's men started to desert as Octavian prepared for battle.

Antony's fleet sailed through the bay of [Actium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actium "Actium") along the [Ambracian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambracian_Gulf "Ambracian Gulf") of western Greece to break the [blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade "Blockade"). There, they fought the [Battle of Actium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Actium "Battle of Actium") on 2 September 31 BC. Cleopatra and her portion of the fleet withdrew early in the battle and Antony later joined them; Cleopatra's fleet spared Antony's remaining forces in a last-ditch effort. Antony's nearby forces on land surrendered to Octavian after attempting a retreat through Macedonia. Various [client kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client_kingdoms_in_ancient_Rome "Client kingdoms in ancient Rome") now defected to Octavian. Octavian would later establish a new city—[Nicopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicopolis "Nicopolis") ('victory city')—near the site of the battle at Actium.

On 1 August 30 BC, Octavian [defeated Antony at Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Alexandria_(30_BC) "Battle of Alexandria (30 BC)"); Antony then died by suicide. After meeting with Octavian and refusing to be paraded in a triumph at Rome, [Cleopatra took her own life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra_took_her_own_life "Cleopatra took her own life") with poison. Well aware of the dangers presented by another potential heir to Caesar, Octavian ordered the death of Cleopatra's son Caesarion. He also had Antony's son [Marcus Antonius Antyllus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Antonius_Antyllus "Marcus Antonius Antyllus") killed, but spared their other children and pardoned many of his opponents. Octavian had previously shown little mercy to surrendered enemies. He also ensured that Cleopatra was buried with Antony in their tomb. He appointed Cleopatra's daughter [Cleopatra Selene II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra_Selene_II "Cleopatra Selene II") and her husband, [Juba II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juba_II "Juba II") of [Numidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numidia "Numidia"), as the new co-rulers of [Mauretania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauretania "Mauretania") following their marriage in 25 BC.

## Sole ruler of Rome

### Control of Egypt

The [conquest of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Egypt "Roman conquest of Egypt") greatly relieved Octavian's debts incurred from the civil wars. He controlled [Roman Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt "Roman Egypt") directly, forbade senators to travel there, and appointed [equestrian governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Roman_Egypt "List of governors of Roman Egypt") [Cornelius Gallus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Gallus "Cornelius Gallus") to supervise its administration and enormously [lucrative taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_in_ancient_Rome "Taxation in ancient Rome"). While in Alexandria in 30 BC, Octavian visited [the tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Alexander_the_Great "Tomb of Alexander the Great") of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great"), the conqueror he emulated and imitated in his own [artistic portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_portraiture "Roman portraiture"). Octavian's conquest of Egypt brought an end to the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period "Hellenistic period"); it also cemented the cultural formation of a [Greek East and Latin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_East_and_Latin_West "Greek East and Latin West") in the Mediterranean and a cosmopolitan [universal monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_monarchy "Universal monarchy") centered on Rome.

Octavian would become [the first Roman emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Roman_emperors "List of Roman emperors") as [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Augustus "Reign of Augustus") and also the first [Roman pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_pharaoh "Roman pharaoh") of Egypt, though he did not partake in [Egyptian coronation rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_coronation_rites "Egyptian coronation rites") or worship of the [Apis bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apis_bull "Apis bull"), and he never visited Egypt again after 30 BC. Before returning to Rome, Octavian wintered in 30 BC on the Greek island of [Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samos "Samos"). In August the following year, he celebrated three triumphs in Rome for his victories in Illyria, Greece, and Egypt. He and Agrippa were elected as the consuls for 28 BC, and granted the powers of a [censor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censor_(Rome) "Censor (Rome)") so as to conduct the [census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_census "Roman census").

### Principate

After defeating Antony and Cleopatra, Octavian could rule the entire Republic under an unofficial [principate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principate "Principate"), with himself as *[princeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeps "Princeps")* ('leading citizen' or 'first citizen'). He achieved this incrementally by courting the [Senate and people of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_and_people_of_Rome "Senate and people of Rome") while purporting not to aspire to dictatorship or monarchy. Influential aristocrats were previously called *princeps* and Octavian would embrace this title as part of his self-representation as restorer of the Republic.

Years of civil war had left Rome in a state of near lawlessness, but republican tradition opposed autocracy. At the same time, Octavian could not give up his authority without risking war. The Senate and people desired a return to stability, traditional legality, civility, and the assurance of [free elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_the_Roman_Republic "Elections in the Roman Republic")—which would be conducted in name at least under Octavian, soon to be *princeps* Augustus. The gradual fashioning of this regime involved trial by error and experimentation, popular support for legally sanctioned moves, and appointed term limits for offices in perhaps a cautious attempt to avoid the same fate as his adoptive father Julius Caesar.

### First settlement

#### Control of provinces

On 13 January 27 BC, Octavian made a show of returning power to the Senate and relinquishing his provinces and armies. However, he retained the loyalty of serving soldiers and veterans. The careers of many clients and adherents [depended on his patronage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patronage_in_ancient_Rome "Patronage in ancient Rome"), as his financial power was unrivaled. Other senators refrained from spending to build and maintain roads in Italy in 20 BC, but Octavian undertook direct responsibility on behalf of the public. The Roman currency issued in 16 BC publicized Octavian's involvement, after he donated vast amounts of money to the *[aerarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerarium "Aerarium") Saturni*, the public treasury.

In an agreement known as the first settlement, Octavian was able to continue the appearance of a still-functional [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Roman_Republic "Constitution of the Roman Republic") through the Senate proposing to him that he once again assume control of the provinces. Feigning reluctance, on 16 January 27 BC he accepted a ten-year responsibility of overseeing provinces that were considered chaotic. The provinces ceded to him constituted much of the Roman world, including Hispania, [Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Gaul "Roman Gaul"), [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Syria "Roman Syria"), [Cilicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia "Cilicia"), Cyprus, and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86gyptus "Ægyptus"). Moreover, command of these provinces provided him control over the majority of Rome's legions. Octavian–Augustus's power ultimately rested in his control over Rome's military.

However, Octavian–Augustus did not have a monopoly on political and martial power. The Senate still controlled the [grain-producing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_ancient_Rome "Agriculture in ancient Rome") North Africa as well as the militarily strategic Illyria and Macedonia. However, the Senate had control of only five or six legions distributed among three senatorial proconsuls, compared to the twenty legions under Augustus's control, and their control of these regions did not significantly challenge his authority. [Divided control between senators and proconsuls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senatorial_provinces "Senatorial provinces") had precedent, and Augustus used republican legal frameworks to amass power.

While Augustus acted as consul in Rome, he dispatched senators to the provinces under his command as his representatives to manage provincial affairs. The Senate chose governors to oversee the remaining provinces. Augustus issued instructions and edicts not only to [his own legates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legatus_Augusti_pro_praetore "Legatus Augusti pro praetore") but also to independent proconsuls governing public provinces that were nominally under senatorial control. Augustus's control of entire provinces followed Republican-era precedents for the limited objective of securing peace and creating stability, with Pompey having been given a [similar level of command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Gabinia_de_piratis_persequendis "Lex Gabinia de piratis persequendis") across the [Roman world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_world "Roman world").

#### Title of Augustus

On 16 January 27 BC the Senate gave Octavian the new title of *[augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_(title) "Augustus (title)")* ('revered'). It was a title of religious rather than political authority, and it indicated that Octavian now approached divinity. Future [Roman emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperor "Roman emperor") inherited the honorific *augustus*, and it became their main title. Another titular option, that of *[Romulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus "Romulus")*, after the legendary founder of Rome, was associated too strongly with [monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_monarchy "Roman monarchy"), an association Octavian sought to avoid. The Senate also confirmed his position as *[princeps senatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeps_senatus "Princeps senatus")* ('leader of the Senate'). Augustus now styled himself as *Imperator Caesar divi filius* ('Commander Caesar son of the deified one'), boasting his familial link to deified Julius Caesar. The use of *[imperator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperator "Imperator")* signified a permanent link to the Roman tradition of victory. Augustus transformed *Caesar*, a cognomen for one branch of the [Julian family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_gens "Julia gens"), into a new imperial family line that began with him.

The Senate allowed Augustus to hang the *[corona civica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corona_civica "Corona civica")* ('civic crown') above his door and to have laurels drape his doorposts. He renounced flaunting insignia of power such as holding a scepter, wearing a [diadem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadem "Diadem"), or wearing the golden crown and purple toga of Julius Caesar. Nonetheless, the Senate awarded him with a golden shield displayed in the meeting hall of the [Curia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curia "Curia"), bearing the inscription *virtus, pietas, clementia, iustitia* ('valor, piety, clemency, and justice'). By the summer of 27 BC he left Rome and traveled to [Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaul "Gaul"). From 26 to 24 BC he governed the Empire from [Tarraco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarraco "Tarraco") in [Roman Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Spain "Roman Spain"), overseeing [military campaigns in the Iberian peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_the_Iberian_Peninsula "Roman conquest of the Iberian Peninsula") until his return to Rome.

### Second settlement

By 23 BC, some of the un-republican implications of the first settlement were becoming apparent. Augustus's continuous consulships drew attention to his de facto dominance of politics and halved the opportunities for others to achieve what was still nominally the preeminent position in the Roman state. His desire to have his nephew [Marcus Claudius Marcellus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcellus_(nephew_of_Augustus) "Marcellus (nephew of Augustus)") eventually assume the principate in his turn also caused problems. To signal reconciliation with pro-republican aristocrats, Augustus appointed the noted republican [Calpurnius Piso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnaeus_Calpurnius_Piso_(consul_23_BC) "Gnaeus Calpurnius Piso (consul 23 BC)"), who had opposed Julius Caesar and supported the assassins, as co-consul in 23 BC, after his choice [Aulus Terentius Varro Murena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulus_Terentius_Varro_Murena "Aulus Terentius Varro Murena") died unexpectedly.

#### Resignation from the consulship

In the late spring Augustus was severely ill, apparently with a [liver disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver_disease "Liver disease"). Expecting his death, he made arrangements ensuring the continuation of the principate while allaying senators' suspicions of his anti-republicanism. He prepared to hand down his signet ring to his friend and general Agrippa but handed to his co-consul Piso all of his official documents, an account of public finances, and authority over troops in the provinces. Augustus's supposedly favored nephew Marcellus came away empty-handed. This surprised many who expected Augustus to name an heir to his position as an unofficial emperor.

Augustus bestowed only properties and possessions to his designated heirs, as an obvious system of institutionalized imperial inheritance would have provoked resistance and hostility among the republican-minded Romans fearful of monarchy. It appears that Augustus did not view the 19-year-old Marcellus as being ready to inherit his political pre-eminence. By giving his signet ring to Agrippa, however, Augustus signaled to the legions that Agrippa was a potential successor who they should obey, [constitutional procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Roman_Empire "Constitution of the Roman Empire") notwithstanding.

The emperor's illness subsided while under the care of his personal physician [Antonius Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonius_Musa "Antonius Musa"), and soon afterwards on 1 July 23 BC Augustus gave up his consulship. He would serve as consul only twice more, in 5 and 2 BC, both times to introduce his grandsons into public life. Augustus's resignation from the consulship allowed him to exercise wider patronage within the senatorial class while allowing other senators a better chance to become consul. However, Augustus desired to retain his consular *[imperium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperium "Imperium")* throughout the empire, leading to another compromise between him and the Senate known as the second settlement.

#### Marcus Primus affair

After Augustus relinquished the annual consulship, he was no longer in an official position to rule the state. However, his dominant position remained unchanged over his 'imperial' provinces where he was still a proconsul. When he annually held the office of consul, he had the power to intervene with the affairs of the other provincial proconsuls appointed by the Senate throughout the empire, when he deemed necessary.

A second problem later arose showing the need for the second settlement in what became known as the "Marcus Primus affair". In late 24 or early 23 BC, charges were brought against Marcus Primus, former proconsular governor of Macedonia, for waging a war without approval of the Senate on the [Odrysian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odrysian "Odrysian") kingdom of [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace "Thrace"), whose king was a Roman ally. [Lucius Licinius Varro Murena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Licinius_Varro_Murena "Lucius Licinius Varro Murena") defended Primus, arguing that Augustus had ordered Primus to attack the client state. Later, Primus testified that the orders came from the recently deceased Marcellus. Such orders suggested that Augustus intended to have his nephew take his place as princeps and establish a monarchy over Rome, and would have been considered a breach of the Senate's prerogatives under the settlement of 27 BC, since Macedonia was a province under senatorial jurisdiction, not Augustus's imperial authority.

The situation was so serious that Augustus appeared at the trial even though he had not been called as a witness. Under oath, he declared that he gave no such order. Murena disbelieved Augustus's testimony and resented his attempt to subvert the trial by using his *[auctoritas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auctoritas "Auctoritas")*. He demanded to know why Augustus appeared at the trial; Augustus replied that he came in the public interest. The jurors found Primus guilty, though some voted to acquit, suggesting that some disbelieved Augustus's claims.

#### Greater proconsular authority

The Senate negotiated the second settlement with Augustus partly to allay confusion and formalize his legal authority to intervene in senatorial provinces, granting him a form of general *imperium proconsulare* ('proconsular power') that applied throughout the empire, not solely to his provinces. Moreover, the Senate augmented Augustus's proconsular imperium into *imperium proconsulare maius* ('greater proconsular power'). This form of proconsular imperium was applicable throughout the empire and in effect [gave Augustus constitutional power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_reforms_of_Augustus "Constitutional reforms of Augustus") superior to all other proconsuls. Augustus stayed in Rome during the renewal process and obtained veterans' support for the renewal of his *proconsular imperium maius* in 13 BC by providing them with lavish donations.

### Additional powers

#### Powers of the tribune

During the second settlement, Augustus was also granted the power of a [tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribune "Tribune") (*tribunicia potestas*) for life, though not the official title of tribune. For some years, Augustus had been awarded *tribunicia sacrosanctitas*, the immunity given to a tribune of the plebs. Now he decided to assume the full powers of the magistracy, renewed annually, in perpetuity. Legally, it was closed to [patricians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricians "Patricians"), a status that Augustus had acquired when adopted by Julius Caesar. This power allowed him to convene the Senate and people at will, lay business before them, and veto their decisions, to preside over elections, and to speak first at any meeting. Augustus's amassing of tribunal powers caused the office of *tribunus plebis* to lose prestige, so he revived its importance by making it a mandatory appointment for any plebeian desiring the praetorship.

#### Powers of the censor

Also included in Augustus's tribunician authority were powers usually reserved for the [Roman censor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_censor "Roman censor"); these included the right to supervise public morals and scrutinize laws to ensure that they were in the public interest, as well as the ability to hold a census and determine the membership of the Senate. There was no precedent within the Roman system for combining the powers of the tribune and the censor, nor was Augustus ever elected to the office of censor. Julius Caesar had been granted similar powers, wherein he was charged with supervising the morals of the state. However, this position did not extend to the censor's ability to hold a census and determine the Senate's roster. Appealing to patriotic sentiments, Augustus is alleged to have used censorial powers to ban [all attire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothing_in_ancient_Rome "Clothing in ancient Rome") except the classic [toga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toga "Toga") for those entering the Forum. However, the powers of the censorship may have only been temporary or even refused by Augustus.

#### Imperium over the city of Rome

The Senate also granted Augustus sole *imperium* within the city of Rome. Traditionally, [proconsuls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proconsul "Proconsul") lost their *imperium* when they crossed the [pomerium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomerium "Pomerium")—the sacred boundary of Rome—and entered the city. In these situations, Augustus held tribunician authority, but the consuls held greater authority. While others would usually obey his wishes owing to his *auctoritas*, there might be some difficulty. In either 23 or 19 BC, the Senate voted that Augustus's *imperium proconsulare maius* ('superior proconsular power') should not lapse when he was inside the city walls. The city's armed forces had formerly been under the control of the urban praetors and consuls, but they now came under the sole authority of Augustus.

#### The Roman triumph

After 19 BC, Augustus received credit for every Roman military victory, because the majority of Rome's armies were stationed in imperial provinces overseen by the *[legati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legati "Legati")*, his provincial deputies. If a battle was fought in a senatorial province, Augustus's proconsular *imperium maius* allowed him to take command and credit for any major victory. With few exceptions Augustus was the only individual who could receive a [triumph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_triumph "Roman triumph"), a tradition that allegedly began with [Romulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus "Romulus"), the legendary first [king of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Rome "King of Rome"). For celebrating his victory against the [Garamantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garamantes "Garamantes") in [Roman Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Libya "Roman Libya") in 19 BC, [Cornelius Balbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Cornelius_Balbus_(proconsul) "Lucius Cornelius Balbus (proconsul)") was the last person outside Augustus's family to receive a triumph. Tiberius, Augustus's eldest stepson, received triumphs in 7 BC and AD 12, respectively for victories in Germania and Illyria ([Pannonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonia "Pannonia")). For the latter campaign, his nephew [Germanicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanicus "Germanicus") instead received the *[ornamenta triumphalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornamenta_triumphalia "Ornamenta triumphalia")* ('triumphal honors'), a praetorship, and the ability to stand for the consulship despite his young age.

#### Diplomacy

Augustus received emissaries from [as far east as India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Roman_relations "Indo-Roman relations"), and his court included political exiles from as far north as the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_Britain "Prehistoric Britain") with the chieftains [Dubnovellaunus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubnovellaunus "Dubnovellaunus") and [Tincomarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tincomarus "Tincomarus"). Foreign embassies typically came to Augustus directly rather than to the Senate, though Augustus was careful to show respect to the Senate in certain cases. For instance, in 20 BC he referred [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia "Parthia") ambassadors to the Senate, but the latter sent them back to Augustus so they could negotiate solely with him instead. As with Roman client states and foreign countries, representatives from [provinces and semi-autonomous municipalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_ancient_Rome "Local government in ancient Rome") travelled to the emperor's court as his administration moved to different locations across the Empire. In AD 8, the elderly Augustus assigned the exhausting work of managing foreign embassies to three ex-consuls, granting them the power to make all decisions that did not require his or the Senate's oversight.

### Conspiracy, titles, and the share of power

Many of the political subtleties of the second settlement seem to have evaded the comprehension of Augustus's plebeian supporters, leading them to insist upon his participation in imperial affairs and form violent mobs on occasion. When Augustus refused to stand for election as consul in 22 BC and traveled to Sicily on another tour of the Empire, the *Comitia centuriata* voted in his absence to have him serve as co-consul for the following year, despite not being one of the candidates. A riot occurred in Rome when only a single consul [Marcus Lollius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Lollius "Marcus Lollius") assumed office on 1 January 21 BC and the factions of the two remaining candidates fought each other. Infuriated, Augustus summoned both candidates to Sicily and settled on having [one of them](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Aemilius_Lepidus "Quintus Aemilius Lepidus") serve as Lollius's co-consul.

A food shortage in Rome during 22 BC sparked widespread panic, as many urban plebs called for Augustus to take on dictatorial powers to personally oversee the crisis. After a theatrical display of refusal before the Senate, Augustus finally accepted authority over Rome's grain supply through the use of his existing proconsular *imperium*, and ended the crisis almost immediately. Another food crisis in AD 8 prompted Augustus to establish a *[praefectus annonae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praefectus_annonae "Praefectus annonae")*, a permanent prefect who was in charge of procuring food supplies for Rome.

Augustus's expansive powers concerned some people, and this came to a head with the apparent conspiracy of Fannius Caepio. Some time prior to 1 September 22 BC, a certain Castricius provided Augustus with information about a conspiracy led by Fannius Caepio. The conspirators, among whom was the consul Murena in the Marcus Primus affair, were tried in absentia with Tiberius acting as prosecutor; the jury found them guilty, but it was not a unanimous verdict. All the accused were executed for treason as soon as they were captured—without ever giving testimony in their defense. Augustus ensured that the façade of republican government continued with an effective cover-up of the events.

In 19 BC, the Senate granted Augustus *imperium consulare maius* proconsular powers in addition to those received in 23 BC, another instance of gaining power from offices he did not hold, now fully applicable to Italy and Rome. To assuage the restless populace, Augustus was allowed to wear the consul's insignia in public and before the Senate, to sit in the symbolic chair between the two consuls, and to hold the magisterial [fasces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasces "Fasces"). On 6 March 12 BC, after the death of [Lepidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepidus "Lepidus"), he assumed the position of *pontifex maximus*, the high priest of the College of Pontiffs. On 5 February 2 BC, the Senate gave Augustus the title *[pater patriae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pater_patriae "Pater patriae")* ('father of the country'), which was then inscribed in various places in Rome such as the [Senate chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curia,_Roman_Forum "Curia, Roman Forum") in the [Forum Romanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_Romanum "Forum Romanum").

Historian [Ronald Syme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Syme "Ronald Syme") wrote that Augustus's death could leave Rome subject to further civil war, given the [public memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_memory "Public memory") of recent wars and Caesar's assassination. Possibly during the 20s BC and certainly by 18 BC, the Senate granted Agrippa proconsular imperium for five years, similar to Augustus's power, in order to accomplish constitutional stability. The grant probably covered Augustus's imperial provinces if not authority over senatorial provinces. Like Augustus, Agrippa was also granted the powers of the tribunate.

### War and expansion

By AD 13, Augustus's troops had proclaimed him *imperator* 21 times following successful battles. The fourth chapter in his publicly released memoirs of achievements known as the *[Res Gestae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Res_Gestae_Divi_Augusti "Res Gestae Divi Augusti")* is devoted to [his military victories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_Augustus "Wars of Augustus") and honors. Augustus also promoted the ideal of a superior Roman civilization, a sentiment the poet [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil "Virgil") attributed to a legendary ancestor of Augustus. All classes at Rome apparently sought [expansionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansionism "Expansionism"), and this impulse is accorded divine sanction in Virgil's *[Aeneid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid "Aeneid")*, in which [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology) "Jupiter (mythology)") promises Rome *imperium sine fine* ('sovereignty without end').

By the end of Augustus's reign, his armies had conquered northern Hispania (modern Spain and Portugal) and the [Alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alps "Alps") regions of [Raetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raetia "Raetia") and [Noricum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noricum "Noricum") (modern Switzerland, Bavaria, Austria, Slovenia), [Illyricum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyricum_(Roman_province) "Illyricum (Roman province)") and Pannonia (modern Albania, Croatia, Hungary, Serbia, etc.), and had extended the borders of [Africa Proconsularis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_Proconsularis "Africa Proconsularis") to the east and south. [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaea_(Roman_province) "Judaea (Roman province)") was added to the [province of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Syria "Province of Syria") when Augustus deposed the [client king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client_king "Client king") [Herod Archelaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Archelaus "Herod Archelaus"). After the Senate assigned Syria to Augustus in 27 BC, it was governed first by legates under Agrippa and then by an equestrian high prefect. In AD 6 Augustus also appointed an equestrian governor in [Sardinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Sardinia "Roman Sardinia") after [pirate raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Mediterranean_piracy "Ancient Mediterranean piracy") necessitated the presence of troops stationed there.

In 25 BC the Romans made [Galatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galatia "Galatia") (part of modern Turkey) a province without any military effort after the murder of its king, [Amyntas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyntas_of_Galatia "Amyntas of Galatia"), while in 19 BC Agrippa [incorporated Asturias and Cantabria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantabrian_Wars "Cantabrian Wars") in modern-day Spain under the provinces of Hispania and [Lusitania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusitania "Lusitania"). This region proved to be a major asset in funding Augustus's future military campaigns, as it was rich in mineral deposits that could be fostered in Roman mining projects. [Conquering the peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Rhetia_and_the_Alps "Roman conquest of Rhetia and the Alps") of the [Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alps "Alps") in 15 BC after the [disastrous defeat of Lollius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disastrous_defeat_of_Lollius "Disastrous defeat of Lollius") was another important victory for Rome, since it provided a large territorial buffer between the Roman citizens of Italy and Rome's enemies in [Germania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germania "Germania") to the north. [Horace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace "Horace") dedicated an ode to the victory, while the monumental [Trophy of Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trophy_of_Augustus "Trophy of Augustus") was built in [La Turbie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Turbie "La Turbie") near Monaco to honor the occasion.

The capture of the Alpine region also served the next offensive in 12 BC, when Augustus's stepsons Tiberius and Drusus launched offensives against the [Pannonian tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_tribes "Pannonian tribes") of Illyricum and [against the Germanic tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_campaigns_in_Germania_(12_BC_%E2%80%93_AD_16) "Roman campaigns in Germania (12 BC – AD 16)") of the eastern [Rhineland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhineland "Rhineland"), respectively. [Both campaigns were successful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drusus%27_Germanic_campaign "Drusus' Germanic campaign"), as Drusus's forces reached the [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe "Elbe") River by 9 BC. Drusus died shortly after from an injury sustained by falling off his horse. Tiberius rushed from Italy to Germany to see his brother and escorted Drusus's body to Rome, where he and Augustus provided eulogies for Drusus. After [Illyrian tribes revolted in Illyricum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrian_tribes_revolted_in_Illyricum "Illyrian tribes revolted in Illyricum") in AD 6, Tiberius and Germanicus's forces quelled their rebellion in AD 9. This was the only major rebellion within Roman provincial territory since Augustus had become emperor, and by this point he had reduced the standing [Roman army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_army "Roman army") from roughly 500,000 soldiers [during the civil wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_army_of_the_late_Republic "Roman army of the late Republic") down to 300,000 soldiers used [primarily for foreign conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Roman_army "Imperial Roman army").

To protect Rome's eastern territories from the [Parthian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire "Parthian Empire"), Augustus relied on eastern client states to act as territorial [buffers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_state "Buffer state") and areas that could raise their own troops for defense. Augustus stationed a Roman army in Syria, while Tiberius [negotiated with the Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman-Iranian_relations "Roman-Iranian relations") as [Rome's diplomat to the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus%27_Eastern_policy "Augustus' Eastern policy"). Tiberius then restored [Tigranes V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigranes_V "Tigranes V") to the [Armenian throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Armenia_(antiquity) "Kingdom of Armenia (antiquity)") in 20 BC, personally placing the crown on his head.

Augustus negotiated with [Phraates IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phraates_IV "Phraates IV") of [Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia "Parthia") in 20 BC for the return of the [battle standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vexilloid "Vexilloid") lost by [Crassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crassus "Crassus") in the [Battle of Carrhae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Carrhae "Battle of Carrhae"), a symbolic victory and great boost of morale for Rome. Historians [Werner Eck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Eck "Werner Eck") and Sarolta Takács claim that this was a great disappointment for Romans seeking to avenge Crassus's defeat by military means. However, Augustus used the return of the standards as [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_in_Augustan_Rome "Propaganda in Augustan Rome") symbolizing the submission of Parthia to Rome. The event was celebrated in art such as the breastplate design on the statue [Augustus of Prima Porta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_of_Prima_Porta "Augustus of Prima Porta") and in monuments such as the [Temple of Mars Ultor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Mars_Ultor "Temple of Mars Ultor") ('[Mars the Avenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_the_Avenger "Mars the Avenger")') built to house the standards. After [Phraates V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phraates_V "Phraates V") of Parthia managed to cleave Armenia away from Roman control, Augustus dispatched his grandson [Gaius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Caesar "Gaius Caesar") with an army to Syria in 1 BC, mounting a diplomatic pressure campaign that in AD 2 convinced Phraates V to concede to Roman demands.

Parthia posed a threat to Rome in [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia "West Asia"), but the more pressing concern was the battlefront along the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine "Rhine") and [Danube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube "Danube") rivers. During the triumvirate, Octavian's campaigns against the tribes in [Dalmatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalmatia_(Roman_province) "Dalmatia (Roman province)") were the first step in expanding Roman dominions to the Danube. Rome's enemies in [Germania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Germany "Roman Germany") almost constantly retook conquered territories. At the [Battle of the Teutoburg Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Teutoburg_Forest "Battle of the Teutoburg Forest") in AD 9, [Arminius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arminius "Arminius"), the leader of the [Cherusci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherusci "Cherusci"), destroyed three entire legions led by [Publius Quinctilius Varus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publius_Quinctilius_Varus "Publius Quinctilius Varus"). Augustus retaliated by dispatching Tiberius to the Rhineland to [pacify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacification_(military_action) "Pacification (military action)") it in AD 10 and AD 11, and these campaigns had some success. However, Augustus advised Tiberius against further conquests after the defeat at Teutoburg, and the Romans abandoned [expansion into Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Germany#Roman_settlement_of_the_Rhine "History of Germany") beyond the Rhine. Although Augustus lamented the loss, his *Res Gestae* merely states that he pacified Germania up to the [mouth of the Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea "North Sea"). Under Augustus's successor Tiberius, Roman general Germanicus took advantage of a Cherusci civil war between Arminius and [Segestes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segestes "Segestes"), defeating Arminius [at Idistaviso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Idistaviso "Battle of Idistaviso") in AD 16.

Rome also experienced loss to the south in [Arabia Felix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia_Felix "Arabia Felix") against the [Kingdom of Saba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Saba "Kingdom of Saba") (in modern [Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen "Yemen")). In 26 BC Augustus had [Gaius Aelius Gallus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Aelius_Gallus "Gaius Aelius Gallus"), prefect of Egypt, invade [South Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Arabia "South Arabia") with Roman troops supported by [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews_in_the_Roman_Empire "Jews in the Roman Empire") and [Nabataean Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_Arab "Nabataean Arab") auxiliaries. They aimed to conquer the [Sabaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabaeans "Sabaeans") or force them to accept client state status so that Rome could gain a share of their profitable [trade with India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Roman_trade_relations "Indo-Roman trade relations"). Roman forces [laid siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Romans_in_Arabia "History of the Romans in Arabia") to [Marib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib "Marib"), but retreated to [Hejaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz "Hejaz") (under allied [Nabataean control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_Kingdom "Nabataean Kingdom")) after a shortage of water supplies. This campaign might have been part of a failed attempt to flank the Parthian Empire, considering how Augustus encouraged [Tiridates II of Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiridates_II_of_Parthia "Tiridates II of Parthia") to invade Mesopotamia and reclaim his throne the same year.

Augustus ordered [Gaius Petronius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Petronius "Gaius Petronius"), Aelius Gallus's successor as prefect of Egypt, to invade [Aethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aethiopia "Aethiopia"), after Queen [Amanirenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanirenas "Amanirenas") of the [Kingdom of Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush") (in modern [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan "Sudan")) invaded Roman Egypt in 24 BC and sacked [Aswan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aswan "Aswan") and [Philae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philae "Philae"). The Romans counterattacked, sacking [Napata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napata "Napata") in [Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia "Nubia") before withdrawing, but Amanirenas invaded Egypt again in 22 BC and threatened [Primis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primis "Primis") (modern [Qasr Ibrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qasr_Ibrim "Qasr Ibrim")). After Petronius withstood a Kushite assault, Amanirenas sent diplomats to [negotiate a peace treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_relations_with_Nubia "Roman relations with Nubia") with Augustus on the island of Samos. The treaty established [Maharraqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharraqa "Maharraqa") as the new border with Kush, lessened the amount of Roman tribute gathered from Kush, and guaranteed peaceful trade relations between Egypt and Nubia. In the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb "Maghreb") of North Africa, [Cossus Cornelius Lentulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossus_Cornelius_Lentulus_Gaetulicus "Cossus Cornelius Lentulus Gaetulicus") quashed a rebellion of the [Gaetuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaetuli "Gaetuli") against Juba II of Mauretania in AD 6.

## Death and succession

Augustus's illness in 23 BC accentuated the problem of succession. To ensure stability, he needed to designate an heir. This needed to be done subtly so that it did not trigger fears of monarchy. If someone was to succeed to Augustus's position of power, he would first have to be recognized as meritful.

### The search for an heir

Some historians argue that Augustus favored his nephew Marcellus, who had married Augustus's daughter Julia, while others argue that he preferred [Marcus Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Agrippa "Marcus Agrippa"), who was Augustus's second in charge and a respected military commander. After Marcellus died in 23 BC, Augustus remarried his daughter Julia to Agrippa in 21 BC. This union produced five children, three sons and two daughters. In 18 BC, Agrippa was granted a five-year appointment to the eastern provinces with proconsular imperium and also the *tribunicia potestas* that Augustus possessed. This grant showed Augustus's favor but upset some conservative senators.

Augustus adopted his grandsons Gaius and [Lucius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Caesar "Lucius Caesar"), illuminating his intent to make them his heirs. He served as consul in 5 and 2 BC so that he could personally usher them into their political careers. Gaius was consul for AD 1, with Augustus having him wait until he turned 21, while Lucius died before his designated consulship. Augustus also showed favor to his stepsons Tiberius and Drusus, granting them public offices while seeming to favor Drusus. Tiberius married Agrippa's eldest daughter, [Vipsania Agrippina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vipsania_Agrippina "Vipsania Agrippina"), while Drusus married Augustus's niece [Antonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonia_Minor "Antonia Minor"). After Agrippa died in 12 BC, Augustus ordered Tiberius to divorce Vipsania for the widowed Julia. Drusus and Antonia's marriage was considered an unbreakable affair, whereas Vipsania was deemed less important. Drusus died in 9 BC.

### Tiberius, heir to Augustus

Tiberius shared in Augustus's tribunician powers from 6 BC but shortly thereafter withdrew into retirement, reportedly wanting no further role in politics. A failing marriage with Julia and envy for her sons Gaius and Lucius could have contributed to Tiberius's departure. Augustus exiled his daughter for adultery in 2 BC, but inducted his grandsons into the college of priests at an early age and introduced them to the army in Gaul.

After the deaths of Lucius in AD 2 and Gaius in AD 4, Augustus recalled Tiberius to Rome in June AD 4 and adopted him, on the condition that Tiberius adopt his nephew Germanicus. This continued the tradition of presenting at least two generations of heirs. In AD 4 Tiberius was also granted the *tribunicia potestas* and a proconsular post in Germany; for his efforts there and in Illyricum, he eventually triumphed. By AD 13, he had received the *imperium maius proconsulare* equalling that of Augustus.

The only other possible claimant was [Agrippa Postumus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrippa_Postumus "Agrippa Postumus"), Augustus's youngest grandson. However, Augustus had exiled him to [Sorrento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorrento "Sorrento") in AD 6 and then to [Planasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planasia "Planasia") in AD 7. The Senate made Agrippa's banishment permanent, and Augustus officially disowned him for his lack of good character and alleged involvement in a conspiracy. After Tiberius succeeded Augustus, he was most likely the one who had Agrippa killed in exile.

### Death of Augustus

On 19 August AD 14, Augustus died at [Nola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nola "Nola"), where his father had died. Both [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus "Tacitus") and Cassius Dio claimed Livia poisoned him. Many historians dismiss the alleged poisoning, however, as a fabrication to discredit her son Tiberius. Livia had long been the target of similar (probably false) rumors of poisoning. If poisoning is accepted, it is possible that Livia supplied a poisoned fig to [assist death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assist_death "Assist death"). Augustus's health had declined in the months before his death and he had prepared for a smooth transition in power to Tiberius.

Augustus's famous last words were, "Have I played the part well? Then applaud as I exit" (*Acta est fabula, plaudite*). An enormous procession of mourners travelled with Augustus's body from Nola to Rome, with all business closed on his funeral. Tiberius and his son [Drusus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drusus_Julius_Caesar "Drusus Julius Caesar") delivered the eulogy while standing atop two *[rostra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rostra "Rostra")*. Augustus's body was coffin-bound and cremated on a pyre close to [his mausoleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum_of_Augustus "Mausoleum of Augustus").

### Deification

On 17 September 27 BC the Senate proclaimed Augustus to have joined the company of the gods and the [deified Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deified_Julius_Caesar "Deified Julius Caesar") as a member of the Roman [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_(religion) "Pantheon (religion)"). People in Rome's eastern provinces had worshipped Octavian as a [living deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_religion "Roman religion") since his victory at Actium. There was limited worship of him in some western provinces, primarily at the [Sanctuary of the Three Gauls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctuary_of_the_Three_Gauls "Sanctuary of the Three Gauls") in [Lugdunum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugdunum "Lugdunum") (Lyon, France) and at [Ara Ubiorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ara_Ubiorum "Ara Ubiorum") in [Oppidum Ubiorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppidum_Ubiorum "Oppidum Ubiorum") (Cologne, Germany), but not at Rome where such worship remained taboo. Only his *[genius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genius_(mythology) "Genius (mythology)")* (spirit) was allowed worship there.

## Legacy

### Overview

Augustus created a regime that maintained relative peace and prosperity in the [Latin West and Greek East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_West_and_Greek_East "Latin West and Greek East") for [two centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_Third_Century "Crisis of the Third Century"), initiating the celebrated *[Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana "Pax Romana")* (or *[Pax Augusta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Augusta "Pax Augusta")*), though the Augustan [golden age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_age "Golden age") myth may obscure the complicated political challenges faced by Augustus. His regime [laid the foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_of_the_Roman_Empire "Legacy of the Roman Empire") of a concept of universal monarchy in the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine "Byzantine") and [Holy Roman Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire "Holy Roman Empire") down to their dissolutions in 1453 and 1806, respectively. Later Romans viewed his reign favorably, embodied by the Senate's formal wish to every emperor after [Trajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajan "Trajan") that they "[be more fortunate than Augustus and better than Trajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Be_more_fortunate_than_Augustus_and_better_than_Trajan "Be more fortunate than Augustus and better than Trajan")". This positive overall image was also helped by his successors copying many of Augustus's policies and forms of self-promotion, which modern research calls *imitatio Augusti*.

The surname Caesar and the title *augustus* became permanent titles of Roman rulers for fourteen centuries after Augustus's death, used in Rome and [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople") following the [Empire's division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_the_Roman_Empire "Division of the Roman Empire"). *Caesar* formed the root of later [regnal titles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regnal_title "Regnal title") such as the German *[kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser "Kaiser")* and Russian *[czar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czar "Czar")*. Emperors preferred his title of *civilis princeps* for three centuries until they adopted the title *[domini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominate "Dominate")* ('lords'), beginning with [Diocletian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocletian "Diocletian"). His adoptive name *Imperator* ('victorious general') served as the etymological root of the word '[emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor "Emperor")', though it did not possess this connotation in Augustus's lifetime. The emperors alone held the office of *pontifex maximus* until [the fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire "Fall of the Western Roman Empire") of the [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire"), after which the [papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papacy "Papacy") adopted it.

### Written works

Augustus composed an account of his achievements, the **[Res Gestae Divi Augusti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Res_Gestae_Divi_Augusti "Res Gestae Divi Augusti")**, to be inscribed in bronze in front of his mausoleum. Copies of the text were inscribed throughout the empire upon his death. The [Latin inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature "Latin literature"), along with Greek translations, were inscribed on many public edifices, and historian [Theodor Mommsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Mommsen "Theodor Mommsen") called them the "queen of inscriptions". The **Res Gestae** is Augustus's only surviving major work, though he is also known to have composed poems entitled "Sicilia", "Epiphanus", and "Ajax", an autobiography of 13 books, a philosophical treatise, and a written rebuttal to Brutus's *Eulogy of [Cato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_the_Younger "Cato the Younger")*. Augustus's private letters also reveal facts about his personal life. The poet [Martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial "Martial") preserved a sexually crude poem allegedly written by Octavian during the Perusine War, which pokes fun at Mark Antony, his wife Fulvia, and his mistress [Glaphyra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaphyra_(hetaera) "Glaphyra (hetaera)"). [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder "Pliny the Elder") suggested that Augustus displayed and finished Agrippa's world map publicly exhibited in the [Porticus Vipsania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porticus_Vipsania "Porticus Vipsania"). This map would later form the basis of various [medieval world maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_world_maps "Medieval world maps").

In his *Res Gestae*, Augustus defined the relative peace established by his reign as a peace "born of victories" (*parta victoriis pax*) in the civil wars, and [Augustan artwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustan_artwork "Augustan artwork") incorporates this theme. This peace ensured Romans and subjugated peoples within their Empire upheld a cohesive social pact: the latter would relinquish their sovereignty and pay taxes in exchange for the preservation of their customs and the protection of Rome. By boasting of his many conquests, the *Res Gestae* emphasizes the same [code of honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dignitas_(Roman_concept) "Dignitas (Roman concept)") found in Republican [funerary inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_funerary_practices "Roman funerary practices") such as [those of the Scipios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_the_Scipios "Tomb of the Scipios"), a key element in elevating the political reputation of Roman families.

### Enduring institutions

Augustus thoroughly transformed the city of Rome, creating a permanent [police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohortes_urbanae "Cohortes urbanae"), firefighting force, and *[praefectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praefectus "Praefectus")* ('municipal prefect'). Established in AD 6 and based on previous firefighting services established in 22 and 7 BC, the *[vigiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigiles "Vigiles")* was a combined fire brigade and [police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_criminal_justice "History of criminal justice") divided into cohorts of 500 to 1,000 men each, with seven units assigned to fourteen divided city sectors. A *[praefectus vigilum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praefectus_vigilum "Praefectus vigilum")* ('prefect of the watch') was put in charge of the *vigiles*, whereas *[vicomagistri](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/vicomagister "wikt:vicomagister")* officials had previously been in charge of each district following the fire of 7 BC. Augustus created a [standing army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Army_during_the_Pax_Romana "Roman Army during the Pax Romana"), fixed at a size of 28 legions of about 170,000 soldiers, reduced from 60 legions at the end of the civil wars in 30 BC. This was supported by many [auxiliary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxilia "Auxilia") units of 500 non-citizen soldiers each, often recruited from recently conquered areas.

With his finances securing the maintenance of [roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads "Roman roads") throughout Italy, Augustus installed an official system of [relay stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursus_publicus "Cursus publicus") overseen by a military officer known as the *praefectus vehiculorum*. Besides speeding communication in Italy, his extensive road construction allowed Roman armies to march swiftly across the country. In AD 6 Augustus established the *[aerarium militare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerarium_militare "Aerarium militare")*, donating 170 million sesterces to the new military treasury that provided for both active and retired soldiers.

One of Augustus's most enduring institutions was the establishment of the [Praetorian Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_Guard "Praetorian Guard") in 27 BC, commanded by two [praetorian prefects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_prefect "Praetorian prefect") (later one) after Augustus created this office in 2 BC. Originally a personal bodyguard unit on the battlefield, the praetorians evolved into an imperial guard as well as an important political force in Rome. They served the emperors into the early 4th century.

### Revenue reforms

[Augustus's tax reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus%27s_tax_reforms "Augustus's tax reforms") greatly impacted the subsequent success of the Empire, bringing it under direct taxation from Rome. This increased and stabilized Rome's revenues from its territories and regularized the financial relationship between Rome and its provinces, avoiding provincial resentments with arbitrary exaction. An equally important reform was the abolition of [tax farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_farming "Tax farming"). The *[publicani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publican "Publican")*, Republican era private tax farmers, were infamous for their depredations and great wealth, so they were replaced by salaried tax collectors.

Under Augustus, the measures of taxation were determined by censuses with fixed quotas for each province. Citizens of Rome and Italy paid [indirect taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_tax "Indirect tax"), while [direct taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_tax "Direct tax") were exacted from the provinces. Indirect taxes included a 4% tax on the price of slaves, a 1% tax on goods sold at auction, and a 5% tax on the inheritance of estates valued at over 100,000 sesterces by persons other than the [next of kin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_of_kin "Next of kin"). Due to protest from equestrians, the suffect consuls for 9 AD modified and lessened penalties in the *[Lex Papia Poppaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Papia_Poppaea "Lex Papia Poppaea")* that affected the inheritance of estates by unmarried or childless individuals, though it continued to generate revenues with properties of the deceased seized by the state.

Augustus's annexation of Egypt allowed him to divert its immense wealth for imperial purposes. Considered Augustus's private property rather than a province, it became part of each succeeding emperor's patrimonium. Instead of a legate or proconsul, Augustus installed a prefect from the equestrian class to administer Egypt and maintain its lucrative seaports. This position became the highest political achievement for any equestrian besides becoming [praetorian prefect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_prefect "Praetorian prefect"). Gold and silver found in the Ptolemaic royal treasury was melted down [for coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_Augustus "Coins of Augustus"). In his will, Augustus left money to his family but also 43 million [sesterces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Imperial_currency "Roman Imperial currency") to the [Roman people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_people "Roman people"), 1,000 sesterces to every praetorian, 500 sesterces to every soldier in urban cohorts, and 300 sesterces to each soldier.

### Month of August

In 8 BC, the [Roman month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_month "Roman month") of [Sextilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextilis "Sextilis") (or *Sextilus*) was renamed August (Latin: *Augustus*) after Augustus. Augustus chose Sextilis as it was the month of his first consulship and of his various victories. In comparison, the month of July (Latin: *Iulius*) in the [Julian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_calendar "Julian calendar") was named after Julius Caesar, the only other month in the [Roman calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_calendar "Roman calendar") named after a Roman.

### Building projects

#### Remodeling of Rome

On his deathbed, Augustus boasted that he converted Rome from a city of bricks into one of [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble "Marble"). Marble could be found in [Roman buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_architecture "Roman architecture") before Augustus, but it was not extensively used as a building material until his reign. He left a mark on the monumental topography of the city's center, as well as on the Campus Martius with the [Ara Pacis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ara_Pacis "Ara Pacis") (Altar of Peace) and monumental [sundial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solarium_Augusti "Solarium Augusti"), whose central [gnomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomon "Gnomon") was an [obelisk taken from Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obelisks_of_Rome "Obelisks of Rome"). The [relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief "Relief") sculptures decorating the Ara Pacis visually augment Augustus's triumphs outlined in the **Res Gestae**. Its reliefs depict [praetorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetor "Praetor"), the Vestals, and the citizenry of Rome.
The [Corinthian order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_order "Corinthian order") of architectural style originating from [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") was the dominant architectural style in the age of Augustus. Suetonius once commented that Rome was unworthy of its status as an imperial capital, yet Augustus and Agrippa set out to dismantle this sentiment. They transformed the appearance of Rome upon the [Greek model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture "Ancient Greek architecture"), incorporating both [Classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece "Classical Greece") and [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Greece "Hellenistic Greece") elements with many [Athenian monuments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Athens "Ancient Athens") as direct inspirations.

Augustus had the temples of [Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Caesar "Temple of Caesar"), [Jupiter Tonans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Jupiter_Tonans "Temple of Jupiter Tonans"), and [Apollo Palatinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Palatinus "Apollo Palatinus") erected, as well as the [Baths of Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baths_of_Agrippa "Baths of Agrippa") and the [Forum of Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_of_Augustus "Forum of Augustus") with its [Temple of Mars Ultor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Mars_Ultor "Temple of Mars Ultor"). He encouraged the establishment of the [Theatre of Balbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Balbus "Theatre of Balbus") and Agrippa's construction of the [Pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon,_Rome "Pantheon, Rome"), and funded additional projects in the name of others, often relations (e.g. [Portico of Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portico_of_Octavia "Portico of Octavia"), [Theatre of Marcellus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Marcellus "Theatre of Marcellus")). Even [his tomb in Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Augustus "Tomb of Augustus") was built before his death to house members of his family. To celebrate his victory at the Battle of Actium, the [Arch of Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_of_Augustus,_Rome "Arch of Augustus, Rome") was built in 29 BC near the entrance of the [Temple of Castor and Pollux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Castor_and_Pollux "Temple of Castor and Pollux"), and widened in 19 BC to include a triple-arch design. He also completed projects left unfinished by Julius Caesar, such as the [Curia Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curia_Julia "Curia Julia"), the [Forum of Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_of_Caesar "Forum of Caesar"), and the [Temple of Venus Genetrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Venus_Genetrix "Temple of Venus Genetrix"). He rebuilt the [Basilica Aemilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_Aemilia "Basilica Aemilia") by 2 BC (previously burned down in a fire of 35 BC).

Augustus also provided grand spectacles in Rome. The [amphitheater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitheater_of_Statilius_Taurus "Amphitheater of Statilius Taurus") constructed by [Statilius Taurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titus_Statilius_Taurus "Titus Statilius Taurus") from 34 to 29 BC was the first [stone amphitheater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_amphitheater "Roman amphitheater") built in the city, and opened with [gladiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gladiator "Gladiator") games around the time Octavian staged shows of live combat and the first ever killing of a rhino and hippopotamus for entertainment in Rome. Augustus staged [lion hunts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bestiarius "Bestiarius") in the [Circus Maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circus_Maximus "Circus Maximus"), temporarily flooded the [Circus Flaminius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circus_Flaminius "Circus Flaminius") for slaughtering [crocodiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_crocodile "Nile crocodile"), and held gladiatorial bouts in the [Saepta Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saepta_Julia "Saepta Julia"). In 2 BC he also staged an elaborate [mock naval battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naumachia "Naumachia"), the *[naumachia Augusti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naumachia_Augusti "Naumachia Augusti")*, by creating an artificial lake on the west bank of the [Tiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiber "Tiber"), its waters fed by a newly built aqueduct, the [Aqua Alsietina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_Alsietina "Aqua Alsietina") that stretched for over twenty miles. In a lethal performance, the combatants reenacted the 480 BC [Battle of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis "Battle of Salamis") between the [Greek city-states led by Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece "Second Persian invasion of Greece") and the Persian [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire").

#### Public works

Augustus put Agrippa in charge of [Rome's water supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome%27s_water_supply "Rome's water supply"), [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation_in_ancient_Rome "Sanitation in ancient Rome"), [drainage system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloaca_Maxima "Cloaca Maxima"), [public baths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_baths "Roman baths"), and [roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads "Roman roads"). Agrippa had overseen these works when he served as aedile in 33 BC, and even privately funded them afterwards. In 33 BC he built the [Aqua Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_Julia "Aqua Julia") aqueduct, along with new [cisterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistern "Cistern") and [water towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_tower "Water tower"). After Agrippa's death in 12 BC, Augustus had to find a solution to maintain Rome's water supply system, and he arranged a system where the Senate designated three of its members as commissioners in charge of the water supply and [repair of aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct "Roman aqueduct").

During the triumvirate and early reign of Augustus, Agrippa oversaw the construction of new roads for military purposes to the Rhine frontier. Augustus created the senatorial commission of the *curatores viarum* ('supervisors for roads'), which worked with local officials and contractors to organize [regular repairs to roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_roads "History of roads"). Augustus repaired all [bridges in Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridges_in_Rome "Bridges in Rome") except the [Milvian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Milvio "Ponte Milvio") and Minucian ones, and paved the [Via Flaminia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Flaminia "Via Flaminia") between Rome and [Ariminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariminum "Ariminum"). In his late reign, he tasked a commission of five senators, the *curatores locorum publicorum iudicandorum* ('Supervisors of Public Property'), with maintaining public buildings and temples.

#### Residences

Augustus's official residence was the *[Domus Augusti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domus_Augusti "Domus Augusti")* ('House of Augustus') on the [Palatine Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatine_Hill "Palatine Hill"), though its identification is not certain. According to Suetonius the [home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domus "Domus") was somewhat modest, but if it was the Carettoni house west of the [Palatine temple of Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatine_temple_of_Apollo "Palatine temple of Apollo"), then Augustus's residence would have been substantially larger and more luxurious than literary sources admit. Augustus dedicated this temple to [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo "Apollo") near his home in 28 BC, and he often appears on coinage wearing the [civic crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civic_crown "Civic crown") with laurels highly associated with Apollo. The *Domus Augusti* is also located near the *[Casa Romuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_Romuli "Casa Romuli")* ('House of Romulus'), purportedly that of Rome's legendary founder Romulus. The [House of Livia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Livia "House of Livia") is located nearby, though it is unclear if Augustus's wife lived there before his death.

Outside Rome, Augustus owned three [countryside villas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa "Roman villa"), which were not extravagant but had [ornamental gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_gardens "Roman gardens"). Augustus built the [Palazzo a Mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palazzo_a_Mare "Palazzo a Mare") palace on the island of [Capri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capri "Capri"), where he hosted a sizable [collection of fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collection_of_fossils "Collection of fossils") and what may have been [dinosaur bones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinosaur_bones "Dinosaur bones"). At the [Villa Giulia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Giulia,_Ventotene "Villa Giulia, Ventotene") on the island of [Ventotene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventotene "Ventotene"), where Augustus exiled his daughter Julia, he constructed a sophisticated [hypocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypocaust "Hypocaust") central heating system for two large bathtubs and a *[caldarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caldarium "Caldarium")* hot plunge bath. Augustus's family home was a villa located in [Nola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nola "Nola"), where he and his father died. This residence was probably [the villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_of_Augustus "Villa of Augustus") discovered at [Somma Vesuviana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somma_Vesuviana "Somma Vesuviana").

## Critical analysis

### Ancient and contemporary views

Writers throughout the ages have both praised and criticized Augustus. The contemporary Roman jurist [Marcus Antistius Labeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Antistius_Labeo "Marcus Antistius Labeo"), fond of the days of pre-Augustan republican liberty in which he had been born, openly criticized the Augustan regime. In his *[Annals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_(Tacitus) "Annals (Tacitus)")*, Tacitus wrote that Augustus had cunningly subverted Republican Rome into a position of slavery, and that the people of Rome traded one slaveholder for another with the succession of Tiberius. Tacitus believed that Emperor [Nerva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerva "Nerva") (r. 96–98) successfully joined principate and liberty. The 3rd-century historian [Cassius Dio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassius_Dio "Cassius Dio") acknowledged Augustus as a benign, moderate ruler, yet like most other post-Augustan historians he viewed Augustus as an [autocrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocrat "Autocrat"). The 1st-century poet [Lucan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucan "Lucan") argued that Caesar's victory over Pompey and the death of [Cato the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_the_Younger "Cato the Younger") in 46 BC marked the end of traditional liberty in Rome.

Modern academics still debate the extent to which Augustus [censored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_censorship "History of censorship") criticism of him. As triumvir, Octavian destroyed all public records dating from the Ides of March 44 BC to the defeat of Sextus Pompey in 36 BC, a convenient political move that aligned with popular sentiment for purging painful memories about the proscriptions. [Augustan poets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustan_poets "Augustan poets") sometimes openly criticized the emperor, such as [Propertius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propertius "Propertius") when he disapproved of the execution of prisoners during the Perusine War. Some of Virgil and Horace's poetry has been interpreted as praising their patron Augustus as an upholder of [moral justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mos_maiorum "Mos maiorum"), and for maintaining the Empire. Octavian was Virgil's patron when the latter penned his *[Eclogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogues "Eclogues")*, which express the discontented views of impoverished farmers and landowners during the triumvirate. Through private letters it appears that Augustus maintained genuine friendships with Virgil and Horace, with no evidence that he intervened directly in their writing of poems. Yet in c. AD 8 Augustus had the poet [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid "Ovid") exiled, and [his literature banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustan_literature_(ancient_Rome) "Augustan literature (ancient Rome)").

Tacitus claimed that the [discipline of history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography "Historiography") declined under Augustus due to [historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_historians "Roman historians") flattering the emperor rather than by active suppression. [Livy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livy "Livy") wrote his highly influential and encompassing *[History of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Rome_(Livy) "History of Rome (Livy)")* during Augustus's reign. Despite championing many of Augustus's views, Livy wrote independently and Tacitus later claimed that Augustus even lightly criticized Livy for glorifying Pompey's career. Augustus may have quietly had his niece Antonia Minor pressure her son [Claudius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius "Claudius") to refrain from writing a history on Rome's civil wars.

### Middle Ages and Renaissance

Augustus became a revered figure in [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") during the Middle Ages due to church fathers [Orosius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orosius "Orosius"), [Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose "Ambrose"), and [Bede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bede "Bede") depicting him as a divinely ordained peacemaker who created a stable realm for the [arrival of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_Christ "Birth of Christ"). The c. 1250 *[Golden Legend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend "Golden Legend")* [hagiographical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagiographical "Hagiographical") anthology promoted the legend of the [Tiburtine Sibyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiburtine_Sibyl "Tiburtine Sibyl"), in which Augustus had an alleged vision of [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") and his mother [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus "Mary, mother of Jesus"). [Petrarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch "Petrarch") viewed Augustus as a righteous ruler, an idea widely accepted in [Renaissance humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanist "Renaissance humanist") literature until the 16th century when more negative views found acceptance.

### Modern perspectives

Views on Augustus varied during the early modern period. The [Anglo-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish "Anglo-Irish") writer [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift "Jonathan Swift") criticized Augustus for installing tyranny over Rome, likening the virtues of the previous Roman Republic to those of [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_Kingdom "Constitution of the United Kingdom")'s [constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy "Constitutional monarchy"). French political philosopher [Montesquieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montesquieu "Montesquieu") remarked that Augustus was a coward in battle, and English playwright [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare "William Shakespeare") portrayed 'Caesar' as such in his 1607 play *[Antony & Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_%26_Cleopatra "Antony & Cleopatra")*. Scottish scholar [Thomas Blackwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Blackwell_(scholar) "Thomas Blackwell (scholar)") deemed Augustus a bloodthirsty usurper and tyrant, views that were shared by Montesquieu and [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire "Voltaire"). During the 19th century Augustus was widely considered a reformer who brought peace and prosperity after the chaos caused by a failed Republic, though [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_Bonaparte "Napoleon Bonaparte") emulated Julius Caesar as a role model and was slightly dismissive of Augustus.

Attitudes about Augustus shifted once again during the 20th century, as scholarship during [the upheavals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_on_Rome "March on Rome") of [fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_in_Fascist_Italy "Propaganda in Fascist Italy") in the 1930s and 1940s generally held negative views about Augustus's seizure of power. In 1937–1938, [Benito Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini "Benito Mussolini") held an 'Augustan exhibition' in Rome to celebrate the bimillenary of the birth of Augustus, an event which influenced [architectural trends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist_architecture "Fascist architecture") of [Fascist Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist_Italy "Fascist Italy"). Mussolini also styled himself as *[Il Duce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Il_Duce "Il Duce")* after *[dux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dux "Dux")* Augustus. Ronald Syme expressed apprehension about Mussolini's espousal of Augustus. He sparked debate by publishing the then controversial *[The Roman Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Roman_Revolution "The Roman Revolution")* (1939) at the onset of the Second World War, acknowledging the political climate that impacted his research. He rejected [fascist appropriations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist_appropriations "Fascist appropriations") of [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") while examining deceptive political terminology employed by totalitarian regimes. Subsequently more peaceful times have led to a greater focus on the art and literature produced in the Augustan age. In 2014 the historian [Adrian Goldsworthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Goldsworthy "Adrian Goldsworthy") stressed that, in modern terms, Augustus was essentially a military dictator, but argued that Augustus was no more ruthless than "other warlords", and that comparing him to Mussolini or other modern dictators is anachronistic and inaccurate.

*The Roman Revolution* was not widely circulated in continental Europe until 1952, but this and other works by Syme left a major impact on scholarship in the English-speaking world and its views on Augustus in particular. Syme viewed Octavian as a "sickly and sinister youth," and his political faction as an entity similar to a modern [crime syndicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_syndicate "Crime syndicate"). Syme criticized some academics for attributing Julius Caesar's political achievements to Octavian, their ready acceptance of Augustan propaganda about Mark Antony, and the view of Augustus as a flawless organizer and peacemaker. Goldsworthy largely agrees with Syme's analysis, but argues that he was very favorable toward Antony and far too harsh in criticizing Augustus's supporters, "especially the majority who came from outside the established aristocracy". Modern academics debate whether Augustus attempted to distance himself from Caesar the dictator when embracing the [deified Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deified_Caesar "Deified Caesar"). Octavian–Augustus placed more emphasis on his own role as princeps over time.

Modern historians have also highlighted the many positive effects of Augustus's reign, the longevity of which is viewed as a major contributing factor in the transformation of Rome into a *de facto* monarchy. Eck and Takács stress that Augustus was responsible for establishing a standing professional army, the dynastic principle of the imperial succession, the embellishment of the capital at the emperor's expense, and relative peace and prosperity for over two centuries. Historian Walter Eder contends that Augustus promoted Republican Roman virtues and addressed the concerns of the [plebs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plebs "Plebs") by means of generosity and cutting back on lavish excess. In 29 BC, Augustus gave 400 sesterces (equal to one-tenth of a Roman pound of gold) each to 250,000 citizens, 1,000 sesterces each to 120,000 veterans in the colonies, and spent 700 million sesterces in purchasing land for his soldiers to settle upon. He also restored 82 different [temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_temple "Roman temple") to display his care for the [Roman pantheon of deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_pantheon_of_deities "Roman pantheon of deities"). In 28 BC, he melted down 80 silver statues commemorating him in an attempt to appear frugal and modest.

## Cultural depictions

### Physical appearance and official images

Suetonius's *[Twelve Caesars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Caesars "Twelve Caesars")* includes a biography of Augustus and details about his appearance. According to Goldsworthy, descriptions of hair color in ancient Roman sources are often ambiguous, and Suetonius's comment that Augustus's curly hair was inclined towards golden (*subflavum*) could mean either "slightly blond" or "brown rather than black" hair. Scientific analysis of [traces of paint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_in_Greco-Roman_sculpture "Color in Greco-Roman sculpture") found in his official statues shows that he most likely had naturally light brown hair. Augustus was likely about 5 feet 9 inches (1.75 m) tall and may have worn built-up soles to appear taller.
Among the best known [portraits of Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portraits_of_Augustus "Portraits of Augustus") are the [Prima Porta Statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_Porta_Statue "Prima Porta Statue"), his sculpted relief on the [Ara Pacis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ara_Pacis "Ara Pacis"), and the sculpted *[Via Labicana Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Labicana_Augustus "Via Labicana Augustus")*. Prominent [cameo portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraved_gem "Engraved gem") include the [Blacas Cameo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacas_Cameo "Blacas Cameo") and **[Gemma Augustea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemma_Augustea "Gemma Augustea")**. The official imagery was tightly controlled and idealized, drawing from a tradition of [Hellenistic portraiture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_portraiture "Hellenistic portraiture"). From c. 29 BC, his portraits proliferated across the Roman world, and they emphasized a youthful appearance until his death.

Julius Caesar first introduced personalized portraits of living individuals on [Roman coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republican_currency "Roman Republican currency") in the 40s BC, and Augustus's image on coins is perhaps one means by which he [emulated Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_depictions_of_Julius_Caesar "Cultural depictions of Julius Caesar"). It is highly likely Augustus personally dictated how these portraits appeared. Augustus's name and image became universal on coinage throughout the Empire. Goldsworthy notes how the ['Caesar' on silver coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%27Caesar%27_on_silver_coins "'Caesar' on silver coins") which was [mentioned by Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Render_unto_Caesar "Render unto Caesar") in the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament") was most likely Augustus rather than Tiberius. Later emperors minted coins depicting both themselves and previous rulers including Augustus.

### Post-classical visual artworks

Augustus has also been depicted in various artworks following classical antiquity. For instance, he appears on the [Hereford Mappa Mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereford_Mappa_Mundi "Hereford Mappa Mundi") dated c. 1300 wearing a [papal tiara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_tiara "Papal tiara") as he orders [geographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_geography "Roman geography") to create a [survey of the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cartography "History of cartography"). In 1765 [Louis XV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XV "Louis XV") commissioned French painter [Charles-André van Loo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Andr%C3%A9_van_Loo "Charles-André van Loo") to create a painting depicting Augustus closing the gates of the [Temple of Janus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Janus_(Roman_Forum) "Temple of Janus (Roman Forum)") in the Forum, a signal that Rome was at peace. Louis XV disliked the painting and had it removed from his hunting lodge, but historian [Mary Beard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Beard "Mary Beard") contends that van Loo's painting served as "an appropriate backdrop" during the signing of the 1802 [Treaty of Amiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Amiens "Treaty of Amiens") during the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars "Napoleonic Wars"). [Napoleon III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_III "Napoleon III") commissioned French painter [Jean-Léon Gérôme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-L%C3%A9on_G%C3%A9r%C3%B4me "Jean-Léon Gérôme") to create the painting titled *The Age of Augustus, the Birth of Christ* (c. 1852–1854), which blends [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_art "Classical art") and [gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_art "Gothic art") elements and depicts Augustus on an imperial [dais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dais "Dais") above a [nativity scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_scene "Nativity scene"), juxtaposing the [birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_Jesus "Birth of Jesus") with the peace brought about by the reign of Augustus. It was exhibited in Paris at the 1855 [Universal Exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_Universelle_of_1855 "Exposition Universelle of 1855").

### Theater, film, televised series, and novels

Augustus is not as widely known as his great-uncle Julius Caesar and is often sidelined as a minor character or brooding villain in theatrical plays, films, TV series, comics, and novels. Goldsworthy attributes this to the fact that [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare "Shakespeare") never wrote [a play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare%27s_plays "Shakespeare's plays") centered around him. Shakespeare's 1599 play *[Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar_(play) "Julius Caesar (play)")* features the character of Octavius, while in the 1607 play *[Antony and Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_and_Cleopatra "Antony and Cleopatra")* he plays a weak, cowardly, and manipulative foe to Antony under the name Caesar. This view is perhaps based on [ancient primary sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_Lives "Parallel Lives") that reflect the propaganda war waged between Antony and Octavian, manifested in the cold performance of actor [Roddy McDowall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roddy_McDowall "Roddy McDowall") as Octavian in the 1963 film *[Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra")*. Robert Graves's 1934 novel *[I, Claudius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I,_Claudius "I, Claudius")* and its subsequent 1976 [television series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I,_Claudius_(TV_series) "I, Claudius (TV series)") depict the older Augustus in a far more sympathetic light as he is outmaneuvered by his murderous wife Livia, though he plays only a [supporting character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supporting_character "Supporting character").
